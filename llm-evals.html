Module 14 · Production track

LLM Evals

Evaluation is how you prove an LLM application works, and prove that a change did not break it. Interviewers ask because you cannot unit-test a model's answer the way you test a function, and they want to hear what you do instead.

⏱ 15–30 min Difficulty · Interview essentials Prereq · 05 RAG and 13 Guardrails

Everything on this page comes back to five ideas.

  1. Check properties, not exact strings, on a fixed golden set.
  2. Rules for anything countable; an LLM judge only for fuzzy qualities, calibrated against people.
  3. Score RAG in two halves: retrieval (context precision and recall) and generation (faithfulness and relevance).
  4. For agents, score the path as well as the answer.
  5. Evals are a gate in CI, and every production failure becomes a new case.
🧠
Memory hook

First, Long, Mine, Moody - the four biases of an LLM judge: position, verbosity, self-preference and inconsistency. Picture a tired examiner who favours the first essay in the pile, the longest one, the ones in their own handwriting, and marks differently depending on mood.

1 · The idea in one minute

An ordinary test checks result == expected. That fails for an LLM for two reasons:

  • Many right answers. The same question can get differently worded answers that are all correct, so an exact match fails a good answer.
  • Many qualities. Correct, supported by the documents, complete, in the right format, polite, fast and cheap - not one pass/fail bit.

Think of marking an essay exam instead of a multiple-choice quiz. You need a marking scheme (a rubric), the same set of questions every year so classes can be compared (a golden dataset), and a second examiner who spot-checks the marking (human review).

You cannot check the exact wording. You check properties of the answer, on a fixed set of cases, every time something changes.

2 · The parts you must know

PartWhat it doesWhat goes wrong without it
Golden datasetA reviewed set of test questions with expected outcomes. 30 to 50 cases is a workable start; include refusals, missing data, ambiguity and attacksNo fixed yardstick, so you cannot tell whether a change helped
Offline evaluationRuns the golden set before releaseChanges ship untested, and customers find the regressions
Online evaluationScores live traffic: thumbs up or down, escalations to a human, judge scores on a sampleYou never learn how real questions differ from your test set
Rule-based checksAnything countable: contains the order id, valid JSON, cites a real document, called the right toolYou pay a judge model to do arithmetic, slower and less reliably
LLM-as-judgeAnother LLM scores fuzzy qualities, such as faithfulness or tone, against a written rubricOpen-ended quality cannot be scored at scale
Human evaluationExperts score a sample, and their scores are used to check the judgeNobody knows whether the judge agrees with people
Trajectory checksFor agents: score the path - which tools ran, how many calls, any forbidden actionA right answer reached by reading another customer's data passes
Regression gateCompares with the previous version in CI and fails the build on a dropA suite that cannot fail the build is only a report

RAG metrics: score the two halves separately

HalfMetricThe question it answers
RetrievalContext precisionOf the chunks retrieved, how many were relevant?
RetrievalContext recallDid retrieval find everything needed to answer?
GenerationFaithfulness (groundedness)Is every claim in the answer supported by the retrieved text?
GenerationAnswer relevanceDoes the answer address the question that was asked?

One blended score hides which half broke. High faithfulness with low recall means the app is faithfully summarising the wrong documents. Libraries such as RAGAS and DeepEval compute these metrics; platforms such as Langfuse, LangSmith and Arize Phoenix store datasets and scores.

LLM-as-judge: its biases, and how to make it trustworthy

BiasWhat it doesCounter
Position biasPrefers whichever answer it saw first in a side-by-side comparisonRun both orders and average
Verbosity biasPrefers longer answersA rubric that rewards correctness and brevity explicitly
Self-preferenceRates answers from its own model family higherUse a judge from a different model family
InconsistencyGives different scores to the same answer on different runsLow temperature where the model allows it (0 reduces variation but does not guarantee identical output, and some reasoning models do not accept it), structured output with a reason, a written rubric, and averaging several runs

Before trusting a judge, calibrate it: score 100 to 200 cases by hand, have the judge score the same cases, and measure agreement with Cohen's kappa, not raw agreement. Twenty cases is too few - at 85% agreement the margin of error is about ±16 points - and raw agreement flatters a judge when most answers are good, because "pass" is right most of the time by default. Version the rubric with the results, because changing the rubric moves every score.

3 · How it fails, and the fix

FailureWhat you seeFix
Three examples checked by handA tone tweak drops refusals on unanswerable questions from 12% to 2%, unnoticed for weeksA golden set that includes unanswerable questions, run on every change
One blended scoreThe score drops and nobody knows whyTrack retrieval and generation metrics separately
Judge trusted blindlyJudge scores look great; users are unhappyCalibrate against human labels; compare judge scores with user feedback
Only the final answer scoredAn agent read three customers' orders to answer about one, and passedTrajectory assertions: count and check tool calls
Looking at the average onlyThe mean improved, but a new critical failure appearedCheck worst cases and failure categories, plus p95 latency and cost

Check yourself

Answer each one out loud before you open it. Recalling an answer is what makes it stick; re-reading it is not.

Q In the essay-exam picture, what are the marking scheme, the same questions every year, and the second examiner? ›
The rubric, the golden dataset, and human review that spot-checks the marking.
Q Name the four judge biases, and one counter for each. ›
Position: run both orders and average. Verbosity: a rubric that rewards correctness and brevity. Self-preference: a judge from a different model family. Inconsistency: low temperature where allowed, structured output with a reason, and averaging several runs.
Q How do you calibrate a judge before trusting it? ›
Label 100 to 200 cases by hand, have the judge score the same cases, and measure agreement with Cohen's kappa. Twenty cases is too few - about ±16 points of error - and raw agreement flatters a judge when most answers are good.
Q Faithfulness is high but context recall is low. What is happening? ›
The app is faithfully summarising the wrong documents. Generation is fine; retrieval did not find what the answer needed.
Q A tone tweak dropped refusals on unanswerable questions from 12% to 2%. Why did nobody notice, and what is the fix? ›
Only three examples were checked by hand, and none was unanswerable. The fix is a golden set that includes unanswerable questions, run on every change.

4 · Say this in the interview

🎤
A 30-second answer

"LLM outputs vary and quality has many dimensions, so I test properties rather than exact strings. I keep a golden dataset of reviewed cases, including refusals and edge cases, and run it offline on every prompt or model change as a gate in CI. I use rule-based checks for anything countable and an LLM judge only for fuzzy qualities, calibrated against human labels because judges have position, verbosity and self-preference biases. For RAG I score retrieval and generation separately - context precision and recall, then faithfulness and answer relevance. For agents I also score the trajectory. Online, I track user feedback and sampled judge scores, and every production failure becomes a new test case."

5 · Interview questions

Answer each one out loud before you open it.

Q Why can't you unit-test an LLM app like normal software? ›
The same input can produce different, equally valid answers, so exact-match assertions fail on correct output. And quality has many dimensions - factual, grounded, complete, formatted, safe, fast, cheap. You test properties of the output instead, using rules, references, a judge model or people.
Q What is a golden dataset, and how do you build one? ›
A curated, reviewed set of representative inputs with expected outcomes, used to score every version the same way. Start with 30 to 50 cases from real questions and domain experts, include hard cases (unanswerable, ambiguous, attacks), and add every production failure as a new case.
Q What is the difference between offline and online evaluation? ›
Offline runs the golden set before deployment and gates releases. Online measures live traffic - user feedback, escalations, judge scores on a sample - and catches drift and new kinds of failure. You need both: offline makes shipping safe, online shows whether you are testing the right things.
Q How do you evaluate a RAG system? ›
Separate the two halves. Retrieval: context precision and context recall against known supporting documents. Generation: faithfulness to the retrieved text and relevance to the question, plus citation correctness. That tells you whether a failure is a retrieval problem or a generation problem.
Q What is LLM-as-judge, and what are its risks? ›
A second LLM scores outputs against a rubric and gives a score with a reason. Risks: position bias, verbosity bias, self-preference and inconsistent scores. Counter them with a clear rubric, low temperature (which reduces but does not remove variation), structured output, both orders in pairwise comparisons, and calibration against 100-200 human-labelled examples measured with Cohen's kappa.
Q How is evaluating an agent different? ›
You score the trajectory as well as the final answer: task completion, correct tool choice and arguments, number of steps, and no forbidden actions. A correct answer reached through a wrong or unsafe path must fail.
Q A new model raises your average score. Do you ship it? ›
Not on the average alone. Check the worst cases and each failure category, especially safety and factual errors, and compare latency and cost. A higher mean can hide a new critical failure.
Q How do evals fit into CI/CD? ›
Treat a prompt or model change like a code change. The golden set runs automatically in the pipeline, results are compared with the last released version, and the build fails if a key metric drops below its threshold. Every result records the code, prompt and rubric version.
Q Give a real example of an LLM app failing without proper evaluation. ›
Air Canada's chatbot invented a refund policy, and in 2024 a Canadian tribunal held the airline responsible for it. In 2023 two New York lawyers were sanctioned for filing ChatGPT-invented case citations. Grounding checks and evaluation on such cases would have caught both.
