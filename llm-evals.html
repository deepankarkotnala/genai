LLM Evals

Anyone can build a RAG chatbot or an agent. The rare, in-demand skill is proving it works — testing an LLM application with repeatable evidence instead of gut feel, so it can be put in front of real users. This page is the interview-focused view of LLM evaluation: why it matters, why it's hard, the methods, and the questions you'll be asked.

🎯
The one-line answer interviewers want

LLM evaluation is measuring the quality of an LLM application's output along several dimensions — correctness, relevance, faithfulness/groundedness, structure, safety, latency and cost — using a repeatable method, rather than manually eyeballing a few responses.

1 · Who is an AI Engineer (and why evals are their edge)

An AI Engineer is someone who builds applications and products on top of foundation models (LLMs) — RAG systems, chatbots, agents — rather than training models from scratch. Most people entering the field learn the build half. Far fewer learn to test and evaluate what they built for production. In interviews, being able to talk about evaluation is what separates "I made a demo" from "I can ship something that serves millions of users."

2 · The flaw of "vibe testing"

Vibe testing is how almost everyone starts: casually trying an app with a few prompts and judging it by feel. "I asked it 5–10 questions, the answers looked good, so it works." No metrics, no fixed test set — just a subjective impression.

⚠️
Why it breaks in production

Vibe testing is informal, subjective and not repeatable. It's fine for a personal project. It is not fine for a production application: you can't put something in front of users when your only evidence is "it felt right when I tried it." You can't catch regressions, compare versions, or prove quality to a stakeholder.

3 · What happens without evals — three case studies

Real, widely-cited failures of shipping LLM apps without proper evaluation and guardrails. Interviewers love these because they show you understand the stakes.

Air Canada chatbot

A grieving user asked the airline's chatbot about a bereavement fare. The bot hallucinated a policy — book now at full price, get a refund later. When the user claimed the refund, Air Canada refused, arguing the chatbot was a "separate entity." The tribunal ruled the chatbot is the company's responsibility. Air Canada lost, refunded the money, and was publicly embarrassed.

Lesson: hallucinated policy is your liability. Faithfulness/grounding evals catch this.

Chevrolet dealer bot

A dealership deployed a chatbot. A user jailbroke it — emotionally convincing it to agree to anything the customer said — then asked to buy a car for $1. The bot agreed and called it a binding offer. Screenshots went viral, and it became a PR disaster.

Lesson: no scope/guardrail + no adversarial eval = brand damage. See Guardrails →.

The ChatGPT lawyer

A lawyer used ChatGPT to find legal precedents for a lawsuit. It confidently fabricated fake cases — fake names, fake dates. He didn't verify, and filed them. When opposing counsel found the cases didn't exist, the judge fined the lawyer and firm $5,000; they lost and went viral.

Lesson: fluent ≠ correct. Never trust unverified output; measure factuality.

4 · Why LLM evaluation is genuinely hard

This is the highest-value conceptual question in an evals interview. It comes down to two differences from traditional software testing.

Deterministic vs. probabilistic

Traditional software is deterministic — for a given input the output is always the same (a calculator: 2 + 2 is always 4). LLM apps are probabilistic — the same input can produce different outputs, and neither has to be wrong. Ask "What is overfitting?" today and in six months and you may get different-but-valid answers. So you can't assert on exact string equality.

Correctness vs. multi-dimensional quality

Software has one benchmark: correctness. An LLM answer has many: factuality, completeness, relevance, groundedness, tone, format compliance, safety — plus latency and cost. Which dimensions matter varies per application. That multi-axis, context-dependent quality is what makes LLM apps far trickier to evaluate.

💡
Consequence for how you test

Because output isn't a fixed string, you evaluate properties of the output (is it grounded? is it relevant? does it match the schema?) rather than exact matches — using references, rubrics, classifiers or a judge model. That's the whole game.

6 · Evaluation methods

Four families. Real pipelines combine them — cheap deterministic checks first, expensive judgement last.

MethodWhat it doesBest forWatch out for
Rule-based / deterministicExact match, regex, JSON-schema validation, "contains", numeric toleranceStructured output, format compliance, classification labelsCan't judge open-ended text quality
Reference-basedCompare to a known good answer — similarity, semantic (embedding) similarity, BLEU/ROUGETasks with a "correct" answer (extraction, translation, QA)Needs curated references; penalizes valid paraphrases
Human evaluationDomain experts rate outputs against a rubricGround truth, nuanced/domain quality, calibrating the other methodsSlow, expensive, doesn't scale
LLM-as-a-judgeA second (often stronger) LLM scores the output against criteria and returns a score + reasonOpen-ended quality: relevance, faithfulness, tone, at scaleCan be biased/inconsistent — anchor with a rubric & a few human-labeled examples
python · LLM-as-a-judge (the pattern to know)
import json
from openai import OpenAI
oai = OpenAI()

def judge_faithfulness(context: str, answer: str) -> dict:
    """Score 1-5 whether the answer is supported ONLY by the context."""
    r = oai.chat.completions.create(
        model="gpt-4o", temperature=0,                 # temp=0 for stable scoring
        response_format={"type": "json_object"},
        messages=[
            {"role": "system", "content":
                "You are a strict evaluator. Given CONTEXT and ANSWER, rate how "
                "fully the ANSWER is supported by the CONTEXT. "
                "5 = every claim supported; 1 = mostly unsupported/hallucinated. "
                'Return JSON: {"score": 1-5, "reason": "..."}'},
            {"role": "user", "content": f"CONTEXT:\n{context}\n\nANSWER:\n{answer}"},
        ])
    return json.loads(r.choices[0].message.content)

7 · Building a custom eval pipeline

The core deliverable of an application-evals project, and a very common "how would you build it?" question.

  1. Curate a golden dataset. 30–50 representative, reviewed examples (input + expected answer / expected supporting docs). Cover the common cases and the tricky/edge ones. This is the single most important asset — quality of evals = quality of this set.
  2. Define a rubric & metrics. Pick 4–6 dimensions that matter for this app (e.g. factuality, completeness, groundedness, format, tone) and how each is scored.
  3. Run the app over the dataset and score each output with the right method per metric.
  4. Produce scorecards and failure-category summaries; compare two configurations (prompt A vs B, model X vs Y) on the same set.
  5. Inspect where an aggregate hides a critical failure — a high average can mask a small number of dangerous answers.
python · minimal eval harness
golden = [
    {"q": "What is the bereavement fare policy?", "must_contain": ["apply before travel"]},
    # ... 30-50 reviewed examples
]

def evaluate(app_fn):
    rows = []
    for ex in golden:
        out = app_fn(ex["q"])
        rule_ok = all(s.lower() in out["answer"].lower() for s in ex.get("must_contain", []))
        judge   = judge_faithfulness(out["context"], out["answer"])
        rows.append({"q": ex["q"], "rule_pass": rule_ok,
                     "faithfulness": judge["score"], "latency_ms": out["latency_ms"]})
    return rows   # -> aggregate into a scorecard, then compare configs
🚩
Interview trap: "the highest average score is best"

Not necessarily. Always look at the worst outputs and failure classes — one confidently-wrong safety/factuality answer can matter more than a 0.03 bump in mean score.

8 · RAG-specific evals

Evaluate retrieval separately from generation so you know where a failure comes from. The metrics you must be able to name:

Retrieval quality

  • Context precision — of retrieved chunks, how many are relevant.
  • Context recall — of the evidence needed, how much was retrieved.
  • Empty / irrelevant / duplicate / stale retrieval.

Generation quality

  • Faithfulness / groundedness — answer supported by retrieved context (no hallucination).
  • Answer relevance — does it actually address the question.
  • Citation correctness — cited sources really back the claim.

9 · Agent-based evals

Agents add a dimension: it's not just the final answer, it's the path taken. Evaluate:

  • Task completion — did the agent actually achieve the goal (end-to-end success rate).
  • Trajectory / tool-selection accuracy — did it pick the right tools with the right parameters, in a sensible order.
  • Efficiency — unnecessary steps, loops, repeated calls, retries, abandoned runs.
  • Handoffs — in multi-agent systems, whether control passed correctly between agents.
🔗
Where you see the trajectory

You measure all of this from traces — one trace per request with every agent step and tool call. That's the observability layer: see Langfuse → and LLMOps §Observability →.

10 · Safety evals

Evaluate whether the app resists misuse and stays within policy — the thing missing in the Chevrolet and Air Canada cases.

  • Jailbreak / prompt-injection resistance — run a red-team set of adversarial prompts and measure how many break scope.
  • Toxicity / harmful content, PII leakage, off-topic (scope) violations.
  • Refusal correctness — refuses what it should, doesn't over-refuse benign requests.

This pairs with runtime Guardrails →: guardrails prevent at runtime; safety evals measure how well they work.

13 · Interview cheat-sheet

Q. Why can't you unit-test an LLM app the way you test normal software?

Because LLMs are probabilistic (same input → different valid outputs), so exact-match assertions fail; and quality is multi-dimensional (factuality, relevance, groundedness, tone, latency, cost), not a single correctness bit. You test properties of the output using references, rubrics, classifiers or a judge model.

Q. What is a golden dataset and why does it matter?

A curated, reviewed set of representative input/expected-output examples used to score the app repeatably and compare versions. It's the foundation — your evals are only as good as this set. ~30–50 examples covering common and edge cases is a solid POC size.

Q. What is LLM-as-a-judge and what are its risks?

Using a second LLM to score outputs against criteria, returning a score + reason. It scales open-ended judgement. Risks: bias (position/verbosity/self-preference) and inconsistency — mitigate with temperature 0, a clear rubric, structured output, and calibration against a few human-labeled examples.

Q. How do you evaluate a RAG system?

Separate retrieval from generation. Retrieval: context precision/recall on a set with known supporting docs. Generation: faithfulness/groundedness, answer relevance, citation correctness. This tells you whether a failure is a retrieval problem or a generation problem.

Q. How is evaluating an agent different?

You evaluate the trajectory, not just the final answer: task-completion rate, tool-selection accuracy, efficiency (loops/retries/wasted steps) and correct handoffs — all read from execution traces.

Q. A model update improved your average score. Ship it?

Not on the average alone. Check the worst cases and failure categories for regressions — especially safety and factuality — plus latency and cost. A higher mean can hide a new critical failure.

Q. Give a real example of shipping without evals going wrong.

Air Canada's chatbot hallucinated a refund policy and the airline was held liable; a Chevrolet dealer bot was jailbroken into "selling" a car for $1; a lawyer filed ChatGPT-fabricated legal cases and was fined. All preventable with grounding/safety evals and guardrails.
