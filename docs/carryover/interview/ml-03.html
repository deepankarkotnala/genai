01Classification vs regression: what actually changes?fundamentalslossmetrics＋
Strong answer

The target’s measurement scale, and then everything depending on it. Regression predicts a continuous quantity where “close” is meaningful, so it uses distance-based losses (MSE, MAE, Huber), an identity link, and error metrics in the target’s own units. Classification predicts membership of a discrete set, so it uses likelihood-based losses (log-loss) or margin-based losses (hinge), a squashing link (sigmoid or softmax) to produce probabilities, and metrics built on the confusion matrix plus a business-chosen threshold. The deepest practical difference is that a classifier’s output requires an extra decision - the threshold - that no regression model needs.

30-second version

The target scale, and then loss, link, metric and error meaning all follow from it. And uniquely, classification needs a threshold the model itself never supplies.

What’s being tested

Whether you can go beyond “continuous versus categorical” into consequences. The threshold observation is what marks a senior answer.

02You must predict how many days until a machine fails. Regression or classification?appliedsurvivalcensoringtrap＋
Strong answer

It depends on the decision the output drives, so I would clarify first. If maintenance is scheduled and the planner needs a horizon, regression on days - or better, survival analysis, because some machines have not failed yet and that is right-censored data, which plain regression either drops or mislabels. If the decision is a binary “service this machine in the next shift or not”, classification on a fixed window is simpler to calibrate and threshold.

A good production answer is often both: a classifier for the alerting decision and a regression or quantile model for the scheduling horizon. And if downside risk is asymmetric, quantile regression - predicting the 10th percentile of time-to-failure is far more useful than the mean when failure is expensive.

30-second version

Clarify the decision first. A scheduling horizon means regression, ideally survival analysis because of censoring; a shift-level go/no-go means classification. Often ship both.

What’s being tested

Whether you ask about the decision before choosing a formulation, and whether you spot censoring. Naming survival analysis is a strong differentiator.

Likely follow-up: “What is censoring and why does it break regression?” - a machine still running at the end of your window has time-to-failure ≥ its current age, not equal to it. Training on that value as ground truth biases predictions systematically downward.
03Multi-class vs multi-label. How do the model and loss differ?multiclassmultilabelsoftmax＋
Strong answer

Multi-class: classes are mutually exclusive, one label per sample. Use a softmax over K units with categorical cross-entropy; probabilities sum to 1 and compete. Multi-label: labels are independent and co-occurring, such as article tags. Use K independent sigmoid outputs and the sum of per-label binary cross-entropies; each probability is independent and they need not sum to 1. Metrics differ too - multi-class uses macro, micro or weighted precision-recall and a confusion matrix; multi-label uses per-label metrics, Hamming loss, subset accuracy and mean average precision.

30-second version

Multi-class is mutually exclusive: softmax plus categorical cross-entropy. Multi-label is independent: K sigmoids plus summed binary cross-entropy. Softmax for multi-label is simply the wrong model.

What’s being tested

An easy question to get subtly wrong. Saying “softmax” for a multi-label problem is an immediate flag.

Likely follow-up: “One-vs-Rest or One-vs-One for multi-class with a binary-only algorithm?” - OvR trains K classifiers, cheap but each faces an imbalanced problem; OvO trains K(K−1)/2 pairwise classifiers, far more models but each on a smaller balanced subset, which is why libsvm uses it where training cost is super-linear in n.
04When is it legitimate to bin a continuous target into classes?target designtrap＋
Strong answer

When the downstream decision is genuinely discrete and the bin boundaries come from the business rather than the data - a regulatory threshold, a fixed SLA, a fixed intervention budget.

It is illegitimate when done for convenience: you destroy within-bin information, make performance sensitive to an arbitrary cutoff, and introduce a discontinuity where cases just below and just above the line are treated as categorically different. If you do bin, report how many samples sit near the boundary and check the model is not merely learning the cutoff.

A better pattern is usually to model the continuous target and threshold the prediction, which keeps the cutoff a configurable business parameter rather than a baked-in modelling choice.

30-second version

Only when the decision is genuinely discrete and the boundary comes from the business. Otherwise model the continuous target and threshold the prediction, keeping the cutoff configurable.

What’s being tested

Resistance to convenient simplifications, and awareness that thresholds should live outside the model where possible.

2026 additions. Framing questions dominate the applied round: interviewers hand you a business ask and score how you turn it into a target, a unit of prediction and a threshold.
05Ops want to reduce late deliveries. Do you predict the delivery time, or predict whether it will be late?2026framingscenario＋
Strong answer

I would start by asking what action the prediction triggers, because the framing should follow the decision. If the answer is “re-route or add a courier when risk is high”, that is a binary decision with a cost on each side, and a classifier optimises for it directly. If the answer is “show the customer an ETA and let three teams set their own escalation rules”, then a regression on the actual time is right, because you can threshold it afterwards at any cut-off, and different teams need different ones.

My default, absent that information, is to regress the time and threshold afterwards. It preserves information rather than destroying it at training time, it survives a business rule change without retraining, it gives ops a magnitude - two minutes late and two hours late are not the same operational event even though both are “late” - and I can still report classification metrics at any cut-off I like. The cost is that squared error spends capacity being accurate about deliveries that were never at risk, and if lateness is rare the classifier concentrates its capacity where the decision is.

What I would actually propose is the version that usually wins: model the distribution, not the point. Predict quantiles of delivery time, then “probability of exceeding the SLA” falls out directly and so does the ETA - one model serving both the decision and the display, and honest about uncertainty. If pushed to one, I would also mention the third framing: because rows include deliveries still in transit, this is naturally a time-to-event problem, and survival analysis handles those censored rows instead of dropping them.

30-second version

It depends what the prediction triggers. Default to regressing the time and thresholding later - it keeps the magnitude, survives a rule change, and I can still report classification metrics. Better still, predict quantiles: then P(late) and the ETA both come from one model. And note in-transit rows make it a censored, time-to-event problem.

What’s being tested

Whether you connect framing to the downstream decision instead of picking a model. Asking what action the prediction triggers, before choosing, is most of the score here.

Likely follow-up: “Lateness is 3% of deliveries. Does that change your answer?” - it strengthens the regression-plus-threshold framing, because a 3% positive rate makes a classifier's training signal thin while every row still carries a usable time. It also means the classification metric must be PR-based rather than accuracy or ROC-AUC, and the threshold must come from the relative cost of an unnecessary intervention versus a missed late delivery.
06Your target is a 1–5 star rating. Regression, 5-class classification, or something else?ordinalframingtrap＋
Strong answer

Neither is right, and saying why is the answer. Plain 5-class classification throws away the ordering - cross-entropy treats predicting 1 when the truth was 5 as exactly as wrong as predicting 4, which is obviously false. Plain regression keeps the ordering but invents equal spacing - it assumes the gap between 1 and 2 stars equals the gap between 4 and 5, and in real rating data that is untrue, since ratings pile up at 5 and 1 and the psychological distance between 4 and 5 is small while 1 to 2 is large.

The honest framings, in order of how often I would use them. Ordinal regression - fit one score and a set of learned cut-points, so the model respects order and learns the spacing rather than assuming it; this is the textbook answer. Classification with an order-aware loss or expected-value output - predict the five-class distribution but report Σ k·P(k), which gives a continuous prediction that respects order and is well behaved when the true rating is genuinely ambiguous. Or the pragmatic route: reframe to the decision - if the product only acts on “is this 4 or 5”, then it is a binary problem and all of this evaporates.

On metrics, I would not use accuracy: quadratic weighted kappa or MAE in stars both charge you more for being further away, which is the property the problem needs.

30-second version

Classification loses the ordering; regression assumes equal spacing between stars, which rating data violates. Ordinal regression - one score with learned cut-points - is the honest version. Or predict the class distribution and report its expected value. And score it with quadratic weighted kappa or MAE in stars, never accuracy.

What’s being tested

Whether you notice that the two obvious options each discard something. Most candidates pick one and defend it; the strong answer names both losses and then offers ordinal regression.

Likely follow-up: “Your prediction is 4.3 stars. What do you show the user?” - that depends on the display contract, and it is a real trap. A 4.3 implies a precision the model may not have, so either round with the rounding rule chosen from the cost of over- versus under-stating, or show the distribution. The deeper point: a mean of 4.3 can come from consistent 4s or from a mix of 1s and 5s, and those are very different products.
07Your model outputs probabilities; the product needs a yes or no. Who picks the threshold, and how?2026thresholdcost＋
Strong answer

The business picks it; my job is to make the choice legible and to make the probabilities trustworthy enough to act on.

Concretely, I would do three things. One, get the costs. What does an unnecessary intervention cost, and what does a miss cost? Once I have those two numbers the optimal threshold is not a matter of taste - for a simple cost matrix it is t* = C_FP / (C_FP + C_FN). If a miss costs nine times a false alarm, the threshold is 0.1, not 0.5. Two, calibrate. That formula is only valid if the probabilities mean what they say, so I would check a reliability curve and apply Platt scaling or isotonic regression if needed - and note that a model trained on resampled data is guaranteed to be miscalibrated, which is a common hidden cause of a threshold that behaves nothing like the maths predicted.

Three, present it as a curve, not a number. I would bring a table: at each threshold, how many alerts per day, expected precision and recall, expected net cost. That converts an abstract statistical choice into a capacity question a manager can actually answer - “we can review 200 cases a day” often picks the threshold by itself. Where review capacity is the binding constraint, precision@k is the metric to optimise rather than F1.

Two practical notes: the default 0.5 is optimal only when the two error costs are equal, and the threshold needs re-checking on a schedule, because prevalence drift moves the operating point even when the model is unchanged.

30-second version

The business picks it from the cost of each error type - for a simple cost matrix, t* = C_FP/(C_FP+C_FN). But the probabilities have to be calibrated first, otherwise that formula is meaningless. I would present a threshold table with alerts per day, precision, recall and net cost, because capacity usually decides it. And 0.5 is only optimal when both errors cost the same.

What’s being tested

The boundary between the model and the product decision. Bringing the cost formula and, crucially, the calibration prerequisite is the strong-candidate pattern; “I would use 0.5” or “I would maximise F1” without asking about costs is the weak one.

Likely follow-up: “They cannot give you the costs.” - then infer them from behaviour rather than stalling. Ask how many alerts a day the team can handle, which fixes the threshold by capacity. Or present two or three operating points with their consequences in plain counts and let them choose - the choice reveals the implied cost ratio, and you write it down for next time.
