01Explain maximum likelihood estimation, and derive a loss function with it.MLEderivationloss＋
Strong answer

MLE chooses the parameters that make the observed data most probable under an assumed model. Write the likelihood L(θ) = ∏ p(xᵢ|θ), take the log to turn the product into a sum - same argmax, numerically stable - and maximize, equivalently minimizing the negative log-likelihood.

Derivation. Assume yᵢ = w·xᵢ + εᵢ with εᵢ ~ N(0, σ²). Then p(yᵢ|xᵢ,w) = (1/√(2πσ²))·exp(-(yᵢ - w·xᵢ)²/(2σ²)). The log-likelihood is -n/2·log(2πσ²) - (1/2σ²)·Σ(yᵢ - w·xᵢ)². The first term is constant in w, so maximizing the log-likelihood is exactly minimizing Σ(yᵢ - w·xᵢ)² - mean squared error.

So MSE is not an arbitrary choice; it is the MLE under Gaussian noise.

30-second version

MLE picks parameters maximizing the probability of the observed data. Assume Gaussian noise on a linear prediction and the negative log-likelihood reduces exactly to squared error.

What’s being tested

Whether you understand that loss functions come from probabilistic assumptions. This is the single most reusable derivation in ML interviews.

Likely follow-up: “So what does using MSE assume about your errors?” - additive, zero-mean, constant-variance, Gaussian. With heavy tails, MSE over-weights outliers and MAE (the Laplace-noise MLE) or Huber is more appropriate.
02What does a p-value of 0.03 mean?statisticsinferencetrap＋
Strong answer

If the null hypothesis were true, there is a 3% probability of observing a test statistic at least as extreme as the one observed. It is a statement about data given a hypothesis - not about the probability of the hypothesis given the data, and not a measure of effect size. A p-value of 0.03 on a very large sample can accompany a business-irrelevant effect.

30-second version

The probability of data at least this extreme if the null were true. Not the probability the null is true, and not an effect size.

What’s being tested

The most common statistical misconception in the industry. Getting it right is a strong positive signal.

Likely follow-up: “You ran 20 independent tests and one came back at p = 0.04. What do you conclude?” - essentially nothing; at α = 0.05 you expect about one false positive in 20 by construction. Apply a multiple-comparison correction, or pre-register a single primary metric.
03A disease affects 1 in 1,000. A test has 99% sensitivity and 95% specificity. You test positive - what is the probability you have it?Bayesbase ratederivation＋
Strong answer

P(D|+) = P(+|D)P(D) / P(+). Prior P(D) = 0.001 and P(+|D) = 0.99, so the true-positive mass is 0.001 × 0.99 = 0.00099. The false-positive rate is 1 - 0.95 = 0.05, so the false-positive mass is 0.999 × 0.05 = 0.04995.

P(D|+) = 0.00099 / (0.00099 + 0.04995) ≈ 0.0194 - about 1.9%.

Despite a “99% accurate” test, a positive result means under a 2% chance of disease, because the prior is so small that false positives from the huge healthy population swamp the true positives.

30-second version

About 1.9%. The base rate is so low that false positives from the large healthy population dominate the true positives.

What’s being tested

Base-rate reasoning. This is exactly the arithmetic behind why precision collapses on imbalanced problems in Module 12 - same computation, different vocabulary.

Likely follow-up: “Connect this to a fraud model.” - precision on a rare positive class is bounded by the base rate, so a model with excellent recall and a plausible false-positive rate can still produce a majority-false-positive alert queue.
04Does zero correlation imply independence?correlationindependencetrap＋
Strong answer

No. Independence implies zero correlation, but not the reverse. Pearson correlation measures only linear association: take x ~ Uniform(-1,1) and y = x² - perfectly dependent, correlation ≈ 0. The exception is the joint Gaussian case, where zero correlation does imply independence. The practical consequence is that screening features by correlation-with-target silently discards non-linear predictors.

30-second version

No - only the converse holds. Pearson correlation sees linear association only; y = x² has correlation near zero while being perfectly dependent.

What’s being tested

Whether you know the limits of your default diagnostic tool, and the feature-selection consequence.

Likely follow-up: “How would you detect non-linear dependence?” - mutual information, Spearman rank correlation for monotone relationships, distance correlation, or simply fit a shallow tree and inspect its gain.
05What is the bias of an estimator, and give an example of a biased one.estimatorbiasvariance＋
Strong answer

Bias is E[θ̂] - θ: the systematic error of the estimation procedure across repeated samples. The maximum-likelihood variance estimator dividing by n is biased downward; dividing by n-1 (Bessel’s correction) is unbiased, because one degree of freedom is consumed estimating the mean. Crucially, unbiasedness is not automatically desirable - ridge regression is deliberately biased in exchange for a large variance reduction, and often achieves lower total error.

30-second version

The systematic error of the procedure across repeated samples. Dividing variance by n is biased low; n−1 corrects it. But biased can be better - that is exactly ridge’s bargain.

What’s being tested

Setup for the bias–variance decomposition in Module 05. The “biased can be better” point is what distinguishes a strong answer.

06Explain Simpson’s paradox with a case where it would change your decision.confoundingexperimentation＋
Strong answer

A trend present in every subgroup can reverse when the subgroups are aggregated, because group membership confounds the comparison. Classic case: treatment A appears better than B overall, but B is better within both mild and severe cohorts - because B was disproportionately given to severe cases.

Decision impact: an aggregate A/B readout showing variant B winning can be driven entirely by a shift in traffic mix - for example more mobile users landing in one arm - so you must check per-segment results and randomization balance before shipping.

30-second version

A trend in every subgroup can reverse on aggregation when group membership confounds the comparison. So an aggregate A/B win can be pure traffic-mix artefact.

What’s being tested

Confounding awareness - the core skill in any experimentation-adjacent role.

Likely follow-up: “How do you protect against it?” - randomize properly, pre-register segments, check covariate balance across arms, and use stratified or CUPED-style variance reduction.

07Your target is “number of insurance claims per policy per year” - mostly zeros, occasionally 3. Which loss, and why not MSE?2026loss selectionMLE＋
Strong answer

I would go back to the likelihood rather than reaching for a loss. The target is a non-negative integer count, so the natural model is Poisson, whose negative log-likelihood gives Poisson deviance - and Poisson has the property that variance equals the mean, which matches count data where high-frequency policies are also noisier. In practice I would fit log λ = Xw, a log link, which also guarantees the prediction stays non-negative.

MSE is wrong here for three concrete reasons. It assumes constant-variance Gaussian noise, but count noise grows with the rate. It permits negative predictions, which are meaningless. And because squared error is symmetric, it treats “predicted 0.5 where the truth was 0” the same as an equivalent overshoot on a policy with a rate of 5, when the business consequences differ.

Then the refinements, which is where the real answer lives. If the variance exceeds the mean - very common - Poisson is overdispersed and I would move to negative binomial. If there are far more exact zeros than Poisson can produce, a zero-inflated or hurdle model separates “does this policy ever claim” from “how many, given that it does”. And if the actual business target is total claim cost, which is zero for most policies and continuous-positive otherwise, that is precisely the case for Tweedie loss - which is why insurance pricing models are usually Tweedie, and why both XGBoost and LightGBM ship it as an objective.

30-second version

Poisson deviance with a log link - count target, variance scales with the mean, predictions stay non-negative. MSE assumes constant Gaussian noise and allows negative counts. If variance exceeds the mean, negative binomial; if the target is claim cost with a mass at zero, Tweedie.

What’s being tested

Whether maximum likelihood is a working tool for you or a piece of trivia. Deriving the loss from the target's nature - rather than defaulting to MSE - is the whole point of Module 01, and Tweedie is the detail that signals real exposure to this problem class.

Likely follow-up: “How would you check the Poisson assumption?” - compare the residual variance to the fitted mean across bins of predicted rate. If variance climbs faster than the mean you have overdispersion; also compare the observed proportion of zeros with the proportion Poisson predicts at your fitted rates, which is the direct test for zero inflation.
08A screen is 99% sensitive and 99% specific for a condition affecting 1 in 10,000. A patient tests positive. What do you tell them?Bayesbase ratetrap＋
Strong answer

That a positive result means roughly a 1% chance of actually having the condition - so the test is useful as a trigger for a second, more specific test, and not as a diagnosis.

The arithmetic is worth doing out loud with round numbers, because it is more convincing than the formula. Take 1,000,000 people. About 100 have the condition, and the test catches 99 of them. The other 999,900 do not, and 1% of them - 9,999 people - test positive anyway. So among roughly 10,098 positives, 99 are real: about 0.98%. Formally that is P(disease | +) = P(+|D)P(D) / P(+), but the counting version is what stops people arguing.

The lesson generalises directly to ML: when the positive class is rare, precision is dominated by the false-positive rate acting on the enormous negative population, no matter how good your recall is. This is exactly why 99.9% accuracy is meaningless on a 0.1% fraud problem, why PR-AUC is preferred to ROC-AUC there, and why lowering the threshold to catch more positives destroys precision so fast.

30-second version

About 1%. Per million people: 100 have it and 99 are caught, but 1% of the 999,900 healthy people gives about 10,000 false positives. Rare base rates mean precision is governed by the false-positive count, not by accuracy - the same reason ROC-AUC flatters rare-positive models.

What’s being tested

Base-rate reasoning, and whether you can explain it in counts rather than symbols. It is asked partly as a communication test - a stakeholder needs the counting version.

Likely follow-up: “What if we screen a high-risk group instead?” - the prior rises, so the same test becomes far more informative. At a 10% base rate a positive means roughly 92% likelihood. Nothing about the test changed; who you point it at changed. That is the argument for two-stage screening pipelines, and for cheap-recall-then-expensive-precision architectures in ML.
09Your A/B test on the new model gives p = 0.03. What does that entitle you to say - and what does it not?2026experimentationtrap＋
Strong answer

It entitles me to say: if the new model were truly no different, data at least this extreme would occur about 3% of the time. That is all. It is not a 3% probability that the null hypothesis is true, and it is not a 97% probability that the new model is better - those are the two misstatements that get flagged.

What I would actually report is the effect size with a confidence interval, because that is the decision-relevant object. “+0.4% conversion, 95% CI [+0.05%, +0.75%]” tells you both that it is positive and that it might be small; a bare p-value tells you neither. Then the distinction that matters commercially: statistical significance is not practical significance. A tiny lift can be significant at large n and still not cover the inference cost and on-call burden of the new model, so the decision threshold should be a minimum detectable effect agreed before the test.

I would also raise the things that make a p-value untrustworthy in practice: peeking at results daily inflates the false-positive rate badly unless you use a sequential test; testing many metrics or many segments needs a multiple-comparisons correction or you will find a winner by luck; and the randomisation unit must match the interference structure - per-user, not per-request, when a user's own history affects their later experience.

30-second version

Only that data this extreme would be unlikely if there were no true difference. It is not the probability the null is true. I would report the effect size and its confidence interval, check the lift against a pre-agreed minimum detectable effect, and confirm nobody peeked daily or tested twenty metrics without correction.

What’s being tested

Statistical literacy plus experiment hygiene. Model launches go through A/B tests, so this now appears in ML rounds and not only in analytics ones. Naming peeking and multiple comparisons unprompted is the differentiator.

Likely follow-up: “The overall result is flat but it wins strongly for mobile users. Do you ship it for mobile?” - treat that as a hypothesis, not a finding. Post-hoc segment discovery on a flat overall result is the classic multiple-comparisons trap. Pre-register the mobile hypothesis and run a confirmatory test on fresh traffic; if the mechanism is plausible and the segment was defined in advance, that is a much stronger case.
