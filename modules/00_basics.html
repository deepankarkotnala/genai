The basics: models, tokens and transformers

Start here if words like transformer, token or parameters mean nothing to you yet. This page uses no mathematics and assumes no machine-learning background. By the end you will know every term Module 01 opens with, which is the whole point of it.

2 · A model is a machine that learned to guess

Imagine an apprentice who spent years reading almost everything ever written, and then had every book taken away. Nothing was memorised word for word. What is left is a feel for how language goes: which word tends to follow which, how an argument is usually structured, what a Python function normally looks like, what a polite refusal sounds like.

That leftover feel is what a model is. Concretely it is an enormous list of numbers — billions of them — and each number is a setting, like one dial on an unimaginably large mixing desk. Those numbers are called parameters or weights. A small open model you can run on a laptop has a few billion of them; the biggest have hundreds of billions.

Training

The one-time, expensive process of finding good settings for all those dials. The model is shown a piece of text with the next word hidden, it guesses, it is told how wrong it was, and every dial nudges slightly. Repeat trillions of times. This is done once, by the model's makers, on very large machines.

Inference

What happens when you use it. The dials are frozen. Your text goes in, a guess comes out. Nothing about the model changes, and it learns nothing from you. Every conversation starts from exactly the same frozen dials.

📌

This is the first thing that surprises people: the model does not remember your last conversation. When a chat app appears to remember, it is quietly re-sending the earlier messages with your new one. Memory is something built around the model, not inside it — Module 08 covers how.

3 · Text has to become numbers first

A model can only do arithmetic. It cannot read letters, so text is first chopped into pieces from a fixed list, and each piece is swapped for its number in that list. The pieces are called tokens; the fixed list of every piece the model knows is its vocabulary, usually somewhere around 100,000 entries.

A token is often a whole common word, but not always. Rarer words get split:

TextSplit into tokensWhy
the cat satthe · cat · satCommon words are each one token.
unbelievableun · believ · ableRarer, so it is built from familiar fragments.
Ramanujanseveral fragmentsNames are often split — one reason models misspell them.

As a rule of thumb for English, one token is about four characters, and 100 tokens is roughly 75 words. This matters more than it looks. Almost everything you will later pay for, wait for, or run out of is counted in tokens: the price of a request, the speed of an answer, and how much text the model can consider at once.

💡
Try it in ten seconds

Take the sentence you are reading and count its characters, then divide by four. That is roughly what this sentence costs the model. Module 01 has an interactive tokenizer if you want to see the real split.

4 · What a neural network actually does

Now the numbers need to be turned into a guess. That is the job of a neural network, and the idea behind it is simpler than its reputation.

Picture a long series of stages. Numbers enter the first stage. At each stage, every number is multiplied by some of those dial settings, the results are added up, and something mildly interesting happens: any result below a threshold gets flattened, so the stage can respond to some patterns and ignore others. The output becomes the input of the next stage. Stack enough stages and the whole pipeline can express extraordinarily complicated relationships — even though every single step is just multiply, add, and flatten.

"Learning" is nothing more than the correction step from the last section, repeated: guess, measure the error, nudge every dial a little in the direction that would have made the error smaller. Nobody writes the rules. The rules are whatever the dials end up encoding.

📌

That is genuinely enough for this course. If you want the next level of detail — one artificial neuron, layers, activation functions, how the nudging actually works — there is a longer plain-language walkthrough in Neural Networks. It is optional, and nothing in Modules 01–15 breaks if you skip it.

5 · What a transformer is, in four sentences

This is the word that ambushes most beginners, so here it is plainly. A transformer is one particular design for arranging those stages, published in 2017, and it is the design behind essentially every language model you have heard of. Its central trick is called attention: at every stage, each token is allowed to look at every other token in the text and decide which ones matter for understanding it. Those stages are stacked — a few dozen of them, each one refining what the last one worked out. That is it. That is a transformer.

Attention is worth one concrete example, because it is the part that does the real work:

🎯
Why "attention" is the whole idea

Read these two sentences:

  • "He sat on the river bank."
  • "He withdrew cash from the bank."

The word bank is identical. Its meaning is not. To handle the first one, the model's representation of bank has to be shaped by the word river; in the second, by cash. Attention is the mechanism that lets it pull in exactly those words and mostly ignore he, the and from. Every token does this, at every stage, for every other token.

Before transformers, models read text strictly one word at a time, carrying a summary forward as they went — which made them slow to train and forgetful over long passages. A transformer takes in the whole passage at once, which is what made it practical to train on an internet-sized pile of text. That change of shape, more than any single clever idea, is why capable language models arrived when they did.

🧊
Treat it as a black box from here

For all of Module 01, "transformer" can safely mean the box that takes tokens and produces a score for every possible next token. You never need to know what happens inside it to understand prompting, context windows, RAG or agents. Module 02 opens the box properly — attention arithmetic, multiple heads, positional information, the lot — and it will feel far easier with this page behind you.

6 · How one guess becomes a whole answer

The model does something much smaller than people assume. It does not write an answer. It produces a score for every single token in its vocabulary, saying how well each one would continue the text so far. Those raw scores have a name you will see constantly: logits.

Scores are not yet probabilities, so they are squashed into a set of percentages that add up to 100% — the function that does this is called softmax, and that is all you need to know about it. Now the system picks one token from those percentages, sticks it onto the end of the text, and asks the model again. And again. Until a stopping point.

StepIn plain wordsThe term for it
1Your text is chopped into tokens and turned into numbers.Tokenizing
2The box scores every possible next token.Forward pass → logits
3Scores become percentages.Softmax
4One token is picked — sometimes the top one, sometimes a near-miss on purpose.Sampling
5The picked token is added to the text, and it all repeats.Autoregressive generation

Two consequences fall straight out of that loop, and both explain things you have probably already noticed:

Answers arrive word by word

The text streams onto your screen because it is genuinely being produced one token at a time. A 500-token answer means roughly 500 trips through the box, which is why long answers are slow and cost more.

The same question gives different answers

Step 4 does not always take the highest-scoring token. How adventurous that choice is, is a setting called temperature: low means predictable and repetitive, high means varied and more likely to go off the rails.

Two more terms and you are done. The model can only look at a limited amount of text at once — your prompt plus its answer so far — and that budget is the context window. And because re-reading the whole text on every one of those 500 trips would be wasteful, the system keeps the intermediate work it already did for earlier tokens; that saved work is the KV cache. When Module 01 says prefill, it means the first trip that processes your whole prompt; decode is each cheap one-token trip after it.

7 · Why something so fluent can still be wrong

Look again at what step 2 is actually asking: which token would plausibly come next? Not which token would be true. Nowhere in the loop is there a fact-checking stage, and the model has no library to consult — remember, the books were taken away. When a plausible continuation happens to be false, you get what the field calls a hallucination: fluent, confident, and made up.

This is not a bug that will be patched. It is the direct consequence of how the machine works, and it is the reason half of this course exists:

🧭
The one sentence that makes the rest of the course make sense

The model is a fixed box that scores next tokens. Every technique you are about to learn is either about what you put into that box, or what you do with what comes out. Prompting, RAG, tools, agents, guardrails — all of it is one of those two things.

Q What is a model, in one sentence, without using the word "AI"? ›
A very large collection of numbers, fixed after training, that scores which chunk of text plausibly comes next. Training set the numbers; using it never changes them.
Q Why does text have to be turned into tokens at all? ›
Because the network can only do arithmetic. Tokens are pieces from a fixed vocabulary, and each is swapped for its number in that list before anything else happens.
Q What is a transformer, and what is attention? ›
A transformer is a network design built from stacked stages, and attention is the step inside each stage where every token looks at every other token to work out which ones matter for its meaning. "River bank" versus "withdrew from the bank" is attention doing its job.
Q The model produces one answer of 300 words. Roughly how many times did it run? ›
Around 400 times — one pass per token, and 300 words is roughly 400 tokens. That is why long answers are slow and cost more than short ones.
Q Why can a confident answer still be false? ›
The loop optimises for a plausible next token, not a true one, and there is no lookup step and no fact-checking stage anywhere in it. Fixing that is what RAG, tools and guardrails are for.
