Everything on this page comes back to five ideas. Level 1 covers the first three.

  1. Attention is score, softmax, blend. A word scores every earlier word, turns the scores into percentages, and takes a weighted mix of what those words carry.
  2. Query, key and value. What a word is looking for, what it offers, and what it passes on. All three are learned.
  3. Attention moves information between words; the feed-forward network processes each word. Stack that pair many times and you have the whole model.
  4. Attention needs help with order. Position has to be supplied, and the causal mask stops a word from reading the words after it.
  5. Cost grows with the square of the length. At serving time the KV cache, not the weights, is usually what runs out.

Memory hook

Score, softmax, blend. Picture bank in "I withdrew cash from the bank": it scores every earlier word, turns the scores into percentages, and blends mostly cash into itself - so it comes out meaning money, not a riverside.

Level 1The core ideaAbout 30 minutes. No maths beyond multiplying and adding.

1 · What you already know

From The Basics: the word bank means one thing in "He sat on the river bank" and another in "He withdrew cash from the bank". Attention is how a model tells them apart.

From module 01: text becomes tokens, and each token becomes a vector - a list of numbers that stands for its meaning. The vectors pass through a stack of layers, and the last layer scores every possible next token. Softmax turns those scores into probabilities.

This page opens up one of those layers.

One note on wording. The examples here say "word" to stay readable. Models actually work on tokens, which are often whole words and sometimes pieces of words. Everything on this page works the same either way.

2 · The problem transformers solved

Before 2017, language models mostly read text one word at a time. They kept a running summary, called the hidden state, and updated it after every word. These were recurrent neural networks, or RNNs.

This design has two problems, and better training cannot fix either of them:

ProblemWhy it happens
Slow to trainWord 400 cannot be processed until word 399 is finished. A GPU with thousands of cores still works through the text one step at a time.
Forgets distant wordsEverything word 1 contributed has to survive inside one fixed-size summary that is rewritten 399 times. In practice it fades.

Both problems have one cause: information travels from one word to another only by passing through every word in between. The transformer removes that. Any word can read any earlier word directly, in one step.

3 · The big idea: every word looks back at every earlier word

Take those two sentences. When the model reaches bank, it lets bank look back at every earlier word and decide how much each one matters right now. Then the vector for bank is updated with a blend of what those words carry.

The percentages are made up for illustration, but the pattern is what real models do. Three things to notice:

  • Same word, different blend. bank leans on cash in the first sentence and on river in the second, so it ends up with a different vector - a different meaning.
  • Nobody wrote these rules. The model learned during training which words should matter to which.
  • Every word does this, not just bank, and all of them at the same time.

That is attention. The next two sections show how a word decides where to look, and then work one example with real numbers.

4 · Query, key and value: how a word decides where to look

From its own vector, every word makes three new vectors, using three matrices the model learned in training:

Plain meaningIn a libraryFor bank
QueryWhat this word is looking forWhat you type into the search box"Which earlier words tell me what kind of bank I am?"
KeyWhat this word offers to othersThe label on a book's spinecash: "I am about money". river: "I am about water".
ValueWhat this word passes on if it is chosenWhat is inside the bookWhat cash actually adds to the new vector for bank

The lookup works like a search. Compare bank's query with every earlier word's key: a close match gives a high score. Then take mostly the values of the best matches.

Keys and values are separate for the same reason a book's spine label is not the whole book. What makes a word easy to find is not the same as what it should pass on.

💡
The quotes are ours, not the model's

Real queries and keys are lists of numbers. Nobody tells the model what to look for. It ends up with useful queries and keys because they help it predict the next word, and the library comparison is only a way to picture that.

5 · Worked example: attention by hand

Here is attention with real numbers, small enough to check with a pencil. The word bank looks back at three earlier words: withdrew, cash and the. Real vectors have dozens of numbers each. These have two.

The query for bank is [1, 1].

Step 1 · Score: compare the query with each key

Multiply the two lists number by number and add the results. This is called a dot product. A bigger result means a closer match.

Earlier wordIts keyQuery · keyScore
withdrew[1, 0]1×1 + 1×01
cash[1, 1]1×1 + 1×12
the[1, -1]1×1 + 1×(-1)0

Step 2 · Softmax: turn the scores into percentages

Raise e (about 2.718) to the power of each score, then divide each result by their total. You met softmax in module 01. It turns any scores into percentages that add up to 100, with the biggest score getting the biggest share.

WordScoreescore÷ total (11.11)Weight
withdrew12.720.24524%
cash27.390.66567%
the01.000.0909%

Step 3 · Blend: mix the values using those weights

Multiply each word's value by its weight, then add everything up.

WordWeightIts valueWeight × value
withdrew0.245[0, 1][0, 0.245]
cash0.665[1, 0][0.665, 0]
the0.090[0.5, 0.5][0.045, 0.045]
Sum[0.71, 0.29]

The new vector for bank is [0.71, 0.29]. Two-thirds of it came from cash, because cash's key matched bank's query best. That is all attention is.

🔑
Attention in three words

Score, softmax, blend. Score every earlier word against your query. Turn the scores into percentages. Take that mix of their values.

A real model differs only in size. Its vectors have 64 to 128 numbers per head instead of 2. A word also scores itself, as well as the words before it. And thousands of words do this at the same time. The three steps do not change.

Optional · check these numbers in Python (nothing to install) ›

Paste this into any Python 3 prompt. It repeats the three steps above.

import math

query  = [1, 1]                                    # the query for "bank"
keys   = {"withdrew": [1, 0], "cash": [1, 1], "the": [1, -1]}
values = {"withdrew": [0, 1], "cash": [1, 0], "the": [0.5, 0.5]}

# 1 - score: dot product of the query with each key
scores = {w: sum(q * k for q, k in zip(query, key)) for w, key in keys.items()}

# 2 - softmax: e to the power of each score, divided by the total
total = sum(math.exp(s) for s in scores.values())
weights = {w: math.exp(s) / total for w, s in scores.items()}

# 3 - blend: weighted sum of the values
blend = [sum(weights[w] * values[w][i] for w in values) for i in range(2)]

print(scores)                                       # {'withdrew': 1, 'cash': 2, 'the': 0}
print({w: round(p, 2) for w, p in weights.items()}) # {'withdrew': 0.24, 'cash': 0.67, 'the': 0.09}
print([round(x, 2) for x in blend])                 # [0.71, 0.29]

Try changing the query to [1, -1] and predict which word wins before you run it.

6 · From one word to a whole model

Section 5 updated one word. A transformer layer does it for every word at once, then adds one more step:

  1. Each word's vector also carries its position in the text. Level 2 explains why that is needed.
  2. Every word makes its query, key and value.
  3. Every word's query is scored against every earlier word's key.
  4. Softmax, then blend, exactly as in section 5.
  5. Each word's new vector goes through a small neural network on its own, called the feed-forward network. This is where the information a word just gathered gets processed.

Attention moves information between words; the feed-forward network processes each word. That pair is one block.

A model stacks many identical blocks, each with its own learned weights: 12 in GPT-2 small, 32 in a typical 7B model. Each block refines the vectors a little more. After the last one, every vector is turned into next-token scores, exactly as in module 01.

This fixes both RNN problems. Every word reads every earlier word directly, so distance no longer causes forgetting. And all the words are processed at once, so training no longer waits on a chain of steps.

7 · Attention, the real version

A real model does section 5 for every word at once, using matrix multiplication. The input is a table with one row per token: [sequence_length, model_width].

  1. Vectors and position. Each token becomes a vector, and the model is given each token's position. Section 8 shows the two ways to do that.
  2. Q, K and V. Multiply the input by three learned matrices. Every position gets its own query, key and value.
  3. Score every pair. Take the dot product of every query with every key, including the position's own key. For n tokens that is an n × n table of scores.
  4. Scale. Divide every score by the square root of the head width (√64 = 8 in GPT-2 small).
  5. Mask the future. Set every score where the key comes after the query to minus infinity.
  6. Softmax each row. Each position's scores become weights that add up to 1. The masked scores become exactly 0.
  7. Blend. Multiply the weights by V. Each position's output is a weighted sum of the values it was allowed to see.
  8. Combine the heads. Several heads did steps 2 to 7 side by side. Join their outputs, multiply by one more learned matrix, and add the result back onto the input.

Steps 3, 6 and 7 are the score, softmax and blend you did by hand. Steps 4, 5 and 8 are support.

Steps 2 to 7 happen for all positions at the same time, as matrix multiplications. There is no loop over the sequence anywhere, and that is what lets training use the whole GPU.

8 · The supporting parts, and why each exists

Each part below is there because something specific goes wrong without it.

PartWhat it doesWhat goes wrong without it
ScalingDivides scores by √(head width)Scores grow with the vector size, softmax puts nearly all the weight on one word, and learning stalls
Causal maskHides later words before softmaxIn training, the model could read the very word it is meant to predict, so it would learn nothing useful
Multiple headsRuns several attentions side by side, each with its own Q, K and VOne set of weights has to cover every kind of relationship at once
Positional informationTells the model where each token sitsAttention compares content only, so word order is lost or blurred (section 10)
Residual connectionAdds each sub-layer's output to its input instead of replacing itDeep stacks stop training: the signal, and the learning signal flowing back, fade layer by layer
NormalisationRescales each vector to a standard size before each sub-layerThe numbers drift in size through the stack and training becomes unstable
Feed-forward networkTwo linear layers with a non-linearity between them, applied to each position on its ownThe model can gather information but cannot process it. About two-thirds of the parameters live here.

Multiple heads, in numbers

In GPT-2 small, the vector width of 768 is split into 12 heads of 64 numbers each. Each head runs attention on its own 64 numbers, then the 12 outputs are joined back into 768. Some heads learn recognisable jobs, such as looking at the previous token or copying a pattern that appeared earlier in the text, but many have no clean description.

Positional information: two ways to supply it

The original 2017 transformer added a fixed pattern of sine and cosine waves to each token vector before the first block. GPT-2 added a learned pattern instead. Most current models use RoPE (rotary position embedding). With RoPE nothing is added to the token vector. Instead, inside every attention layer, Q and K are rotated by an angle that depends on position, so a score depends on how far apart two tokens are.

Normalisation: where it goes

The original paper normalised after each sub-layer. Modern models normalise before it, which trains more stably in deep stacks. Many use RMSNorm, a cheaper variant that only rescales.

The block, in two lines

Put together, one modern block is:

x = x + attention(norm(x))      # words exchange information
x = x + feed_forward(norm(x))   # each word processes what it gathered

The x + is the residual connection. The stack is this block repeated, each copy with its own weights. Nothing changes shape from bottom to top.

10 · Break it

Two ways to break a transformer. The first loses meaning; the second loses money.

Remove positional information

Attention compares tokens by content only. Shuffle the input and every token gets the same scores as before, just in a different order. In a model that reads in both directions, "The dog bit the man" and "The man bit the dog" contain the same tokens, so it cannot tell them apart. Nothing raises an error: the model simply loses word order.

Left-to-right models are a partial exception. The causal mask leaks some order, because each position sees a different number of earlier tokens, and research models trained with no positional information still pick up word order from it. Current models still supply position explicitly, usually with RoPE, partly because it is one of the main levers for extending context length.

Make the input long

The second failure is arithmetic, and it is the one that costs money.

The score table has one entry for every pair of tokens, in every head of every layer. With a naive implementation that keeps whole tables in memory, a small GPU runs out long before the model's advertised context length.

11 · Fix it

Positional information goes back in. RoPE is what current models use, because it makes the score depend on the distance between two positions rather than on their absolute indexes.

The quadratic cost cannot be removed, only managed. Three levers, in the order you should reach for them:

LeverWhat it changesWhat it costs
Send lessRetrieve the relevant 2k tokens instead of sending 32kA retrieval system - 05 · RAG
FlashAttentionNever builds the full score table in memory; same maths, computed in tilesNothing, if your serving stack supports it
Sliding window / GQAEach position attends to fewer keys, or heads share keys and valuesSome long-range accuracy

The first lever is the one engineers skip and the one that helps most. A 16× reduction in prompt length is a 256× reduction in attention score work.

Check yourself: Level 2

Q Why are the scores divided by the square root of the head width? ›
Dot products grow with the vector size. Unscaled, softmax puts nearly all the weight on one token and learning stalls. Dividing by √(head width) keeps the scores in a range where softmax stays soft.
Q What does the causal mask do, and why does a left-to-right model need it? ›
It sets every score for a later token to minus infinity before softmax, so those weights come out as exactly 0. Without it, training would let the model read the token it is supposed to predict.
Q GPT-2 small has a width of 768, but Q @ K.T works on [4, 64]. Where did 64 come from? ›
The width is split across 12 heads: 12 × 64 = 768. Each head runs attention on its own 64 numbers, and the outputs are joined back to 768.
Q A prompt grows from 2,000 to 8,000 tokens. Roughly how much bigger is the score table? ›
16 times. The length is 4 times bigger, and the table grows with the square of the length.
Q Write one modern transformer block in two lines. ›
x = x + attention(norm(x)), then x = x + feed_forward(norm(x)). The x + is the residual connection; the norm comes first in modern models.

12 · The KV cache: memory at generation time

Recall the loop from module 01: generate one token, append it, and run the model again. Without help, every new token would recompute the keys and values of every earlier token.

It does not have to. Because of the causal mask, earlier tokens never look at later ones, so their keys and values never change. Each layer stores them instead: the KV cache. A new token computes its own query, key and value, appends its key and value to the cache, and compares its one query against everything stored.

What is storedThe keys and values of every earlier token, in every layer
How it growsBy one entry per layer for every token processed. It never shrinks during a request.
Where it livesIn GPU memory, for one request. It is freed when the request finishes.
How bigAbout 0.5 MB per token for a 7B model without GQA, such as Llama 2 7B (2 × 32 layers × 4,096 numbers × 2 bytes). That is about 2 GB at 4,000 tokens - per concurrent request.
ReuseSome servers keep it across requests that share the same prompt prefix. This is called prefix caching.

The KV cache, not the weights, is usually what limits how many users one GPU can serve. Grouped-query attention (GQA) lets several heads share one set of keys and values, which shrinks the cache several times over.

14 · Transformer vs RNN, side by side

A reference summary of sections 2 to 12, in the six-label format used across the course.

A Transformer

Basic level

What it is. A neural network built from identical stacked blocks. Each block has a self-attention step, where every position reads every earlier position directly, and a feed-forward step that processes each position on its own.

Core purpose. It removes the sequential dependency between positions, so a whole sequence can be processed in parallel during training and long-range relationships survive intact.

Execution model. All positions move through each block together: make Q, K and V, score every pair, softmax, blend, then feed-forward. The diagram in section 6 shows it.

Intermediate level

Architecture under the hood. One block holds multi-head self-attention, a two-layer feed-forward network, two normalisation steps and two residual connections. Blocks are identical in shape and stacked - 12 in GPT-2 small, 32 in a typical 7B model - with no change in vector width from bottom to top.

State handling. During training there is no state between positions: the whole sequence is one matrix. During generation the KV cache stores the keys and values already computed, so each new token attends to them instead of recomputing everything.

Limitations and advanced features. Attention compares every pair of positions, so compute and memory grow with the square of sequence length. The KV cache grows linearly and often dominates GPU memory at long context. Mitigations include grouped-query attention, sliding-window attention, and FlashAttention, which changes how the same maths is executed rather than the maths itself.

B RNN and LSTM

Basic level

What it is. A recurrent neural network processes a sequence one position at a time, carrying a fixed-size hidden state vector forward from each step to the next.

Core purpose. It handles sequences of any length with a fixed, small amount of memory and a cost that grows linearly rather than quadratically.

Execution model. One position at a time: combine the token with the hidden state, update the state, emit an output, move on. The diagram in section 2 shows it.

Intermediate level

Architecture under the hood. A plain RNN applies one weight matrix to the input and another to the previous hidden state, then a non-linearity. An LSTM adds three learned gates - input, forget and output - plus a separate cell state, so the network can hold a value across many steps without it being rewritten each time. A GRU merges those gates into two.

State handling. One hidden vector, overwritten at every position. Everything the model knows about all previous tokens must fit in it. Nothing else survives between steps.

Limitations and advanced features. Positions cannot be computed in parallel, so training does not scale with GPU width. Gradients shrink or explode across long chains, which the LSTM gates reduce rather than remove. Modern state-space models such as Mamba revisit the same idea with a formulation that can be parallelised during training.

The core differences

TransformerRNN / LSTM
Workflow design All positions computed together. Path length between any two positions is 1. Positions computed in order. Path length between positions i and j is the distance between them.
Inspecting a wrong answer Attention weights can be inspected to see which positions a layer drew on. Treat them as a clue, not an explanation: research has shown they can mislead about why a model produced an answer. The hidden state is one opaque vector, so there is not even a per-position clue to inspect.
Architectural paradigm Direct, learned, all-pairs lookup over the sequence. A compressed running summary carried forward step by step.

In short - which one to choose

Use a transformer when:

  • You are working with language, which is essentially always in this course.
  • Training throughput matters and you have parallel hardware.
  • Relationships between distant positions matter.

A recurrent model is still reasonable when:

  • The sequence is very long and the memory per step must stay constant.
  • You are running on a small embedded device with strict memory limits.
  • Input arrives continuously and there is no fixed window to attend over.

Do not spend time on this comparison when:

  • You are choosing an API model. This decision was made for you years ago.
  • What you actually need is a longer context, which is a serving question, not an architecture one.

16 · Interview preparation

Beginner What problem did the transformer solve over RNNs? ›

RNNs processed tokens sequentially (slow, un-parallelizable) and struggled with long-range dependencies due to vanishing gradients. The transformer uses self-attention to connect any two tokens directly in one parallel step, enabling both long-range modeling and GPU-efficient training.

Beginner In attention, what are Q, K, and V? ›

Three learned linear projections of each token's embedding. Query = what this token is looking for; Key = what each token offers for matching; Value = the information each token contributes. Output = softmax(Q·Kᵀ/√d)·V - a relevance-weighted blend of values.

Intermediate Why divide attention scores by √dₖ? ›

In high dimensions, dot products have large variance (∝ dₖ), pushing softmax toward a near one-hot distribution where gradients vanish. Scaling by √dₖ normalizes the variance, keeping the softmax soft and the model trainable.

Intermediate Why do residual connections matter for deep transformers? ›

Each sub-layer computes x + f(x). The identity path lets gradients flow directly to earlier layers (avoiding vanishing gradients) and lets each block learn a refinement rather than a full transformation, making 100+ layer stacks trainable.

Senior Your model degrades when prompts exceed ~8k tokens. Why, and what are your options? ›

Likely exceeding the trained context length - positional encodings are extrapolating to unseen positions, and attention quality drops (lost-in-the-middle). Options: switch to a longer-context model; apply RoPE scaling/interpolation; reduce input via better retrieval/chunking (RAG, End-to-End); summarize or compress context; or use sliding-window/long-context architectures. Measure with a needle-in-haystack eval before/after.

Senior Explain the KV cache in terms of the causal mask. ›

Because of the causal mask, token i's keys/values never depend on future tokens and never change once computed. So during autoregressive generation we cache each token's K and V; generating token t+1 only requires computing its own Q and attending over the cached K/V - turning per-step cost from O(t²) recompute into O(t). The cache size grows linearly with context, which is the dominant inference memory cost (mitigated by GQA/paged attention).

Staff · design Design an inference-serving layer to maximize throughput for a 70B model. ›

Use a server like vLLM/TGI exploiting architectural facts: continuous batching (interleave requests at token granularity since each step is independent), paged KV cache (non-contiguous KV blocks → near-zero memory fragmentation, more concurrency), FlashAttention kernels, tensor/pipeline parallelism across GPUs for the large FFN/attention matmuls, quantization (e.g. FP8/INT8) to fit weights and speed matmuls, and GQA models to shrink the cache. Add prefix/prompt caching for shared system prompts. Tune the batch/context tradeoff against the O(n²) attention curve and your latency SLO.

17 · In short, and what comes next

The five ideas from the top of the page. Try to say each one before you read it.

  1. Attention is score, softmax, blend. A word scores every earlier word, turns the scores into percentages, and takes a weighted mix of what those words carry.
  2. Query, key and value. What a word is looking for, what it offers, and what it passes on. All three are learned.
  3. Attention moves information between words; the feed-forward network processes each word. Stack that pair many times and you have the whole model.
  4. Attention needs help with order. Position has to be supplied, and the causal mask stops a word from reading the words after it.
  5. Cost grows with the square of the length. At serving time the KV cache, not the weights, is usually what runs out.
🔑
The one idea to remember

Attention moves information between positions; the feed-forward network does the work at each one. Every other part of the block - residuals, norms, multiple heads, positional information - exists to make those two operations trainable and order-aware.
