Vector databases

Page 03 compared a question against every stored vector. That is exact, and it stops being fast at around a million vectors. A vector database finds near matches by checking only a few thousand candidates - and accepts being occasionally wrong in exchange. This page is built in three levels: read Level 1 first, and stop there if you like.

Everything on this page comes back to five ideas. Level 1 covers the first three.

  1. Checking every vector is exact but does not scale. The work grows with the collection, and past about a million vectors it is too slow.
  2. An index checks a few thousand candidates, whatever the size. The most common one is HNSW, a layered graph.
  3. The price is approximate answers. The share of true matches you get back is called recall, and nothing reports it unless you measure it.
  4. Filters belong inside the search, on indexed fields. That is also where permissions are enforced.
  5. Start with pgvector, and give every chunk a stable id. Move to a dedicated vector database only when a measured limit pushes you.
🧠
Memory hook

Motorway, main road, side street. HNSW finds a vector the way you find a house abroad, and its three dials fit that trip: M is how many roads leave each junction (set when you build), ef_construction is how carefully the roads were surveyed (also build time), and ef_search is how many side streets you check before you stop - the one dial you turn live.

1 · What you already know

From 03 Embeddings: every chunk of text becomes a vector, scaled to length 1. A question becomes a vector too, and the best matches are the stored vectors with the highest cosine score.

You also know that something is always nearest, so a minimum score is compulsory.

This page is about what happens when there are 10 million vectors instead of 10,000.

2 · The problem: checking everything does not scale

Comparing a question with every stored vector is called a full scan. Its cost grows in step with the collection. With 768 numbers per vector:

VectorsMemoryWork per questionVerdict
10,00031 MB7.7 million multiply-addsFine
1,000,0003.1 GB768 million multiply-addsSlow once real traffic arrives
10,000,00031 GB7.7 billion multiply-addsImpractical for every question

And three more needs arrive at the same time as the scale:

  • Filter during the search. "Only documents this user's team may see" must limit what is searched, not just what is shown.
  • Update in place. When a document changes, its old vectors must go without rebuilding everything.
  • Survive a restart. Rebuilding 31 GB in memory on every deploy is not a system.

All of these are solved by one thing: an index that does not look at every vector, inside a database that stores, filters and updates.

3 · The big idea: don't check everything

To find a house in another country, you do not knock on every door. You take a motorway to the right city, main roads to the right district, then walk a few streets. Each stage gets you closer with a few big moves, then small ones.

The most common vector index, HNSW (hierarchical navigable small world), works the same way. It links each vector to a few of its nearest neighbours, in several layers:

Three things to notice:

  • The search only touches the points along its path and their neighbours - a few thousand, not millions.
  • Adding more vectors barely lengthens the path. The layers keep the number of hops small as the collection grows.
  • Each step is greedy: it moves to whichever neighbour is closer to the question. That is what makes it fast, and also what lets it occasionally miss.

The road trip is an analogy, but the layered graph and the greedy walk are exactly what HNSW does.

4 · The price: approximate answers, measured as recall

Because the walk is greedy, it can settle in a good-but-not-best neighbourhood and miss a true nearest neighbour. Indexes like this are called ANN, for approximate nearest neighbour.

How often it misses is measured by recall: of the true top results, what fraction did the index actually return?

Top 4 results
Exact search (checks everything)A, B, C, D
The indexA, B, C, E
Recall@43 of the true 4 found = 3/4 = 0.75

Nothing tells you that E replaced D. No error, no warning. The only way to know your recall is to run exact search on a sample of questions and compare.

Two different "recall@K"s. This one compares the index with exact search - it measures the approximation. The RAG page's recall@K compares results with human-labelled relevant chunks - it measures whether search found the right evidence. An index can score 1.0 here and still return the wrong chunks there. Say which one you mean in an interview.

5 · Worked example: how much work, how much accuracy

Step 1 · Work saved

A collection holds 10,000,000 vectors. A full scan compares the question with all of them. A typical HNSW search computes distances to a few thousand vectors; call it 10,000 to be generous.

10,000,000 ÷ 10,000 = 1,000 times less work per question.

Step 2 · Recall measured on three test questions

Run each question through exact search and through the index, and count how many of the true top 4 the index found:

Test questionTrue top 4 found by the index
14 of 4
23 of 4
34 of 4
Total11 of 12 = recall@4 of 0.92

So the index does about a thousandth of the work and finds 92% of the true top results. That is the trade every vector database makes. Your job is to measure it, then decide whether it is good enough. Real tests use hundreds of questions, not three.

The trade in one line

A vector database swaps exactness for speed. Recall is the price, and you only know the price if you measure it.

Q Why does comparing a question against every vector stop working at scale? ›
The work and memory grow in step with the collection. At 10 million vectors of 768 numbers that is 7.7 billion multiply-adds and 31 GB for every single question.
Q How does an HNSW index avoid checking everything? ›
It links vectors to their near neighbours in layers. A search makes a few long jumps on the sparse top layers, then short steps on the bottom layer, touching only a few thousand vectors.
Q What is recall@4, and how do you measure it? ›
The fraction of the true top 4 results that the index actually returned. Measure it by running exact search on a sample of questions and comparing the two result lists.
Q What does "approximate" in ANN cost you, and why can't you see it? ›
Sometimes a true nearest neighbour is missing from the results. Nothing raises an error - the missing result is simply absent - so only measuring recall reveals it.

6 · How a search runs

Inside the graph walk, HNSW does this:

  1. Build: each vector becomes a node, linked to about M of its nearest neighbours.
  2. Build: nodes are placed in layers at random, with each higher layer holding far fewer nodes. The bottom layer holds all of them.
  3. Search: start at a fixed entry point in the top layer.
  4. Move greedily to whichever neighbour is closer to the question, until no neighbour is closer.
  5. Drop to the next layer down and repeat from that node.
  6. On the bottom layer, keep a list of the best ef_search candidates seen so far, and keep exploring until the list stops improving.
  7. Return the best k from that list.

Step 4 is where it can go wrong: a greedy walk can settle in the wrong region and never reach the true nearest neighbour. A bigger ef_search makes that rarer, at the cost of more work.

The three HNSW settings

IVF is the main alternative to HNSW. It groups vectors into clusters and searches only the clusters nearest the question. It builds faster and uses less memory, but usually has lower recall at the same speed. pgvector offers both (hnsw and ivfflat). Both index the vector type only up to 2,000 dimensions; for 3,072-dimension embeddings, store or index them as halfvec (up to 4,000).

8 · What is stored, and how changes work

A vector database stores the vectors, their payloads, and the graph linking them. One question touches only a small slice of that:

WritingUpsert by id: insert if new, replace if the id exists. The graph is updated incrementally.
DeletingUsually soft: the point is marked deleted but stays in the graph until a clean-up pass (compaction), so it still costs memory.
IdsDerive each id from the document id and chunk number, for example handbook-v3#chunk-12. Re-ingesting the same document then replaces its chunks instead of duplicating them.
DurabilityData is persisted to disk, with snapshots for backup. Rebuilding a large HNSW graph from raw vectors can take hours.
Model changesA new embedding model means a new collection. Build it alongside, check it, then switch.

10 · Break it

Filter hard on a field with no index

Search with a filter that only 12 of 10 million points pass, on a field that has no payload index. The walk heads towards the question, but almost every node it reaches is rejected by the filter. It runs out of candidates before finding the 12 that qualify. The results are wrong and nothing says so - people often misdiagnose this as "the embeddings are bad".

Ingest the same document twice with random ids

Every chunk now exists twice. A top-4 search returns two passages, each repeated, the model sees the same text twice, and the collection has doubled in size. Nothing raises.

11 · Fix it

  • Index every field you filter on. With a payload index, a database such as Qdrant can estimate how many points pass a filter, and when the number is small it switches to an exact scan of just those points - correct and fast. pgvector applies the WHERE clause after the approximate index scan; from version 0.8 it can keep scanning until enough rows pass the filter, but only once you switch it on (SET hnsw.iterative_scan = relaxed_order, or the ivfflat equivalent).
  • Measure recall instead of assuming it. Run exact search on a sample of real questions, compare with the index, and re-measure after any settings change.
  • Make ids deterministic. Build them from the document id and chunk number, so re-ingesting replaces instead of duplicating.

Together these turn every silent failure on this page into either a measured number or an impossible state.

Check yourself: Level 2

Q Which HNSW setting do you tune first, and what does raising it do? ›
ef_search, because it can change per query. Raising it improves recall and slows queries, and the recall gain flattens quickly.
Q A filtered search returns only one result when you expected four. What is the likely cause? ›
A selective filter on a field with no payload index: the graph walk keeps reaching rejected points and runs out of candidates. Index the field so the database can switch to an exact scan of the few matching points.
Q Why should chunk ids be derived from the document id and chunk number? ›
So re-ingesting a document replaces its chunks. Random ids create silent duplicates that crowd the top results and double the collection.
Q Why is filtering after the search worse than filtering during it? ›
If the top results are mostly filtered out afterwards, too few remain. And permissions enforced only after the search depend on every caller remembering the filter.
Q What is the difference between HNSW and IVF? ›
HNSW walks a layered graph of near neighbours. IVF groups vectors into clusters and searches only the nearest clusters. IVF builds faster and is smaller; HNSW usually gets higher recall at the same speed.

13 · Vector database vs relational database, side by side

A reference summary in the six-label format used across the course.

A Vector database

Basic level

What it is. A database that stores vectors with their metadata and returns the approximate nearest neighbours of a query vector, using an index such as HNSW or IVF.

Core purpose. It keeps similarity search fast as the collection grows, by checking a small fraction of the vectors instead of all of them.

Execution model. Filter, walk the index to a few thousand candidates, score those exactly, return the top k with their text. The diagram in section 6 shows it.

Intermediate level

Architecture under the hood. The default index is HNSW, a layered graph: a search enters at the sparse top layer, walks greedily towards the query, drops a layer and repeats. M (links per node) is fixed at build time; ef_search (candidates kept during the walk) can change per query. IVF is the alternative: cluster the vectors and search only the nearest clusters.

State handling. Vectors, payloads and the graph are persisted to disk. Inserts update the graph incrementally. Deletes are usually soft until compaction, so deleted points still cost memory and search time.

Limitations and advanced features. Results are approximate, and the miss rate is invisible unless you measure recall. Building HNSW is slow and memory-hungry, and very selective filters interact badly with the graph. Advanced features include quantisation, built-in hybrid search with BM25, multi-tenancy and replication.

B Relational database

Basic level

What it is. A database that stores rows in typed tables and answers queries with exact conditions, using B-tree and hash indexes to avoid reading every row.

Core purpose. It returns exactly the rows that match a condition, with transactional guarantees about what is stored.

Execution model.

Intermediate level

Architecture under the hood. A B-tree keeps keys sorted, so a lookup descends a shallow tree and a range query walks the leaves. That works because the key has one sortable order. Vectors with hundreds of dimensions have no such order, so a B-tree cannot answer "what is nearest" - which is why a different index exists.

State handling. Rows persist with ACID guarantees: a committed write is durable and visible to the next reader, and a transaction applies completely or not at all.

Limitations and advanced features. No built-in notion of "similar" for vectors. pgvector closes most of the gap by adding vector columns and HNSW or IVF indexes to Postgres, so one database holds rows, metadata and vectors together - usually the right first choice.

The core differences

Vector databaseRelational database
Workflow design Ask for the k nearest vectors and get a ranked, approximate list. Tuning trades recall against latency. Ask for rows matching a condition and get all of them, exactly. There is nothing to trade.
When something goes wrong A missed neighbour raises nothing - it is silently absent. Only measuring recall against exact search reveals it. A wrong result is a bug in the query, and the same query always returns the same rows, so it is reproducible.
Architectural paradigm Approximate nearest-neighbour search over a graph or clusters. Exact set retrieval over a sorted index.

In short - which one to choose

Use a dedicated vector database when:

  • You are past roughly ten million vectors, or need very high query throughput.
  • Vector search is the product and you need its tuning settings exposed.
  • You need built-in hybrid search and reranking.

Use pgvector in your existing Postgres when:

  • You have up to a few million vectors, which covers most systems.
  • The vectors belong next to rows you already store, and you want one backup story.
  • You would rather not run another database. This is the right default.

Use neither when:

  • You have fewer than roughly 100,000 vectors (a rule of thumb - it depends on dimensions and query rate). Comparing against all of them in memory is exact, fast enough, and has no recall to worry about.
  • You have not yet measured whether retrieval is your bottleneck.

15 · Interview preparation

Beginner What is a vector database for? ›

To store embeddings and answer "which stored vectors are nearest to this query vector?" at scale and low latency, using approximate nearest-neighbor indexes - plus metadata filtering. It's the storage + retrieval engine of RAG.

Beginner What does "approximate" mean in ANN? ›

The index may not return the exact true nearest neighbors every time - it returns very-close ones much faster than brute force. The miss rate (1 − recall) is small and tunable; you trade a little accuracy for big speed gains.

Intermediate Explain HNSW and its key parameters. ›

A hierarchical navigable small-world graph: multiple layers, sparse on top for long hops, dense at the bottom holding all vectors. Search starts at the top and greedily descends toward the query - roughly O(log N). Params: M (edges/node → recall & memory), ef_construction (build-time quality), and ef_search (query-time candidates → the live recall/latency dial).

Intermediate Pre-filtering vs post-filtering - when does it matter? ›

Post-filtering runs ANN then discards non-matches - fine for permissive filters, but selective filters can leave you with too few or zero results because the top-k were all filtered out. Pre-filtering restricts the search space to matching vectors first, giving correct top-k for selective filters. Use pre-filtering for tenant/permission/date constraints.

Senior Recall is fine in tests but bad in prod. Diagnose. ›

Suspects: (1) production data distribution differs from test; (2) ef_search lowered for latency; (3) selective metadata filters + post-filtering starving results; (4) index not rebuilt after a bulk load, or stale after deletes; (5) embedding model/version drift between index and query (03 Embeddings). Reproduce with a flat-index ground truth on prod queries, compare recall@k, and isolate the variable. Fix the specific cause - often raise ef_search or switch to pre-filtering.

Staff · design Design vector search for 1B vectors, multi-tenant, <50ms p99. ›

1B × 768 floats won't fit one node in RAM as raw HNSW - use IVF+PQ or quantized HNSW to compress, and shard by tenant or hash with replicas for HA. Pre-filter by tenant (route to the tenant's shard for isolation + smaller search space). Tune ef_search per route to hit p99 under the recall SLO; add a small re-rank stage (05 RAG, End-to-End) on the top candidates to recover precision lost to quantization. Operate: background re-index jobs for model upgrades (versioned vectors), warm caches, monitor recall@k continuously with a sampled ground-truth job, and capacity-plan RAM. Consider a tiered design: quantized index for breadth, exact rescoring on finalists.

The one idea to remember

A vector database trades exactness for speed, and the trade is invisible by default. Measuring recall, indexing filter fields and using stable ids are what make that trade something you chose rather than something that happened to you.
