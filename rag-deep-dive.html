RAG, End-to-End

Module 01 showed that a model only knows what was in its training data. Modules 03 and 04 showed how to find text by meaning. RAG - retrieval-augmented generation - puts the two together: search your documents first, then let the model answer from what it found. It is one of the most-asked topics in GenAI interviews, so this page builds it in three levels. Read Level 1 first.

Everything on this page comes back to five ideas. Level 1 covers the first three.

  1. RAG is search, then answer from what you found. The model answers from text you put into the prompt, not from its memory.
  2. There are two pipelines. Indexing runs ahead of time: split, embed, store. Querying runs for every question: embed, search, build the prompt, generate.
  3. Retrieval always returns something. A score floor and permission to say "I don't know" are what stop invented answers.
  4. Quality is decided before the model runs. Chunking, hybrid search and reranking decide whether the right text reaches the prompt.
  5. The two halves fail separately. Measure retrieval (did we find it?) and generation (did we use it faithfully?) on their own.
🧠
Memory hook

The 4 Nots: Not there, Not found, Not ranked, Not used. When an answer is wrong, walk them in order: the answer was not in the documents, retrieval did not find it, ranking cut it, or the model did not use it. Each has its own fix - section 9.

Level 1The core ideaAbout 25 minutes. One real-looking example, start to finish.

1 · What you already know

From module 01: a model's knowledge is frozen in its weights at training time. It reads only what fits in its context window, and it predicts plausible text - including when it does not actually know the answer.

From module 03: an embedding turns text into a vector, and texts with similar meaning get vectors that are close together.

From module 04: a vector database stores millions of those vectors and finds the nearest ones to a query in milliseconds.

This page joins those pieces into one system.

2 · The problem: the model has never seen your documents

Ask a model "How many weeks of paid leave do I get when I have a baby?" and it cannot know your company's answer. Your handbook was never in its training data. So it does what it always does and writes something plausible:

⚠️
Without RAG

"Parental leave depends on your country and employer. Many companies offer between 12 and 26 weeks..." - fluent, generic, and no help to you. Worse, a model sometimes states a specific number with full confidence. That is called a hallucination.

There are three ways to give a model your knowledge:

ApproachHow the model gets the knowledgeCost of a change
Fine-tuneTrain it further on your documents, changing its weightsAnother training run
Long contextPaste the whole handbook into every promptTokens on every request, forever - and it stops working once the handbook no longer fits
RAGFind the few relevant paragraphs and paste only thoseRe-index the one document that changed

RAG wins for knowledge because knowledge changes and weights do not. A policy updated this morning is answerable this afternoon. Pasting everything is a fine choice while your documents are small and stable; RAG is what you do once they are not.

3 · The big idea: look it up, then answer

An analogy: a closed-book exam versus an open-book exam. Without RAG, the model answers from memory. With RAG, it first finds the right pages, then answers with those pages open in front of it - and can tell you which page it used.

A RAG system has two parts that run at different times:

  • Indexing happens ahead of time. Each document is cut into short passages called chunks, each chunk is turned into a vector, and the vector is stored together with the chunk's text and where it came from.
  • Querying happens for every question. The question is turned into a vector, the store returns the chunks closest in meaning, and those chunks are pasted into the prompt next to the question.

The model itself is unchanged. RAG is not a new kind of model - it is a way of building the prompt.

4 · One question, traced end to end

A company has indexed its employee handbook. Here are four of its chunks, each stored with its source:

ChunkSourceText
[1]handbook.pdf, p.14, "Parental leave"Employees who become parents through birth or adoption receive 16 weeks of fully paid parental leave. Leave must start within 12 months of the birth or adoption. Tell HR at least 8 weeks before you plan to start.
[2]handbook.pdf, p.12, "Sick leave"Employees receive 10 days of paid sick leave per year. A doctor's note is required for absences longer than 3 consecutive days.
[3]handbook.pdf, p.20, "Remote work"Employees may work remotely up to 3 days a week with their manager's approval.
[4]handbook.pdf, p.31, "Public holidays"The office is closed on the public holidays listed below.

Step 1 · Search

The question "How many weeks of paid leave do I get when I have a baby?" is embedded and compared with every chunk. Each chunk gets a similarity score: higher means closer in meaning.

Notice that the question never says "parental" or "birth". Chunk 1 still wins, because embeddings compare meaning, not exact words. The floor - a minimum score, here 0.50 - drops the two chunks that are plainly unrelated.

Step 2 · Build the prompt

The kept chunks are numbered and pasted into the prompt, under three instructions:

Answer the question using ONLY the sources below.
Cite the source number after every claim, like [1].
If the sources do not contain the answer, reply exactly: NOT_IN_CONTEXT

[1] handbook.pdf, p.14, "Parental leave"

of fully paid parental leave. Leave must start within 12 months of the

[2] handbook.pdf, p.12, "Sick leave"

is required for absences longer than 3 consecutive days.

Question: How many weeks of paid leave do I get when I have a baby?

All three instructions earn their place: the first keeps the model to your evidence, the second makes every claim checkable, and the third gives it a permitted way to say it does not know. That third one is the instruction people forget.

Step 3 · Generate, with citations

✅
With RAG

"You get 16 weeks of fully paid parental leave [1]. It must start within 12 months of the birth, and you need to tell HR at least 8 weeks before it starts [1]."

The model ignored chunk 2 because it does not answer the question - it was given evidence, not told what to say. The [1] is a citation: anyone can open page 14 and check the answer. That traceability is half the value of RAG.

5 · When the answer is not there

Now ask "Can I bring my dog to the office?" The handbook has no pet policy. But search never comes back empty: it returns the closest chunks it has, however far away they are. Here the best is remote work at 0.38, and every score is below the 0.50 floor.

A naive pipeline with no floor sends those chunks to the model anyway:

The model, handed a chunk about remote work and asked about dogs, may write "Pets are allowed on remote-work days [3]". Nothing errors. The answer looks checked, and it is made up.

Two guards stop this:

  • The floor. If no chunk scores above it, skip the model and reply "I couldn't find this in the handbook."
  • The refusal instruction. If weak chunks do get through, the model has a permitted answer: NOT_IN_CONTEXT. Your application shows that as "not found", not as an answer.

Retrieval always returns something. Deciding whether it deserves to be in the prompt is the hard part of RAG.

6 · The two pipelines, step by step

Indexing - ahead of time, once per document version:

  1. Load. Read each source into text plus metadata: file, page, section, date, who may see it. Metadata set here is what a citation shows later.
  2. Split. Cut the text into chunks, copying the metadata onto each one.
  3. Embed. Turn each chunk into a vector, and record which embedding model did it. Vectors from different models cannot be compared.
  4. Store. Write the vector, the chunk text and the metadata under a stable id.

Querying - for every question:

  1. Rewrite (optional). Turn the user's message into a good search query - for example, resolve "what about adoption?" using the conversation so far.
  2. Retrieve. Search for the 20-50 most similar chunks, usually by meaning and by keyword together (section 7).
  3. Rerank. A slower, more accurate model scores each candidate against the question and reorders them.
  4. Cut. Keep the chunks above the floor, up to a token budget - often 3 to 5.
  5. Build the prompt. Number the chunks and add the three instructions from section 4.
  6. Generate. Return the answer together with the ids of the chunks it was given.

Retrieve and rerank are separate steps for a reason. Retrieval is cheap and approximate, so it can scan millions of chunks. Reranking is accurate and expensive, so it only looks at fifty.

Indexing choices

Chunk size

SizeGood forFails at
200–400 tokensFAQs, short policies, precise lookupAnything needing surrounding context
500–800 tokensGeneral prose. A common starting pointVery long procedures
1,000–1,500 tokensNarrative, legal clausesPrecision - the vector blurs

Overlap. Repeat the end of one chunk at the start of the next - typically 10–20% of the chunk size - so a sentence cut at a boundary is whole in at least one chunk.

Split on structure. Cut at headings and paragraphs rather than at a fixed character count. A recursive splitter tries headings first, then paragraphs, then sentences, and only falls back to raw length.

A chunk must fit the embedding model's input limit. Many classic embedding models stop at 512 tokens, some at 256, and anything longer is silently cut off before it is embedded - so a 1,200-token chunk on a 512-token model is searched by its first 512 tokens only. Check the limit before you pick a size.

There is no universally best chunk size. Start from a sensible default and measure (section 11).

Retrieval choices

Hybrid search: meaning plus keywords

Dense search compares embeddings. It handles paraphrase well - "have a baby" finds "parental leave" - but is weak on exact identifiers. Keyword search, usually an algorithm called BM25, scores a chunk by how many of the query's words it contains, giving extra weight to rare words. Search for error "E-4012" and BM25 finds the one chunk with that exact string; dense search may not.

Hybrid search runs both and merges the two ranked lists. A common way is reciprocal rank fusion (RRF): each list gives a chunk 1 ÷ (60 + its rank), and the scores are added.

ChunkKeyword rankDense rankRRF score
A181/61 + 1/68 = 0.0311
B321/63 + 1/62 = 0.0320
Cnot found11/61 = 0.0164

B wins: ranking well in both lists beats coming first in only one. Hybrid search is often one of the two biggest upgrades over a basic pipeline - reranking is the other. Which helps more depends on your data, so measure both.

Query rewriting

The user's message is often a poor search query. Two common fixes: rewrite a follow-up into a standalone question ("what about adoption?" becomes "parental leave for adoption"), or expand one question into several searches and merge the results.

Reranking

Search uses a bi-encoder: the question and each chunk are embedded separately, so chunk vectors can be computed in advance and search is fast. A reranker is a cross-encoder: it reads the question and one chunk together and scores the pair. That is far more accurate, but nothing can be computed in advance, so it is only affordable on a shortlist. Retrieve 50 cheaply, rerank those, keep the best few.

Parent-document retrieval and metadata filtering

Parent-document retrieval searches small chunks for precision, then sends the model the larger section each one came from, for context. Metadata filtering restricts the search before it runs - by team, date or document type. Done with the user's permissions, it is your access control: a user must never retrieve a chunk they are not allowed to read.

Generating

The grounding prompt is the three instructions from section 4. Citations should be checked, not trusted: a model can cite the wrong number, so verify that the cited chunk actually supports the claim. Order matters: models use information at the start and end of a long prompt more reliably than information in the middle, so put the strongest chunk first.

Correcting: CRAG and Self-RAG

Basic RAG trusts whatever search returned. Two well-known patterns add checks:

  • CRAG (corrective RAG) grades the retrieved chunks first. Good chunks are used, and can be trimmed to their useful sentences. Bad ones trigger a fallback, such as a web search.
  • Self-RAG also checks the answer: does the question need retrieval at all, are the chunks relevant, is every claim supported by them, does the answer address the question? In most production versions, a failed check sends it back to rewrite the query and try again.

The original Self-RAG paper works differently. It trained one model to emit these checks as special reflection tokens - Retrieve, IsRel, IsSup and IsUse - which decide when to retrieve and pick the best-supported answer segment. The paper does not rewrite the query. The rewrite-and-retry loop above is the common production variant (the LangGraph tutorial builds this one), which runs the checks as separate model calls - which is why the loop needs an attempt limit. Both patterns multiply latency and cost - add them only when measurement says basic retrieval is not good enough.

9 · Break it: one wrong answer, four possible causes

A user reports a wrong answer. Four different failures produce that same symptom, and each needs a different fix:

FailureWhere it happensHow you tell
The answer is not in the documentsNowhere - it never existedThe top score is below the floor
It is there but was not retrievedRetrievalSearch the raw documents for it; if it is there, retrieval missed it
It was retrieved but ranked below the cutRankingLook further down the candidate list, or raise the number kept, and check again
It was in the prompt and the model ignored itGenerationRead the logged prompt: the evidence is there and the answer contradicts it

You cannot fix it until you know which one it is - and a pipeline that does not log what it retrieved gives you no way to tell.

10 · Fix it

  1. Calibrate a floor. Collect questions you know are answerable and questions you know are not. Pick the score that best separates them, enforce it, and re-check it whenever the embedding model changes.
  2. Permit refusal. Keep the third instruction, and treat NOT_IN_CONTEXT as a status your application handles - never as text shown as an answer.
  3. Verify citations. Check that each cited chunk exists and supports the claim.
  4. Log every request. Question, retrieved chunk ids and scores, prompt version, answer. The ids are what let you explain an answer next month.
  5. Measure the two halves separately - next section.

11 · Measuring RAG: did we find it, and did we use it?

Retrieval recall asks whether the right chunk reached the prompt. Faithfulness (also called groundedness) asks whether every claim in the answer is supported by the chunks given. Read together, they tell you where to look:

Faithfulness highFaithfulness low
Recall highWorking. Check answer quality.Fix generation: the prompt or the model
Recall lowFix retrieval: chunking, hybrid search, rerankingFix retrieval first, then re-measure

Chasing the wrong half is the most common way weeks are lost on a RAG system.

Build a small evaluation set

Write 50–100 real questions, each with the expected answer and the section that contains it - for example "What is the work-from-home policy?" → handbook.pdf, section 4. Include some questions the documents cannot answer, so you can test refusal too. When you compare two setups, change one thing at a time and keep the questions fixed.

The metrics

MetricQuestion it answersExample
Recall@KDid the right chunk appear in the top K? (With one right chunk per question this is also called hit rate. It is not the vector-database recall@K, which compares the index with exact search.)Right chunk in the top 5 for 90 of 100 questions → Recall@5 = 90%
Precision@KHow many of the top K are relevant?2 of the top 5 relevant → Precision@5 = 40%

  1. Did we find it? Recall@K, Precision@K, MRR.
  2. Did we find enough? Context recall.
  3. Was it relevant? Context precision.
  4. Was the answer good? Faithfulness and answer correctness.

Check yourself: Level 2

Q Why retrieve 50 chunks and rerank them, instead of just retrieving the top 4? ›
Search is cheap but approximate; the right chunk is often in the top 50 but not the top 4. A cross-encoder reranker is much more accurate but too slow to run over everything, so it reorders the shortlist.
Q Users search for an error code like "E-4012" and get unrelated chunks. What do you add? ›
Keyword search (BM25) alongside dense search - hybrid search. Embeddings are weak on exact identifiers; keyword search matches the literal string.
Q A RAG answer is wrong. What do you check first? ›
Whether the right chunk was in the prompt, from the logs. That one check tells you whether it is a retrieval problem (not found, or ranked too low) or a generation problem (found but ignored).
Q Retrieval recall is 95% but faithfulness is 70%. Which half do you work on? ›
Generation. The right evidence is arriving; the model is not sticking to it. Fix the prompt (stricter grounding, refusal allowed) or the model.
Q In three test questions, the first correct chunk is at ranks 1, 2 and 5. What is the MRR? ›
(1/1 + 1/2 + 1/5) ÷ 3 = 1.7 ÷ 3 ≈ 0.57.

14 · RAG vs fine-tuning, side by side

The comparison interviewers ask for most, in the six-label format used across the course.

A RAG

Basic level

What it is. A pattern in which the application retrieves relevant text at request time and includes it in the prompt, so the model answers from supplied evidence rather than from its weights.

Core purpose. It gives a model access to private or changing information without retraining it, and makes each answer traceable to a source.

Execution model. Index ahead of time; for each question embed, retrieve, rerank, build the prompt and generate. The diagram in section 6 shows the query side.

Intermediate level

Architecture under the hood. Two pipelines that run at different times. Indexing: load, split, embed, store. Querying: embed the question, retrieve, optionally rerank with a cross-encoder, assemble the prompt, generate, attach citations. The retriever and the generator are separate systems and fail separately, which is why they are evaluated separately.

State handling. The index is persistent state, keyed by chunk id and valid only for the embedding model that built it. Per request nothing persists: the question, the retrieved chunks and the answer live for one call. Conversation history is a separate concern the application carries.

Limitations and advanced features. Retrieval failure is silent - search always returns something. The model can also ignore correct evidence and answer from its weights, which no retrieval fix addresses. Latency is at least two network calls, plus 100–300 ms if reranking. Advanced features are hybrid search, query rewriting, parent-document retrieval, CRAG and Self-RAG.

B Fine-tuning

Basic level

What it is. Fine-tuning continues training an already pre-trained model on a smaller task-specific dataset, changing the weights so the model behaves differently.

Core purpose. It is best at changing behaviour, format and tone. It is an unreliable way to add facts.

Execution model.

Intermediate level

Architecture under the hood. The same forward pass, loss and optimizer as pre-training, on far less data. Full fine-tuning updates every parameter; LoRA freezes the base model and trains small extra matrices per layer, producing an adapter that is a small fraction of the model's size. Preference methods such as DPO train on chosen-versus-rejected answer pairs rather than single targets.

State handling. The output is weights. Nothing is retrieved at request time and nothing changes between requests until you train again.

Limitations and advanced features. A poor way to add facts: the model absorbs the style of the training data while still getting details wrong, and there is no citation to check. Every update needs another run. Too much training on a narrow dataset degrades general ability. It is excellent at format, tone, domain vocabulary and reliable structured output.

The core differences

RAGFine-tuningLong context
Workflow design Index ahead of time, retrieve per request. Updating knowledge means re-indexing one document. Train offline, serve directly. Updating knowledge means another training run. No index. Paste everything into every request, until it stops fitting.
Inspecting a wrong answer You can read exactly what was retrieved and see why. Retrieval failure is silent unless you check it. No evidence trail. The only fix is more data and another run. Nothing to inspect. Facts buried in the middle of a long context are recalled less reliably.
Architectural paradigm Request-time evidence supply. Weight modification. Brute-force context stuffing.

In short - which one to choose

Choose RAG when:

  • The knowledge is private, large, or changes more often than you could retrain.
  • Answers must cite a source.
  • You need to remove a document and have it stop being answerable immediately.

Choose fine-tuning when:

  • You need a consistent format or tone that prompting cannot hold.
  • Domain vocabulary is unusual enough that the base model misreads the question.
  • A smaller tuned model would be cheaper than a large prompted one at your volume.

Choose neither when:

  • The documents fit in the context window and rarely change. Just paste them.
  • You have not tried a better prompt first.
  • You are treating them as alternatives - they solve different problems, and production systems often use both.

16 · Interview preparation

Beginner What is RAG and why use it over fine-tuning? ›

RAG retrieves relevant text at request time and puts it in the prompt, so the model answers from supplied evidence rather than its weights. Fine-tuning changes the weights, which changes behaviour and format but is a poor way to add facts. The deciding factor is usually change rate and traceability: a policy updated this morning is answerable this afternoon with RAG, and every answer can cite the chunk it came from. They are not alternatives - production systems often fine-tune for format and use RAG for knowledge.

Beginner Walk me through a RAG pipeline. ›

Two pipelines. Indexing, offline: load the source, split into chunks, embed each chunk, store the vector with its text and metadata. Querying, per request: embed the question, retrieve candidates, rerank them with a cross-encoder, keep those above a score floor within a token budget, build a prompt that constrains the model to that evidence and permits refusal, generate, and return the answer with the chunk ids used. The two pipelines run at different times and fail in different ways.

Intermediate How do you choose chunk size and overlap? ›

Chunk size decides what one vector represents. Too small and the chunk lacks the context to be understood on its own; too large and its vector averages several topics into a direction matching none of them. I start at 500–800 tokens for prose with 10–20% overlap so a sentence split across a boundary survives intact in one chunk, check that this fits the embedding model's input limit (many classic models stop at 512 tokens and silently cut the rest), and I split on structure - headings, paragraphs - rather than a character count. Then I measure retrieval recall on a labelled set and adjust, because the right number is corpus-specific and no default survives contact with real documents.

Intermediate How do you evaluate a text chunking strategy? ›

I wouldn't evaluate chunking in isolation. I would create a representative evaluation dataset of questions and expected source information, run different chunking strategies through the same embedding and retrieval pipeline, and compare retrieval metrics such as Recall@K, Precision@K and MRR, followed by end-to-end metrics such as context recall, context precision, answer correctness and faithfulness.

Intermediate Your RAG system gives a wrong answer. How do you debug it? ›

First separate the two halves, because four different failures produce the same symptom. Check whether the evidence exists in the corpus at all - grep for it. If it is there, check whether retrieval returned it; if not, that is a chunking, hybrid-search or reranking problem. If it was returned but ranked below the cut, raise k and re-check. If it was in the prompt and the answer contradicts it, that is generation, and the fix is the prompt or the model. I track retrieval recall and groundedness as separate metrics precisely so this question is answerable in one look rather than by guesswork.

Intermediate Why add a reranker when you already have similarity search? ›

Similarity search uses a bi-encoder: query and document are embedded separately, so documents can be embedded in advance and the search is cheap over millions. A reranker is a cross-encoder that reads the question and the chunk together, which is far more accurate but cannot be precomputed. So you use the cheap one to get 50 candidates out of millions and the expensive one to order those 50. In the illustrative trace in section 8 that costs about 210 ms - measure your own - and it is often one of the largest quality gains in the pipeline, alongside hybrid search.

Senior How do you stop a RAG system answering when it should not? ›

Retrieval always returns something, so the pipeline must decide whether what came back is good enough. Three layers. A score floor calibrated on labelled answerable and unanswerable questions, enforced in code - not guessed. A grounding prompt that explicitly permits refusal, returning a sentinel like NOT_IN_CONTEXT. And a distinct status in the response so "no evidence", "refused" and "answered" are three different things the caller handles differently. The failure I am guarding against is an off-corpus question producing a fluent invented answer with a citation attached, which looks more verified than an uncited one.

Senior When would you use CRAG or Self-RAG? ›

CRAG grades the retrieved evidence as correct, ambiguous or incorrect, and either refines it, supplements it with web search, or replaces it entirely. Self-RAG adds checks on the answer - is it grounded in the evidence, does it address the question. In the paper one trained model emits these as reflection tokens and picks the best-supported answer; production versions usually run them as separate grader calls and loop back to rewrite the query if a check fails. I would reach for CRAG when corpus coverage is patchy and an external fallback is acceptable, and Self-RAG when a wrong answer is expensive enough to justify three to nine model calls per query instead of one. Both need bounded attempts, and both are graphs rather than chains, so LangGraph rather than LCEL.

Staff · design Design RAG over confidential documents with per-user access control. ›

Authorisation belongs at retrieval, as a metadata filter derived from the caller's identity, with the filtered fields payload-indexed - post-filtering means you already searched data the user cannot see, and it also silently breaks top-k. Redact PII at ingestion, not at generation, because once it is in the index it is retrievable. Store the permission attributes - ACL or group ids - on each chunk at index time, resolve the caller's groups at query time, and filter on them before any evidence reaches the model. When a document's permissions change, update the chunk metadata at once so the filter excludes it immediately; deletion by document id is for documents that are actually removed (vector databases already exclude deleted or soft-deleted records from search). Log the question, retrieved chunk ids, scores and prompt version for every answer, since the chunk ids are the only way to explain an answer during an audit months later. Gate prompt and model changes on an eval set measuring retrieval recall and groundedness separately, and alert on the refusal rate - a sudden drop usually means the index broke, not that the questions got easier.

17 · In short, and what comes next

The five ideas from the top of the page. Try to say each one before you read it.

  1. RAG is search, then answer from what you found. The model answers from text you put into the prompt, not from its memory.
  2. There are two pipelines. Indexing runs ahead of time: split, embed, store. Querying runs for every question: embed, search, build the prompt, generate.
  3. Retrieval always returns something. A score floor and permission to say "I don't know" are what stop invented answers.
  4. Quality is decided before the model runs. Chunking, hybrid search and reranking decide whether the right text reaches the prompt.
  5. The two halves fail separately. Measure retrieval (did we find it?) and generation (did we use it faithfully?) on their own.
🔑
The one idea to remember

RAG is prompt construction with a search step, and every hard problem in it is deciding whether the search result deserves to be in the prompt. Chunking, hybrid search, reranking, floors, CRAG and Self-RAG are all answers to that one question, at increasing cost.
