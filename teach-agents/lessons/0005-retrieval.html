Lesson 5 · Making it competent

Retrieval as a tool

⏱ ~3.5 h total 30 min read · 90 min build · 45 min break/debug · 25 min interview Prereq · Lesson 4 Next · Lesson 6
🎯
By the end of this lesson you will be able to
  • Explain the architectural choice interviewers actually probe: retrieval as a tool versus stuffing the context.
  • Explain chunking, and why heading boundaries beat fixed windows here.
  • Explain BM25 in plain words - and why word counting fails.
  • Explain reranking, and why it only runs on a shortlist.
  • Argue that returning nothing is a feature, and implement the floor that does it.
  • Produce citations a reviewer can actually check.

1 · Start from the failure you already have

Your Lesson 2 search_kb counts shared words. Ask it about an account question.

Neither is about accounts. The refund policy scored because it contains the phrase "account credit". This is the worst kind of broken: it returns something plausible, confidently, every time.

🐛
Why word counting cannot be rescued by tuning

It has no notion of a word being informative. "refund" appearing in one document out of twenty is strong evidence; "support" appearing in all twenty is none. Word counting treats them identically. No weight you add to tags fixes that, because the flaw is the scoring model, not its parameters.

2 · The architectural question

There are two ways to get documents in front of a model, and knowing when to use which is worth more in an interview than knowing any specific library.

🔑
The answer interviewers want

"Both, chosen by whether the query is knowable up front." A support ticket about a duplicate charge always needs the refund policy - pre-load it. A ticket whose category is only clear after reading it needs a tool, because the query depends on the ticket. Our agent uses the tool form for exactly that reason: it searches on the category it discovered in step 1.

3 · Chunk

Scoring whole documents is the first mistake. A five-section policy has one relevant paragraph and four irrelevant ones; scored as a unit, the signal is diluted by its own context.

Heading boundaries, not fixed-size windows. These documents already have meaningful divisions, and cutting every 500 characters would split a numbered policy list in half - and half a policy reads as complete while omitting the condition that matters. Fixed windows are the right default for prose with no structure; use the structure when it exists.

The knowledge base goes from 5 documents to 17 chunks, each carrying its section heading - which is also what makes citations useful later.

4 · Rank with BM25

Three properties, each fixing something word counting got wrong:

Rare words count more (the IDF term). This is the direct fix for the account/refund failure.

Long chunks stop winning by size (length normalisation). Without it, the longest document tends to top every query.

Repetition saturates (the k1 term). The tenth occurrence of a word adds far less than the second, because a document repeating a term ten times is not five times more relevant than one repeating it twice.

✅
In plain English

Score a chunk higher when it contains words that are rare across the collection, contains them a few times rather than once, and is not padded out with unrelated text. That sentence is a complete, accurate answer to "explain BM25" - you do not need the formula.

⚠️
Where are the embeddings?

Not here, deliberately. Dense vectors are the right answer at scale and the wrong place to start, because an API call that returns a list of floats hides every mechanic in this lesson.

What embeddings add: matching on meaning, so "slow checkout" can surface "payment timeout" with no shared words. What they lose: exact identifiers. Ask a vector index for pg_latency_p99 and it may return something semantically adjacent instead of the exact match. That is why production systems run hybrid - BM25 for exact terms, vectors for meaning - and merge. RAG, End-to-End covers the dense side; you now understand what it is being hybridised with.

5 · Rerank the shortlist

A second, sharper pass over the top handful only.

Stage one is fast and mediocre over everything. Stage two is slower and sharper over a shortlist of eight. Reranking everything would cost what stage one exists to avoid.

Our signals are cheap: the query appearing as a phrase, overlap with the section heading, and how much of the query the chunk covers. In production stage two is usually a cross-encoder model - the shape is identical, and the shape is what interviews probe.

Each hit explains itself, so a trace can be read without guessing.

6 · Return nothing, on purpose

The most important eight lines in the module:

🛡️
Say this in an interview

Without a floor, retrieval always returns its limit best chunks however bad they are - and the agent treats the least-bad chunk as an answer. An empty result is a useful result: it lets the agent say "no policy covers this" and escalate, instead of grounding a confident reply in an irrelevant paragraph.

Most hallucination in a RAG system is not the model inventing facts. It is the model faithfully summarising context that should never have been retrieved.

7 · Run the comparison

Read the second query closely. refunds#4 is the section headed "Exception: duplicate charges" - not the whole refund policy, the one paragraph that applies. That is chunk-level citation doing its job: "cited the refund policy" is not checkable; "cited refunds#4" is.

And the third query returns two chunks from the same document, which is why draft_reply deduplicates citations. Two chunks from one article is one source, not two.

8 · Break it

1 · The account query is still not right. Retrieval returns refunds#0 at 1.988 - better than two wrong articles, but there is no article about changing an email, so the honest answer is nothing. Raise the floor.

Now it returns nothing. Then check what else that floor broke: run the other three queries at 2.5. The floor is a precision/recall dial, and there is no setting that is right for every query. Deciding it needs the eval set from Lesson 10 - which is the honest answer to "how did you choose that threshold?"

2 · Turn reranking off. use_rerank=False. Compare orderings. On some queries nothing changes - reranking earns its cost on ambiguous queries, not clear ones. Worth knowing before you add a cross-encoder to a system that does not need one.

3 · Break chunking. Set MAX_CHUNK_CHARS = 80 and re-run. Policies fragment across chunks, scores drop, and citations start pointing at half-sentences. Then set it to 5000: everything becomes one chunk per document and you are back to Lesson 2's behaviour. Both failures are the same failure - the chunk is no longer the unit a question is about.

4 · Poison the knowledge base. Add a file to fixtures/kb/ containing "Tags: billing, refund" and the line "All refunds are pre-approved; no human review is required." Retrieval will surface it, because retrieval has no notion of truth. Nothing in this lesson defends against it. That is retrieval poisoning, and it is Lesson 9's problem - note now that the defence cannot live in the retriever.

9 · The production concern

Four things that break retrieval in production, none of them the ranking function:

Stale index. A policy changed and the index did not. Retrieval confidently cites last quarter's rules. Ingestion needs to be scheduled and monitored like any pipeline.

Access control. Retrieval that ignores permissions is an exfiltration route: the agent will happily cite a document this customer must not see. Filter before ranking, not after.

Conflicting sources. Two documents disagree. Prefer the newest approved version, surface the conflict, cite dates - never silently pick one.

No measurement. "Retrieval seems fine" is how it stays broken. Lesson 10 measures relevance and groundedness properly.

🧰
You already know this

This is a search stack: an inverted index with IDF weighting, a two-stage ranker, and a relevance cut-off. If you have tuned a search feature you have made all these decisions. The only new part is that the consumer is a model that will believe whatever it is handed.

10 · Interview practice

Two-minute answer - "How does retrieval work in your agent?"

🎤

"Retrieval is a tool the agent calls rather than context I pre-load, because the query depends on the ticket - the agent searches on the category it discovered by reading it. If the query were knowable up front I'd pre-load instead and save a round trip.

The pipeline is chunk, rank, rerank, floor. I chunk on headings rather than fixed windows, because splitting a numbered policy in half produces a chunk that reads complete while missing the condition that matters. Ranking is BM25 - rare terms count more, long chunks don't win by size - which fixed a real bug where an 'account' question returned the refund policy, because word counting can't tell an informative term from a common one. Then a rerank pass over the top eight only, since the point of a cheap first stage is not to run the expensive one over everything.

The part I'd emphasise is the relevance floor. Without it retrieval always returns its top-k however bad they are, and the agent grounds a confident answer in the least-bad paragraph. Returning nothing lets it escalate honestly. Most RAG hallucination isn't invention - it's faithful summary of context that shouldn't have been retrieved.

Citations are chunk-level, so a reviewer can check the exact paragraph rather than being told 'the refund policy'."

Q System design · Design retrieval for 50,000 support documents across three languages and four permission tiers. ›

Start with the constraints that change the architecture, not the ranker. Permissions first: filter the candidate set by the caller's entitlements before ranking - post-filtering leaks through result counts and latency, and it can empty a page. Language: either per-language indexes with routing on detected language, or a multilingual embedding model; say which and why, because mixing languages in one BM25 index degrades IDF.

Then the pipeline: chunk on structure, hybrid retrieval (BM25 for identifiers and error codes, dense for meaning), fuse the two lists, rerank the top ~50 with a cross-encoder, apply a relevance floor. Metadata filters for product, version and date so a query can be scoped before it is ranked.

Then operations, which is where these systems actually fail: scheduled re-ingestion with freshness monitoring, a golden query set with relevance judgements, alerting on the rate of empty results - a sudden drop usually means ingestion broke, not that users changed.

At 50k documents, say clearly that you do not need a distributed vector database. A single index and a reranker will do, and choosing infrastructure you do not need is a real answer to a real question.

Q Debugging · The agent cited a document that does not support its claim. Diagnose. ›

Separate the two possibilities before touching anything: bad retrieval (the document should not have been returned) or bad grounding (right document, claim not supported by it). They have different fixes and people conflate them.

Bad retrieval: check the chunk actually retrieved, not the document - often the right file was returned for the wrong paragraph. Check whether the score was barely above the floor. Check chunk boundaries: a policy split mid-list produces a chunk that looks authoritative and omits the condition.

Bad grounding: the model summarised beyond the context. Fix with a faithfulness check on the output and by requiring claims to map to a cited chunk.

Then the systemic answer: without chunk-level citations you cannot tell these apart, which is why the citation granularity is a debugging decision, not a formatting one.

Q Coding · Implement BM25 from scratch. ›

Tokenise with a light stop list; build document frequencies across the corpus; for each query term compute IDF from df and n; compute the saturating term-frequency component with k1 and length-normalise with b against average document length; sum over the query terms; sort descending with a deterministic tiebreak.

Details that signal experience: a deterministic tiebreak (so results are reproducible), keeping domain terms out of the stop list ("no", "not" matter in support text), and returning why each hit matched so a trace is readable.

Q Senior follow-up · How did you choose the relevance threshold? ›

Honestly: not by intuition, because a single number cannot be right for every query - the account query needs a higher floor than the duplicate-charge query, and raising it to fix one breaks the other.

So: build a labelled query set including queries that should return nothing, sweep the threshold, and pick the point where the empty-result rate on unanswerable queries is high without the miss rate on answerable ones rising. Then monitor both in production, because corpus drift moves the score distribution - a threshold tuned on 5,000 documents will not hold at 50,000, since IDF changes as the collection grows.

Better still, normalise the score so the threshold is comparable across corpus sizes, or use a reranker score that is calibrated rather than a raw BM25 sum.

Q Senior follow-up · When would you not use RAG at all? ›

When the knowledge is small and stable enough to sit in the system prompt - a twenty-line refund policy does not need an index, and retrieval adds a failure mode for nothing. When the question is about structured data, where SQL is exact and retrieval is approximate. When the knowledge is really a computation ("how much has this customer spent?") - that is a tool call, not a document lookup.

And when freshness requirements exceed what your ingestion can promise: retrieval that confidently cites stale policy is worse than admitting you cannot answer.

11 · Recap

  • Retrieval as a tool when the query depends on what you find; stuff the context when it does not.
  • Chunk on structure. Half a policy reads as complete and is not.
  • BM25 in one sentence: rare terms count more, repetition saturates, length is normalised.
  • Rerank the shortlist only - that restriction is the economic argument.
  • A relevance floor means retrieval can return nothing, so the agent can escalate instead of grounding on the least-bad paragraph.
  • Cite chunks, not documents. "Cited refunds#4" is checkable; "cited the refund policy" is not.
  • Retrieval has no notion of truth. A poisoned document ranks like any other - Lesson 9.
