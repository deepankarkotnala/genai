1 · How interviews are actually scored

Before planning what to study, understand what's being measured — it changes how you practise.

A coding interview is not graded solely on whether you reach the optimal solution. Interviewers score a handful of signals, and strong candidates deliberately produce each one out loud:

Problem solving

Do you clarify the problem, consider a brute force, spot the repeated work, and choose a fitting pattern — reasoning aloud rather than jumping to memorised code?

Coding

Is the code clean, correct, and idiomatic? Good names, no dead ends, handles edge cases. Can you turn the idea into working code without flailing?

Communication

Do you narrate your thinking, take hints gracefully, and keep the interviewer with you? Silence and defensiveness are the most common killers.

Verification

Do you test on a tiny case and an awkward one (empty, single, duplicates, overflow) before claiming you're done — and fix bugs calmly?

The takeaway for practice: rehearse the whole loop out loud, not just the algorithm. A correct silent solution scores worse than a slightly slower one delivered with clear reasoning and testing.

The solving loop (say it, every time)

✓
Seven steps, in order

1. Clarify inputs, ranges, edge cases. 2. State a brute force and its complexity. 3. Find the repeated work — the key to the speed-up. 4. Choose a pattern (from §2 / the chapter cheat sheets). 5. State the invariant you'll maintain. 6. Code it cleanly. 7. Test a tiny case and the awkward ones, then state the final complexity.

2 · Role tracks — what to prioritise

All roles benefit from the whole curriculum, but the emphasis differs. Do the Core-for-every-role chapters first, then weight your time toward your track.

Track A · Software Engineer (SDE)

The broadest bar: expect data structures, algorithms, and at least one design-flavoured question. Cover everything, but go deep on graphs, DP, and design.

Priority order
  • Must master: Arrays, Hashing, Two Pointers, Sliding Window, Stacks/Queues, Binary Search, Trees, Graphs, DP 1D, Design.
  • Strongly recommended: Linked Lists, Backtracking, Heaps, Advanced Graphs, Greedy, DP 2D.
  • Round out: Tries, Bit/Math/Matrix, Sorting/Intervals.

Track B · Data Science / Analytics

Expect array/string manipulation, SQL-adjacent grouping logic, and heavy emphasis on clean, efficient pandas/NumPy. Deep graph/DP theory matters less than fluency with data wrangling and complexity awareness.

Priority order
  • Must master: Arrays, Hashing, Strings, Sorting/Intervals, Binary Search, Heaps (top-k), Python/NumPy/Pandas Performance, Complexity.
  • Strongly recommended: Two Pointers, Sliding Window, DP 1D, Design (streaming stats).
  • Lighter touch: advanced graphs, tries, hard interval/interval DP — know the ideas, don't grind them.

Track C · ML / AI / GenAI Engineer

A hybrid bar: the SDE algorithmic core plus data-heavy fluency. Matrix/vector reasoning, efficient data pipelines, and caching/design (think embedding and KV caches) carry extra weight.

Priority order
  • Must master: Arrays, Hashing, Binary Search, Heaps, DP 1D, Bit/Math/Matrix, Design, Python/NumPy/Pandas Performance.
  • Strongly recommended: Trees, Graphs (GNN/message-passing intuition), DP 2D (alignment/Viterbi), Backtracking.
  • Round out: two pointers, sliding window, strings, sorting/intervals.

3 · Study schedules

Pick the plan that matches your runway. Each assumes you do the foundation + a few problems per chapter, then rely on §4's revision set and §5's mocks to consolidate.

The 2-week sprint (interview imminent)

  • Days 1–3: Arrays, Hashing, Two Pointers, Sliding Window — the highest-frequency patterns.
  • Days 4–6: Stacks/Queues, Binary Search, Trees, Heaps.
  • Days 7–9: Graphs (DFS/BFS/union-find), DP 1D, Backtracking.
  • Days 10–11: Design (LRU, two heaps) + your track's priority chapter.
  • Days 12–14: Mocks daily (§5) + the Must-know 12. Review only what you get wrong.

The 6-week plan (balanced)

  • Weeks 1–2: Foundations through Sliding Window (Ch 01–07), ~4 problems/chapter.
  • Weeks 3–4: Stacks through Heaps and Tries (Ch 08–14), plus binary search and backtracking depth.
  • Week 5: Graphs, Advanced Graphs, Greedy, DP 1D & 2D (Ch 15–19) — the heaviest theory.
  • Week 6: Bit/Math/Matrix, Design, your track's chapter; two mocks; start spaced revision (§6).

The 12-week plan (thorough, from rusty)

  • Weeks 1–3: Ch 00–05 slowly, all foundations, ~6 problems/chapter, get complexity fluent.
  • Weeks 4–7: Ch 06–14 — the core structures and search/recursion patterns, with weekly review.
  • Weeks 8–10: Ch 15–21 — graphs, greedy, DP, design; the hardest material, spaced over three weeks.
  • Weeks 11–12: Ch 22 (+ your track), full mock loops twice a week, and the complete revision set (§4).

Why it's on the list. If you can reverse a list cleanly, you understand pointers — the basis of every linked-list manipulation.

6 LC 704Binary Search Must-know Halve a sorted range

Why it's on the list. The gateway to tree recursion — 'do something, then recurse on both children'.

Why it's on the list. Kadane's 'extend or restart' is a mental model you'll reuse for product, circular, and 2D variants.

9 LC 70Climbing Stairs Must-know Fibonacci-style DP

Why it's on the list. The simplest DP recurrence — recognising it as Fibonacci trains you to spot recurrences everywhere.

10 LC 200Number of Islands Must-know Grid DFS / flood fill

Why it's on the list. The prototype for 'count/measure connected components' — a huge fraction of grid questions.

Internalise Each unvisited land cell starts a component; flood it (sink cells) so you count each island once.

Why it's on the list. The canonical variable-size sliding window — shrink on a violation, track the best.

6 · The mock-interview kit

Mocks are the highest-return practice there is — they train the delivery, not just the solution. Do them under realistic constraints.

How to run one

45 minutes, one unseen problem, talking aloud the whole time. Use a plain editor (no autocomplete), a timer, and ideally a partner playing interviewer. Solo? Record yourself and rubber-duck the narration.

Score yourself honestly

After each mock, rate the four signals from §1 (problem solving, coding, communication, verification) 1–4. Your lowest score is next week's focus — most people's is communication or testing, not algorithms.

⚠️
Common failure modes to drill out

Jumping to code before clarifying · going silent when stuck · not stating complexity · skipping tests and declaring victory · arguing with a hint instead of using it · giving up on a brute force when it was an acceptable partial. Each is fixable with deliberate reps.

7 · Spaced revision that sticks

Patterns fade without review. Instead of re-reading, use active recall on a spaced schedule — it's far more efficient than passive review.

A simple system
  • Same day: after solving a problem, write the pattern name and the one-line key idea in your own words.
  • Day 3, Day 7, Day 21: revisit each cheat sheet and recall the trigger→move mapping before looking. Re-solve any problem you can't explain.
  • Weekly: run the revision set in 🙈 Practice mode; anything you can't reproduce goes back into the next cycle.
  • Use the per-chapter cheat sheets as your recall prompts — each chapter's §6 review distils it to trigger→technique lines.

Concept checks

You reach the optimal algorithm but code it in near silence and skip testing. How does that typically score?
Worse than a slightly slower solution delivered with clear reasoning, edge-case tests, and good communication — because problem-solving, communication, and verification are scored alongside the final answer
Best possible, since only the optimal solution matters
It can't be scored because the interviewer didn't see your thinking
Interviews measure signals, not just the endpoint. A silent, untested solution forfeits the communication and verification signals and makes the problem-solving one hard to observe. Narrating your reasoning and testing deliberately often matters more than shaving a log factor.
Why is spaced active recall better revision than re-reading the chapters?
Retrieving the trigger→technique mapping from memory (and re-solving what you can't) strengthens recall far more than passively re-reading, and spacing the reviews fights forgetting efficiently
Re-reading is better; recall wastes time
They are equally effective
Re-reading feels productive but builds weak, recognition-only memory. Forcing yourself to recall the pattern and reproduce the solution — then spacing those attempts over days — is what makes the knowledge retrievable under interview pressure.
