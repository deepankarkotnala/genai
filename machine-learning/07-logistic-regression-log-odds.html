Logistic Regression & Log Odds

The most-interviewed algorithm in machine learning. “Why log odds?” is a signature question because it has a shallow memorised answer and a deep correct one — and the two are easy to tell apart.

Prerequisites · Modules 01, 06Study time · 10 h12 interview questionsHighest priority
🎯
Your interview goal

Deliver the five-argument answer to “why log odds” on a whiteboard cold, derive log-loss and its gradient from the Bernoulli likelihood, and interpret a coefficient as an odds ratio at two different base rates.

1 · From the ground up

🧩
In plain words

You want a probability, and a linear model produces an unbounded number. Those two facts are incompatible, so something has to give. The trick is not to squash the probability into the model — it is to stretch the probability out onto the model's scale. Probabilities live in [0,1]; take odds and you get [0,∞); take the log of the odds and you get the whole real line, which is exactly where w·x lives. Logistic regression is that one substitution, and everything else follows from it.

Start from the failure you are repairing

Fit ordinary linear regression to a 0/1 target and four things break: predictions go outside [0,1]; squared error keeps punishing points that are already confidently correct, so far-away points drag the boundary; the noise is Bernoulli with variance p(1−p), which is not constant, so the Gaussian justification for MSE does not apply; and a straight line implies a constant marginal effect on probability, when probability must flatten out near 0 and 1. Every one of those is a symptom of the same disease — you are modelling a bounded quantity with an unbounded function.

So what: logistic regression is not a different idea from linear regression. It is linear regression applied to a different quantity.

Build the bridge in two steps

Take a probability p, trapped in [0,1]. First form the odds: p/(1−p). A probability of 0.5 becomes odds of 1; 0.9 becomes 9; 0.99 becomes 99. The ceiling is gone, and you are now on [0,∞) — but there is still a floor at zero. Second, take the logarithm: log(p/(1−p)). Odds of 1 become 0, odds below 1 go negative, odds above 1 go positive. You are now on the entire real line, with 0 meaning “even chance”, and the mapping is symmetric — swapping the two classes just flips the sign.

That quantity is the log-odds, or logit. Set it equal to the linear score:

log(p/(1−p)) = w·x

Solve for p and the sigmoid appears — you do not invent it, you derive it: p = 1/(1+e^(−w·x)). The sigmoid is the inverse of the logit, nothing more.

So what: when asked “why the sigmoid?”, the answer is not “because it's S-shaped” — it is “because it is the inverse of the transformation that makes a probability linear-model-shaped”.

Now read the weights, because the interpretation changed

In linear regression, w₁ = 3 means “one more unit of x₁ adds 3 to the prediction”. Here, the prediction being modelled is the log-odds, so w₁ = 3 means “one more unit of x₁ adds 3 to the log-odds”. Exponentiate to get something people can use: e³ ≈ 20, so one more unit multiplies the odds by 20. That multiplier is the odds ratio, and it is constant — which is exactly the property that makes the model linear on this scale.

The consequence people miss: the effect on the probability is not constant. Going from log-odds 0 to 1 moves probability from 0.50 to 0.73 — a big jump. Going from 4 to 5 moves it from 0.982 to 0.993 — almost nothing. Same weight, same one-unit change, wildly different effect on probability, because the sigmoid saturates.

So what: “interpret this coefficient” has a right answer (odds ratio) and a wrong one (percentage-point change in probability). This is one of the most reliable places to separate candidates.

The loss is not a choice either

Apply the Module 01 machine. Each label is a coin flip with probability p, so the likelihood of one observation is p^y (1−p)^(1−y). Take the log, negate, sum: −Σ [y log p + (1−y) log(1−p)]. That is log-loss, also called binary cross-entropy, and it is simply the Bernoulli negative log-likelihood. No design decision was made.

Then the elegant part, worth having ready: differentiate it and the gradient comes out as Xᵀ(p − y) — the same form as linear regression's gradient, with the predicted probability in place of the predicted value. The messy sigmoid derivative cancels exactly against the log in the loss. That cancellation is not luck; it happens because the sigmoid is the canonical link for the Bernoulli distribution, and it is why log-loss is convex in w while squared error on a sigmoid output is not.

Why log-loss and not squared error: what each one charges you for being wrong

Log-loss is unbounded on the wrong side, so a confident mistake is catastrophically expensive and the optimiser is forced to correct it. Squared error tops out at 1, so being 98% sure and wrong costs barely more than being undecided. That is the first reason MSE fails here; losing convexity is the second.

Five independent arguments for the logit — have at least three

Interviewers push on “why log-odds?” until you run out of reasons, so know that there are several distinct ones.

  1. Range matching. Log-odds is the natural unbounded reparameterisation of a probability, so a linear model can output it without ever producing an impossible value.
  2. The canonical link. In the exponential-family formulation, the logit is the canonical link for the Bernoulli distribution, which is exactly what makes the gradient collapse to Xᵀ(p−y) and the loss convex.
  3. Bayes with equal covariances. If both classes are Gaussian with the same covariance matrix, then Bayes' rule proves the log posterior odds are linear in x. Logistic regression is therefore correctly specified for that data — it is not an approximation.
  4. Maximum entropy. Among all distributions matching the observed feature expectations, the least-committal one — maximum entropy — has exactly this form.
  5. Interpretability. Constant additive effects on the log-odds mean constant multiplicative effects on the odds, which is precisely how epidemiology, credit risk and insurance already talk.

Where it goes wrong, and what the failure looks like

Perfect separation. If some feature separates the classes exactly, the likelihood is maximised by pushing that weight to infinity — the model can always make the log-odds more extreme and gain a little more likelihood. You will see coefficients in the hundreds, huge standard errors, and a convergence warning. The cure is any regularisation at all, which is why scikit-learn applies L2 by default and why an unregularised fit on wide data is a bad idea. This is also the mechanism by which a leaked feature announces itself.

It is still a linear boundary. Logistic regression cannot learn XOR or a ring-shaped boundary without your help. It is linear in log-odds space, so if the truth needs interactions or curvature, you must supply them as features — or use a model that finds them itself.

The multi-class extension is softmax, which is the same derivation with a categorical distribution instead of a Bernoulli: exponentiate each class score and normalise. One-vs-rest is the alternative and it does not guarantee probabilities that sum to one, which is a real difference worth knowing.

Analogy — a probability is a ruler that gets compressed at both ends, like a map where the poles are squeezed to points. Log-odds is the Mercator projection of that ruler: the ends stretch out to infinity, so a step is a step everywhere and a straight-line model is finally allowed to work. And just as Mercator makes Greenland look enormous, the log-odds view makes the difference between 0.99 and 0.999 look large — which it is, if you are the one being denied a loan.

3 · Core intuitions

Odds convert a bounded probability into an unbounded ratio; the log makes it symmetric

Read the symmetry in that table: logit(1-p) = -logit(p), and p = 0.5 sits at exactly zero. That is what makes log-odds the natural scale for a linear predictor — both classes are treated symmetrically, and the “no information” point is the origin.

Logistic regression is a linear model wearing a non-linear output

The relationship between the features and the log-odds is linear. The relationship between features and probability is an S-curve, because you pushed the linear predictor through a sigmoid. The decision boundary is w·x + b = 0 — a hyperplane. So it is a linear classifier, and the sigmoid changes the calibration of the output, not the geometry of the boundary.

A coefficient is a multiplier on the odds

w_j = 0.7 does not mean “probability increases by 0.7” or even “by 70%”. It means each one-unit increase in x_j adds 0.7 to the log-odds, i.e. multiplies the odds by e^0.7 ≈ 2.01 — the odds roughly double. What that does to the probability depends entirely on where you started:

Starting pStarting oddsOdds × 2.01New pChange
0.010.01010.0203≈ 0.020+1 point
0.501.002.01≈ 0.668+17 points

Same coefficient, wildly different probability effect. Being able to say this fluently is one of the strongest signals in the whole module.

The gradient is beautiful, and that is the point

With the logit link and log-loss, the gradient is Xᵀ(p - y) — feature matrix transposed times the prediction error. Identical in form to linear regression’s Xᵀ(ŷ - y). This is not a coincidence: it is a general property of using the canonical link of an exponential-family distribution. The messy σ'(z) factors cancel exactly.

Logistic regression outputs honest probabilities

Because it is trained by maximum likelihood on a proper scoring rule, its outputs are typically well-calibrated out of the box — if it says 0.3, about 30% of such cases really are positive. SVMs (hinge loss) and boosted trees are not calibrated by default. In any application where the score drives a decision with a monetary cost, this is a decisive advantage — and a great answer to “why would you ever pick logistic regression over XGBoost?”

4 · Why log odds — the complete answer

This is the section to over-prepare. There are five independent arguments; a 30-second answer uses argument 1, a strong answer adds 2 and 3, and a standout answer reaches 4 or 5.

Argument 1 — Range matching (the one everyone should have)

A linear predictor η = w·x + b is unbounded over (-∞, +∞). A probability is bounded to (0,1). You cannot set an unbounded thing equal to a bounded thing without either breaking the model or crushing the linear structure. So do not model p linearly — model a transformation of p whose range is all the reals. The logit does exactly that, and inverting it recovers a probability:

logit_inverts_to_sigmoid.txt
   log( p/(1-p) ) = η
      → p/(1-p)   = e^η
      → p         = e^η / (1 + e^η)  =  1/(1 + e^(-η))  =  σ(η)

Note the direction of the reasoning: the sigmoid is a consequence of choosing the logit link, not the starting point. Candidates who answer “because we use a sigmoid” have the logic backwards, and interviewers listen for exactly that.

Argument 2 — MLE gives you the loss, and the algebra comes out clean

Model each observation as Bernoulli with parameter pᵢ = σ(w·xᵢ). The likelihood is ∏ᵢ pᵢ^{yᵢ}(1-pᵢ)^{1-yᵢ}; the negative log-likelihood is -Σᵢ [ yᵢ log pᵢ + (1-yᵢ) log(1-pᵢ) ] — that is log-loss. It was not invented, it was derived. Now take the gradient. Using σ'(z) = σ(z)(1-σ(z)), the σ' factors cancel exactly against the derivative of the log terms:

gradient_collapse.txt
∂NLL/∂w = Σᵢ (σ(w·xᵢ) - yᵢ)·xᵢ   =   Xᵀ(p - y)

Simple, stable, and structurally identical to linear regression. This cancellation is a specific consequence of pairing the Bernoulli likelihood with the logit link.

Argument 3 — Convexity, hence a unique global optimum

The negative log-likelihood is convex in w — its Hessian is Xᵀ diag(pᵢ(1-pᵢ)) X, positive semi-definite because every pᵢ(1-pᵢ) > 0. Convexity means no local minima, no dependence on initialization, and reliable convergence with Newton or quasi-Newton methods. If instead you kept the sigmoid but used squared error, the objective is not convex in w. Choosing the logit link with the matching likelihood is what buys you the well-behaved optimization problem.

Argument 4 — GLM theory: the logit is the canonical link for Bernoulli

In the generalized-linear-model framework you write a distribution in exponential-family form p(y|θ) = h(y)exp(θ·T(y) - A(θ)). The canonical link maps the mean parameter to the natural parameter θ. For Bernoulli with mean p, writing p^y(1-p)^{1-y} = exp( y·log(p/(1-p)) + log(1-p) ) shows the natural parameter is exactly log(p/(1-p)) — the log-odds.

So the logit is not a convenient hack; it is the structurally distinguished link that Bernoulli hands you. This is also why the gradient simplifies (argument 2) and why the loss is convex (argument 3) — both are general properties of canonical links. The same framework gives the identity link for Gaussian regression and the log link for Poisson regression. If you can deliver this, you are answering above the level of the question.

Argument 5 — It is what the data-generating process actually implies

Suppose the two classes are Gaussian with different means and a shared covariance: x|y=1 ~ N(μ₁, Σ), x|y=0 ~ N(μ₀, Σ). Apply Bayes and take the log of the posterior odds. The quadratic terms in x cancel because the covariances are equal, and what survives is linear in x. Naive Bayes with exponential-family class-conditionals gives the same result.

So under a broad and not-unreasonable family of generative assumptions, the true log-odds is a linear function of the features — meaning logistic regression fits exactly the right functional form, and does so discriminatively without having to estimate the μs and Σ, so it is robust to those assumptions being somewhat wrong.

Interpretability, as a bonus

Because effects are additive in log-odds and multiplicative in odds, e^{w_j} is an odds ratio — the exact quantity epidemiology, credit scoring and clinical research have used for decades. “Smoking multiplies the odds of the outcome by 2.4” is a sentence a regulator, a doctor or a credit officer can act on. That is a large part of why logistic regression is still deployed in regulated domains in 2026.

The 30-second version, for a screen

“A linear combination of features is unbounded, but a probability is stuck in [0,1] — so you cannot model probability linearly. The log-odds is unbounded, so we model that linearly, and inverting it gives the sigmoid. 6 · Hands-on drill

  1. Implement logistic regression from scratch in numpy: sigmoid, log-loss, the gradient Xᵀ(p-y), batch gradient descent. Match sklearn’s coefficients to three decimal places with regularization disabled (penalty=None).
  2. Add L2 and confirm that as λ grows, coefficients shrink toward zero but never reach it.
  3. Plot the loss surface for a one-feature problem under log-loss and under MSE; verify visually that the MSE version has a non-convex region. This makes your convexity answer concrete.
  4. Build a perfectly separable 2D dataset, fit with penalty=None, and watch the coefficients blow up and the solver warn. Then add a tiny L2 penalty and watch it stabilize.
  5. Fit on an imbalanced dataset and produce the full precision/recall-versus-threshold curve. Pick a threshold from an explicit cost matrix and show the expected cost at your threshold versus at 0.5.
  6. Compare calibration curves for logistic regression against an untuned XGBClassifier. The logistic curve will hug the diagonal — that plot is worth describing in interviews.
⚠️
Gotchas that fail candidates
  • “Why log odds?” answered with “because of the sigmoid”. Backwards — the sigmoid is the consequence of the logit link.
  • Interpreting w_j as a change in probability. It is a change in log-odds; e^{w_j} is the odds multiplier.
  • Calling logistic regression a non-linear model. Linear in log-odds; linear decision boundary.
  • Not knowing sklearn’s C is inverse regularization. Setting C=100 to “regularize more” actually regularizes less.
  • Giving only one reason MSE fails. Give both — non-convexity and vanishing gradients.
  • Not knowing what happens under perfect separation. The MLE does not exist; coefficients diverge.
  • Forgetting the default 0.5 threshold is a choice, not a property of the model.
  • Using predict() instead of predict_proba() on an imbalanced problem, then wondering why recall is zero.

7 · Interview questions

12 questions
01Why log odds? Why not model the probability directly with a linear function?log oddslogitcanonical linktrap＋
Strong answer

Because the ranges do not match. w·x + b is unbounded over (-∞,+∞) while a probability lives in (0,1), so a linear model of p produces predictions below 0 and above 1, implies a constant marginal effect where probabilities must saturate, and has errors whose variance p(1-p) depends on the mean — violating the Gaussian-noise assumption that justifies squared error in the first place.

Instead, transform: the log-odds log(p/(1-p)) maps (0,1) onto all of ℝ, so that is what we model linearly. Inverting gives p = σ(w·x+b) — the sigmoid falls out, it is not assumed.

Three further reasons the logit specifically: it is the canonical link for the Bernoulli distribution in GLM theory, which makes the negative log-likelihood convex and collapses the gradient to Xᵀ(p−y); it is symmetric, with p=0.5 at the origin and logit(1−p) = −logit(p); and it yields interpretable odds ratios, since e^{wⱼ} is the multiplicative effect of a unit change in xⱼ on the odds.

30-second version

A linear predictor is unbounded, a probability is not — so model the log-odds linearly and invert, which produces the sigmoid. It is also Bernoulli’s canonical link, giving convexity, a clean gradient, and odds ratios.

What’s being tested

Whether you can reason from constraints to model form. The direction of the argument — logit chosen first, sigmoid derived — is what interviewers grade. Mentioning the canonical link puts you in the top band.

Likely follow-up: “Are there other valid links?” — yes. The probit link (inverse normal CDF) also maps (0,1) to ℝ and gives very similar fits; it is standard in econometrics. Complementary log-log is used for asymmetric responses. The logit’s advantages are the odds-ratio interpretation and being canonical, not uniqueness.
02Derive the log-loss for logistic regression from first principles.derivationMLElog-loss＋
Strong answer

Assume each yᵢ ∈ {0,1} is Bernoulli with parameter pᵢ = σ(w·xᵢ). A single observation’s probability can be written compactly as pᵢ^{yᵢ}(1-pᵢ)^{1-yᵢ} — it evaluates to pᵢ when yᵢ=1 and 1-pᵢ when yᵢ=0.

Assuming independence, the likelihood is ∏ᵢ pᵢ^{yᵢ}(1-pᵢ)^{1-yᵢ}. Take logs to turn the product into a sum — monotone, so the same argmax, and numerically far more stable: Σᵢ [yᵢ log pᵢ + (1-yᵢ)log(1-pᵢ)]. Maximizing that is minimizing its negative, which is log-loss.

So log-loss is the negative log-likelihood of a Bernoulli model — the direct classification analogue of MSE being the negative log-likelihood of a Gaussian model.

30-second version

Bernoulli likelihood ∏ pᵢ^yᵢ(1−pᵢ)^(1−yᵢ), take the log, negate. Log-loss is Bernoulli’s negative log-likelihood, exactly as MSE is the Gaussian one.

What’s being tested

The MLE-to-loss pipeline from Module 01, applied. Landing the parallel to MSE shows you see the general pattern rather than two memorized facts.

Likely follow-up: “Now derive the gradient.” — differentiate, use σ'(z)=σ(z)(1-σ(z)), and the σ' terms cancel against the 1/p and 1/(1-p) from the log derivatives, leaving ∇J = Σᵢ(pᵢ - yᵢ)xᵢ = Xᵀ(p-y).
03Why not use MSE as the loss for logistic regression?lossconvexityvanishing gradienttrap＋
Strong answer

Two independent reasons, and a complete answer gives both.

(1) Non-convexity. Composing the sigmoid with squared error produces an objective that is not convex in w, so gradient descent can converge to a local minimum and the result depends on initialization. Log-loss with the logit link is convex — Hessian XᵀSX with S = diag(pᵢ(1-pᵢ)) ⪰ 0 — so there is a unique global optimum.

(2) Vanishing gradients. With MSE the gradient carries a factor of σ'(z) = σ(z)(1-σ(z)), which goes to zero when z is large in magnitude. So a confidently wrong prediction — p ≈ 0.999 when y = 0 — produces an almost-zero gradient and the model barely learns from its worst mistakes. Log-loss cancels that σ' factor, so the gradient is proportional to the raw error (p − y) and confident mistakes generate the largest updates.

A third point if you want to go further: log-loss is a proper scoring rule, so it is minimized by the true conditional probability — making it the right objective if you care about calibrated probabilities and not just the argmax.

30-second version

Two reasons: sigmoid-plus-MSE is non-convex, so you can hit local minima; and its gradient carries a σ' factor that vanishes exactly when the model is confidently wrong. Log-loss is convex and cancels σ'.

What’s being tested

Whether you know both reasons. Most candidates give the vanishing-gradient answer only. Both, plus the proper-scoring-rule note, is a top-band answer.

04Why is it called logistic regression if it does classification?terminologyGLMtrap＋
Strong answer

Because it is a regression — it just regresses the log-odds rather than the target. The model fits a continuous, unbounded quantity logit(p) = w·x + b by regression; the classification step is a separate act of thresholding the resulting probability.

Historically it belongs to the generalized-linear-model family alongside linear and Poisson regression, differing only in the link function and assumed response distribution, and the name reflects that lineage plus the logistic function used to invert the link.

The practical payoff of remembering this: the native output is a probability, and turning it into a class label requires a threshold you choose from business cost — the model itself never commits to 0.5.

30-second version

It regresses the log-odds, a continuous unbounded quantity; classification is a separate thresholding step. It sits in the GLM family beside linear and Poisson regression.

What’s being tested

Conceptual clarity on a question designed to catch rote learners. The “regresses the log-odds, then you threshold” framing is the answer.

05A coefficient in your fitted model is 0.7. Interpret it for a business stakeholder.odds ratiointerpretationcommunication＋
Strong answer

A one-unit increase in that feature, holding the other included features constant, adds 0.7 to the log-odds of the positive outcome — which means it multiplies the odds by e^0.7 ≈ 2.0, so the odds roughly double.

Critically, that is not “the probability doubles”. What happens to the probability depends on the starting point: from a 1% baseline (odds ≈ 0.0101) doubling the odds gives about 2.0% — a one-point rise. From a 50% baseline (odds = 1) it gives about 67% — a 17-point rise.

So for stakeholders I would say “this factor roughly doubles the odds”, then, if they need a probability figure, quote the effect at their actual base rate. I would also caveat that this is association, not causation, and that “holding others constant” is only over the variables in the model.

30-second version

It adds 0.7 to the log-odds, so it multiplies the odds by about 2. Not the probability: from 1% that is a one-point rise, from 50% it is seventeen points.

What’s being tested

Whether you truly internalized the log-odds scale, plus communication skill. The two worked base-rate examples are what make this answer land.

Likely follow-up: “What if features are on wildly different scales?” — raw coefficients are not comparable. Standardize first and compare standardized coefficients, or report the odds ratio per one standard deviation, which is the version stakeholders can rank.
06Is logistic regression a linear or non-linear model?linearitydecision boundary＋
Strong answer

Linear — in two precise senses: it is linear in the parameters, and its decision boundary is the hyperplane w·x + b = 0. The sigmoid is a monotone transformation applied after the linear combination; it changes how the score maps to a probability, not the shape of the boundary.

The probability-versus-feature relationship is an S-curve, which is where the confusion comes from, but the classifier can only separate classes with a hyperplane. Consequence: logistic regression cannot solve XOR, and to capture interactions or curvature you must supply them explicitly as engineered features — at which point it is still linear in parameters but non-linear in the original inputs.

30-second version

Linear: linear in the parameters, with a hyperplane decision boundary. The sigmoid changes the output mapping, not the boundary geometry — so it cannot solve XOR unaided.

What’s being tested

Precision. Also sets up the SVM and kernel discussion in Module 14.

07Your coefficients are enormous — values like 400 — and the solver warns it did not converge. What is happening?perfect separationdebuggingleakagetrap＋
Strong answer

Almost certainly perfect or near-perfect separation: some feature or linear combination separates the two classes cleanly. Then the likelihood keeps increasing as you scale w up — pushing predicted probabilities toward exactly 0 and 1 — so the MLE is at infinity and does not exist. The optimizer chases it until it hits the iteration limit.

Diagnosis: look for a feature that perfectly predicts the target, which is very often leakage, or a category appearing in only one class.

Fixes: add L2 regularization, which bounds the coefficient norm and guarantees a finite unique solution; use Firth’s penalized likelihood if you need principled inference; or remove and investigate the separating feature. Practically, the first thing I would do is treat it as a leakage alarm rather than an optimizer problem.

30-second version

Perfect separation — the likelihood is maximized only as ‖w‖ → ∞, so no MLE exists. Usually a leakage signal. L2 regularization gives a finite solution, but investigate the separating feature first.

What’s being tested

Whether you recognize a specific well-known failure mode and connect it back to leakage. A strong senior signal.

Decrease it. sklearn parameterizes with C = 1/λ, so C is the inverse of regularization strength — the objective is roughly C·Σloss + ½‖w‖². Small C (say 0.01) means strong regularization and heavily shrunk coefficients; large C (say 1000) means weak regularization, approaching unpenalized MLE.

Also worth noting: LogisticRegression applies L2 by default, so it is never plain unregularized MLE unless you set penalty=None — which matters when reproducing textbook coefficients or a statsmodels fit.

Decrease it. C = 1/λ, so small C means strong regularization. And note sklearn applies L2 by default unless you set penalty=None.

09How does logistic regression extend to multi-class problems?softmaxmultinomialone-vs-rest＋
Strong answer

Two routes. Multinomial (softmax) logistic regression — the principled one — learns a weight vector per class and sets P(y=k|x) = e^{w_k·x}/Σⱼe^{w_j·x}, trained with categorical cross-entropy. Probabilities are jointly normalized to sum to 1 and the model is fitted in one optimization.

One-vs-Rest trains K independent binary classifiers, each “class k versus everything else”, and picks the largest score. Simpler and parallelizable, but the K outputs are not calibrated against each other and must be normalized post hoc, and each sub-problem is artificially imbalanced.

Softmax also has a redundancy worth knowing: adding a constant vector to every w_k leaves the probabilities unchanged, so the parameterization is over-specified by one degree of freedom — which is why some implementations fix one class’s weights at zero, and why regularization helps identifiability.

30-second version

Softmax over K weight vectors with categorical cross-entropy, jointly normalized — or One-vs-Rest with K binary models, which is simpler but not mutually calibrated.

What’s being tested

Whether you know both approaches and their calibration difference. The softmax shift-invariance detail is a nice depth signal.

10When would you choose logistic regression over XGBoost in 2026?appliedmodel selectioncalibration＋
Strong answer

Several genuine cases, and the answer should be affirmative rather than apologetic. (1) Regulatory or explainability requirements — credit decisioning, insurance underwriting, clinical risk — where you must state a monotone, auditable per-feature effect; odds ratios are the accepted currency in those fields. (2) Well-calibrated probabilities out of the box, needed when the score feeds an expected-cost calculation or a price rather than a classification. (3) Small or low-signal datasets, where a high-variance model overfits and the strong linear prior wins. (4) Extreme latency or footprint constraints — a dot product is microseconds and the model is a vector of numbers. (5) A strong baseline you should always fit first, to know whether the boosted model’s extra complexity buys anything. (6) Stability and easy monitoring — coefficient drift is directly interpretable.

Also note that logistic regression on well-engineered features closes much of the gap to boosting, and that the combination — boosting for accuracy, logistic regression for the explainable production model — is a common real-world pattern.

30-second version

Regulated or explainability-critical settings, when you need calibrated probabilities, on small or low-signal data, under tight latency budgets, and always as the baseline that justifies anything more complex.

What’s being tested

Whether you can defend the simple model on its merits. This question exists to catch candidates who reflexively reach for the most complex tool.

11What does it mean for logistic regression to be “well-calibrated”, and why is it?calibrationproper scoring rule＋
Strong answer

Calibrated means the predicted probabilities are truthful in aggregate: among all cases scored around 0.3, roughly 30% are actually positive. It is a different property from discrimination — a model can rank perfectly (AUC 1.0) while being badly calibrated, and vice versa.

Logistic regression tends to be calibrated because it is fitted by maximizing the Bernoulli likelihood, and log-loss is a proper scoring rule — uniquely minimized by the true conditional probability, so the training objective pushes directly toward honest probabilities.

By contrast an SVM optimizes hinge loss, which only cares about the margin, so its decision-function output is not a probability at all; and boosted trees, though trained on log-loss, are typically pushed toward over-confident extremes by sequential fitting and need post-hoc correction — Platt scaling or isotonic regression, fitted on held-out data. Measure calibration with a reliability diagram, expected calibration error, or the Brier score.

30-second version

Predicted probabilities match observed frequencies. Logistic regression gets it free because log-loss is a proper scoring rule, minimized by the true conditional probability — unlike hinge loss or untuned boosting.

What’s being tested

A topic that has grown much more prominent in 2026 loops because scores increasingly drive automated cost-based decisions. Naming “proper scoring rule” and separating calibration from discrimination is the strong answer.

12Show that the log-odds is linear in x if both classes are Gaussian with shared covariance.derivationLDAgenerative＋
Strong answer

Let x|y=1 ~ N(μ₁,Σ) and x|y=0 ~ N(μ₀,Σ) with priors π₁, π₀. The log posterior odds is log(P(x|y=1)/P(x|y=0)) + log(π₁/π₀).

Substituting the Gaussian densities, the normalizing constants cancel, leaving -½(x-μ₁)ᵀΣ⁻¹(x-μ₁) + ½(x-μ₀)ᵀΣ⁻¹(x-μ₀) + log(π₁/π₀). Expanding both quadratic forms, the xᵀΣ⁻¹x terms are identical and cancel — this is exactly where the shared-covariance assumption earns its keep — leaving terms linear in x plus constants:

shared_covariance_log_odds.txt
(μ₁-μ₀)ᵀΣ⁻¹x - ½(μ₁ᵀΣ⁻¹μ₁ - μ₀ᵀΣ⁻¹μ₀) + log(π₁/π₀)

That is w·x + b with w = Σ⁻¹(μ₁-μ₀). So the true log-odds is linear, which is precisely the form logistic regression assumes — this is the generative justification (Linear Discriminant Analysis) for the logistic model.

30-second version

Write the log posterior odds of two shared-covariance Gaussians; the quadratic xᵀΣ⁻¹x terms cancel, leaving w·x + b with w = Σ⁻¹(μ₁−μ₀). So a linear log-odds is what that generative model implies.

What’s being tested

Serious mathematical depth — an Applied Scientist or research-track question, and the most satisfying answer to “why should the log-odds be linear?”

Likely follow-up: “What if the covariances differ?” — the quadratic terms no longer cancel and the boundary becomes quadratic: that is QDA, more flexible but needing a separate covariance matrix per class and far more data. Note too that logistic regression estimates w discriminatively without assuming Gaussianity, so it is more robust when the assumption fails, while LDA is more efficient when it holds.
2026 additions. Logistic regression is now probed for calibration and for its role as the reference model — including its identity as a one-layer neural network, which is the standard bridge into the GenAI portion of a loop.
13Your model says 0.7. Does 70% of that group actually convert? How would you know, and what if not?2026calibrationproduction＋
Strong answer

That property is calibration, and it is separate from discrimination. A model can rank perfectly — AUC of 0.95 — and still have probabilities that are systematically wrong, which matters the moment anyone multiplies your probability by a rupee amount.

How I check. A reliability diagram: bin the predictions, and for each bin plot mean predicted probability against the observed fraction of positives. Perfect calibration is the diagonal; sagging below it means over-confidence. I would quantify it with expected calibration error and, better, with the Brier score — which decomposes into a calibration term and a refinement term, so it tells you which half is broken. And I would look at reliability per segment, because good overall calibration routinely hides a badly calibrated subgroup.

Why it might be broken, which is the more interesting half. Unregularised logistic regression fitted by maximum likelihood is naturally well calibrated on its training distribution — that is a real advantage over SVMs and boosted trees, and worth stating. So if it is not calibrated, the cause is usually something I did to it: resampling or SMOTE, which shifts the base rate and guarantees inflated probabilities; class weights, which do the same thing; heavy regularisation, which shrinks the log-odds toward zero and makes the model systematically under-confident; or prevalence drift since training, which moves the intercept out from under you.

Fixes. If resampling caused it, apply the prior-correction adjustment to the intercept — it is a closed-form shift, not a retrain. Otherwise fit a calibrator on a held-out set: Platt scaling (a one-dimensional logistic regression on the scores) when data is limited and the distortion is roughly sigmoidal, or isotonic regression when there is more data and the distortion is an arbitrary monotone shape — with the caveat that isotonic overfits on small sets and produces a step function. Either way the calibrator must be fitted on data the model did not train on, and it becomes a versioned artefact that needs re-checking on the same cadence as the model.

30-second version

Check with a reliability diagram plus Brier score, sliced by segment. Unregularised logistic regression is naturally calibrated, so if it is not, suspect resampling, class weights, heavy regularisation or prevalence drift. Fix resampling with the closed-form intercept correction; otherwise Platt scaling on a held-out set, or isotonic if I have the data for it.

What’s being tested

Whether you separate ranking from probability. The 2026 tell of a strong candidate is knowing that logistic regression is calibrated by default and that the usual culprit is your own resampling — most candidates jump straight to Platt scaling without asking what broke it.

Likely follow-up: “Do you always need calibrated probabilities?” — no, and saying so shows judgment. If the output is only used to rank and take the top k, any monotone transformation is irrelevant and AUC or precision@k is the right metric. Calibration becomes mandatory the moment the probability enters an arithmetic decision — expected-value thresholds, expected loss, pricing, or combining with another model's output.
14Connect logistic regression to a neural network, and then to how an LLM is trained.2026bridgeGenAI＋
Strong answer

Logistic regression is a neural network — specifically a single neuron with a sigmoid activation, trained by gradient descent on cross-entropy. There is no analogy involved; it is the same object. w·x is the neuron's pre-activation, the sigmoid is its activation function, log-loss is the network's loss, and Xᵀ(p−y) is one step of backpropagation on a network with no hidden layer.

Three specific correspondences make the bridge concrete. Depth is automatic feature engineering. Logistic regression needs me to hand it the interactions and non-linearities; hidden layers learn them, which is the entire difference. Softmax is multi-class logistic regression, and it is exactly the output layer of a classifier network. An LLM's final layer is a softmax over the vocabulary — so next-token prediction is a classification problem with roughly 100,000 classes, trained on cross-entropy, which is the same loss on this page.

Then the sentence that lands the point about pretraining: LLM pretraining is maximum likelihood estimation. Minimising cross-entropy over next tokens is maximising the log-likelihood of the training corpus under the model — the same loss factory from Module 01, applied to a categorical distribution over tokens. Perplexity is just the exponential of that average cross-entropy, so it is the same number in different units. And the connection continues into the failure modes: benchmark contamination is data leakage, temperature is a sharpness dial on the softmax — exactly the confidence scaling you get by multiplying the logits, which is also what Platt scaling adjusts — and an over-confident LLM is a calibration problem, not a mysterious new phenomenon.

30-second version

It is literally a one-neuron network: w·x is the pre-activation, sigmoid is the activation, log-loss is the loss, and Xᵀ(p−y) is backprop with no hidden layer. Hidden layers just learn the interactions I would otherwise engineer. An LLM's output layer is a softmax over the vocabulary, so next-token prediction is 100,000-class classification on cross-entropy — which means pretraining is maximum likelihood, and perplexity is exp of that same loss.

What’s being tested

Whether classical ML and modern ML are one body of knowledge for you. This is the highest-leverage bridge question in a 2026 loop, and reaching the “pretraining is MLE” and “temperature is softmax sharpness” connections signals real understanding rather than vocabulary.

Likely follow-up: “If a neural network is a superset, why ever use logistic regression?” — because the superset costs you the things the simple model gives free: convexity and therefore a unique reproducible solution, coefficients an auditor can read, microsecond inference, calibrated probabilities without extra work, and stable behaviour on small data. On wide tabular data with hand-engineered features, logistic regression is frequently within a point of a network, at a fraction of the operational cost.
15Your fit returns coefficients in the hundreds and a convergence warning. Diagnose it.separationdebuggingregularization＋
Strong answer

The signature points to perfect or near-perfect separation — some feature, or a linear combination of features, splits the classes exactly. When that happens the likelihood has no finite maximum: the model can always increase the likelihood a little further by scaling that weight up, pushing predicted probabilities toward exactly 0 and 1, so the optimiser runs to its iteration limit chasing an infinite optimum. The coefficient magnitude is the symptom; the non-existence of the MLE is the cause.

But the important move is not to fix it — it is to ask why a feature separates the classes perfectly, because in real data that is almost always a bug rather than a discovery. My checks, in order: is it target leakage — a feature that is a consequence of the label, like account_closed_date for churn or chargeback_flag for fraud? Is it a one-hot column for a rare category that happens to contain only positives, which is separation by small-sample accident rather than by signal? Is the label derived from that very feature by some business rule upstream? Only after ruling those out would I accept that the problem is genuinely easy.

The fixes, once the cause is understood. Any penalty removes the pathology, because it makes the objective's minimum finite — L2 is the standard choice and is why scikit-learn regularises by default, which is also why many people never see this error. Firth's penalised likelihood is the principled small-sample option and reduces the bias as well. Dropping or merging the offending rare category fixes the accidental version. And if it is leakage, no amount of regularisation helps — the feature must go.

30-second version

Perfect separation — the likelihood has no finite maximum, so the weight runs to infinity and the optimiser hits its iteration cap. Regularisation makes it finite and is the mechanical fix. But first ask why a feature separates perfectly: usually target leakage, or a rare one-hot category that happens to be all positives. If it is leakage, regularisation just hides it.

What’s being tested

Whether you read an error message as a signal about the data rather than as a nuisance to suppress. Candidates who answer only “add regularisation” miss the point; the leakage question is the one being listened for.

Likely follow-up: “The model has 0.99 AUC and separation. Ship it?” — no. Those two facts together are the classic leakage signature, and shipping produces a model that collapses on day one because the leaked feature is not available at prediction time. Drop the suspect feature, refit, and see what honest performance looks like.
16A coefficient is 0.8. A stakeholder asks “so how much does this raise the chance?” Answer them.2026interpretationcommunication＋
Strong answer

The honest answer is “it depends where you start”, and the skill is saying that without sounding evasive.

Mechanically: e^0.8 ≈ 2.2, so a one-unit increase multiplies the odds by about 2.2. That is exact and constant. But the stakeholder is asking about probability, and the effect on probability is not constant, because the sigmoid saturates. I would answer with two concrete cases rather than a formula:

  • A customer starting at 10% — odds 0.111 — moves to odds 0.244, which is 19.6%. Roughly a ten-percentage-point gain.
  • A customer starting at 80% — odds 4 — moves to odds 8.8, which is 89.8%. Under ten percentage points, and the ceiling is doing most of the limiting.

So the same coefficient is worth a lot in the middle of the range and very little at the extremes. If they need a single number for a business case, the right one is the average marginal effect: compute the probability change for every customer in the actual population and average it — that answers “if we moved this feature by one unit across our real customer base, what happens to the conversion rate”, which is the question they actually mean. And I would restate the caveat plainly: this is an association within the observed data, so acting on it needs an experiment.

30-second version

e^0.8 ≈ 2.2, so it multiplies the odds by 2.2 — that part is constant. The effect on probability is not: from 10% it goes to about 20%, from 80% to about 90%. If they need one number, I would compute the average marginal effect across the real customer base, which is the question they are actually asking.

What’s being tested

Whether you understand that constant on the log-odds scale means non-constant on the probability scale — and whether you can translate that into a business answer instead of correcting the questioner. Offering the average marginal effect is the senior move.

Likely follow-up: “Which customers should we target then?” — the ones in the steep middle of the sigmoid, where a one-unit push moves the probability most. Customers already near 0.9 will convert anyway and customers near 0.02 will not be rescued by one unit of anything. That is uplift reasoning, and the rigorous version is to model the treatment effect directly rather than the outcome — which is a genuinely different model and worth naming.
