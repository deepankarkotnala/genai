Regression Metrics

The deepest idea here makes several interview questions trivial: your loss choice decides which statistic you predict — the mean, the median, or a quantile.

Prerequisites · Modules 03, 06Study time · 4 h4 interview questionsTHEORY · APPLIED
🎯
Your interview goal

Explain MAE versus RMSE in terms of the statistic each targets, read the RMSE/MAE gap as a diagnostic, and turn an asymmetric cost into a quantile objective.

1 · What to learn

  1. MSE, RMSE, MAE — definitions, units, and outlier sensitivity.
  2. Why RMSE ≥ MAE always, and what the gap tells you about your error distribution.
  3. MAPE and its failure modes — undefined at zero, asymmetric penalty — plus SMAPE, WAPE, MASE.
  4. R² and adjusted R²; out-of-sample R² and why it can be negative.
  5. Huber loss and quantile loss.
  6. Which statistic each loss actually predicts: MSE → the conditional mean, MAE → the conditional median, quantile loss → a conditional quantile. The highest-value idea in the module.
  7. RMSLE for multiplicative or skewed targets.
  8. Aligning the metric with the business cost of over- versus under-prediction.
  9. Residual analysis as evaluation, not just as an assumption check.
  10. Time-series evaluation: naive and seasonal-naive baselines, backtesting, forecast horizons.

2 · Core intuitions

Your loss choice decides which statistic you predict

Minimizing squared error over a set of numbers gives the mean; minimizing absolute error gives the median; minimizing asymmetric quantile (pinball) loss gives that quantile. So a model trained with MSE predicts the conditional mean of y|x, and one trained with MAE predicts the conditional median. On a right-skewed target — house prices, insurance claims, revenue — those are very different numbers, and choosing between them is a business decision, not a technical preference.

RMSE punishes large errors; MAE treats all errors linearly

Squaring means one error of 10 contributes as much as a hundred errors of 1. Choose RMSE when a few large misses are genuinely much worse than many small ones — a badly wrong ETA, a large capacity shortfall. Choose MAE when errors scale linearly with cost and you do not want a handful of anomalies to steer the model. Huber gives you both: quadratic near zero for a smooth gradient, linear in the tails for robustness, with the crossover set by δ.

The RMSE/MAE gap is a free diagnostic

RMSE ≥ MAE always, by Jensen’s inequality, with equality only when all errors are identical in magnitude. A large ratio means your error distribution is heavy-tailed — a few big misses. So reporting both, and commenting on the gap, tells the interviewer something a single number cannot: RMSE 40 with MAE 12 is a very different model from RMSE 14 with MAE 12, and only the first has an outlier problem worth investigating.

MAPE is asymmetric and often the wrong choice

|y−ŷ|/|y| is undefined when y = 0 and explodes as y → 0. Worse, it penalizes over-prediction more than under-prediction: predicting 150 when the truth is 100 gives 50% error, predicting 50 gives the same 50%, while predicting 0 caps out at 100%. So a MAPE-optimizing model is systematically biased low. It is popular because “percent error” communicates well — so if you must report it, pair it with WAPE (total absolute error over total actual, robust to near-zero rows) or MASE (scaled against a naive forecast).

Always report against a baseline

An RMSE of 4,200 is meaningless alone. RMSE of 4,200 versus 11,000 for “predict the mean” and 5,100 for “predict last month’s value” is a result. For time series the seasonal-naive baseline is the standard bar, and beating it is genuinely harder than people expect.

3 · Whiteboard formulas

regression_metrics.txt
MSE    = (1/n) S (y - yhat)²                    units: target²
RMSE   = sqrt(MSE)                              units: target      <- report this one
MAE    = (1/n) S |y - yhat|                     units: target
MAPE   = (100/n) S |y - yhat|/|y|               undefined at y=0
WAPE   = S|y - yhat| / S|y|                     robust alternative
RMSLE  = sqrt( (1/n) S (log(1+y) - log(1+yhat))² )   penalizes under-prediction more

R²     = 1 - SSres/SStot                        can be < 0 out-of-sample

Huber (delta):  0.5·e²              for |e| <= delta
                delta|e| - 0.5·delta²   for |e| >  delta

Quantile (pinball) loss for quantile tau:
                max( tau·e , (tau-1)·e )        where e = y - yhat
                tau=0.5 -> MAE/2 -> predicts the MEDIAN

4 · Hands-on drill

  1. On a right-skewed target, train two models — one with MSE, one with MAE — and compare their predictions against the conditional mean and median. Confirm the theory holds.
  2. Add 1% extreme outliers and re-measure both. Quantify how much more the MSE model moves.
  3. Fit a quantile-regression model (or LGBMRegressor(objective='quantile', alpha=0.9)) and produce a 10th/50th/90th-percentile prediction interval. Being able to say “I shipped a P90 rather than a mean, because under-supply cost 5× over-supply” is a strong applied answer.
  4. Compute RMSE, MAE and their ratio on a real dataset, and interpret the ratio.
⚠️
Gotchas that fail candidates
  • Reporting MSE rather than RMSE to a stakeholder. Squared units are uninterpretable.
  • Using MAPE on data containing zeros or near-zeros.
  • Not knowing that MSE targets the mean and MAE the median. This is the differentiating fact.
  • Reporting a metric with no baseline.
  • Assuming R² near 1 means a good model — it can be leakage, or a trivially predictable target.
  • Ignoring the sign of the error when over- and under-prediction have different costs.

5 · Interview questions

4 questions
01RMSE or MAE? How do you choose?RMSEMAEmean vs mediantrap＋
Strong answer

It depends on how error cost scales and how you want outliers treated. RMSE squares errors, so large misses dominate — use it when one big error is much worse than several small ones, such as a severely wrong delivery ETA or a large inventory shortfall. MAE weights all errors linearly — use it when cost is proportional to error size and you do not want a handful of anomalies steering the model.

The deeper distinction: a model trained on squared error predicts the conditional mean of y|x, while one trained on absolute error predicts the conditional median. On a skewed target those differ substantially, so the choice is really “does the business want the average or the typical case?”

Practically I would report both, since RMSE ≥ MAE always and the size of the gap reveals how heavy-tailed the errors are. For robustness without giving up a smooth gradient, Huber.

30-second version

RMSE if a few large misses are much worse; MAE if cost is linear in error. Deeper: MSE targets the conditional mean, MAE the conditional median — so on skewed targets it is a business question.

What’s being tested

Whether you can go beyond “RMSE punishes outliers more”. The mean-versus-median point marks a genuinely strong answer.

Likely follow-up: “So which is easier to optimize?” — MSE is differentiable everywhere and has a closed form for linear models; MAE has a discontinuous derivative at zero, needing subgradient or specialized methods. That convenience is a real reason MSE is the default, independent of whether it is the right cost.
02Your stakeholder wants MAPE. What do you say?MAPEcommunicationtrap＋
Strong answer

I would accept it as a reporting metric while flagging three problems and proposing a companion.

First, MAPE is undefined when the actual is zero and explodes as it approaches zero, so with any zeros or small values the metric is unstable or unusable. Second, it is asymmetric: over-prediction can incur an unbounded percentage error while under-prediction is capped at 100%, so a MAPE-optimized model is systematically biased toward under-predicting — exactly the wrong bias if under-supply is the expensive direction. Third, it weights low-value rows far more heavily than high-value ones, which usually inverts the business priority.

So I would report MAPE for communication but add WAPE — total absolute error over total actual — which is robust to small denominators and aggregates the way the business experiences error, and use RMSE or a quantile loss for model selection. For time series I would add MASE, which scales against a naive forecast and is comparable across series.

30-second version

Accept it for reporting, but flag that it is undefined at zero, asymmetric so it biases predictions low, and over-weights small values. Pair it with WAPE, and select models on RMSE or a quantile loss.

What’s being tested

Whether you can push back on a metric request constructively rather than capitulating or refusing. The asymmetry-induced bias is the key technical point.

03Under-predicting demand costs you 5× what over-predicting does. How do you build for that?quantile regressionasymmetric costapplied＋
Strong answer

Encode the asymmetry in the objective rather than fixing it afterwards. The cleanest approach is quantile regression with a pinball loss at τ = 5/6 ≈ 0.83, which directly targets the quantile where the marginal costs of over- and under-prediction balance — most gradient-boosting libraries support this via a quantile objective, so it is a one-line change.

Alternatively use a custom asymmetric loss multiplying the under-prediction branch by 5, which XGBoost and LightGBM support through a custom gradient and Hessian.

A weaker but common fallback is to train a mean model and apply a learned safety-stock multiplier, but that is a post-hoc patch that does not adapt per row — a quantile model gives a conditional buffer, larger where uncertainty is genuinely larger.

Evaluate with the same asymmetric cost you optimized, plus realized total cost on a backtest — never plain RMSE, because a model better on RMSE can easily be worse on your actual cost.

30-second version

Quantile regression with pinball loss at τ = 5/6, so the model targets the quantile where marginal costs balance — and evaluate on the asymmetric cost, not RMSE.

What’s being tested

Whether you can align the loss function with an asymmetric business cost. Naming quantile regression and computing τ = 5/6 is exactly the answer senior interviewers want.

04Your regression has R² = 0.95. Is the model good?R-squaredscepticismtrap＋
Strong answer

Probably suspicious rather than good, and I would check four things before celebrating. (1) Is it out-of-sample? In-sample R² rises mechanically with feature count. (2) Leakage — a very high R² on a genuinely hard target is the classic leakage signature; I would look for a feature computed from or after the outcome. (3) Is the target trivially predictable? If y is nearly a linear function of an obvious input — predicting total price from quantity × unit price — then 0.95 is unimpressive; compare against a naive baseline first. (4) Autocorrelation — on a trending time series, R² against the mean is flattering because the mean is a terrible baseline; compare against a seasonal-naive forecast instead.

Separately, R² says nothing about whether errors are usefully small: I would want RMSE and MAE in the target’s own units plus a residual plot, because R² can be high while errors remain operationally unacceptable.

30-second version

Suspicious rather than good. Check it is out-of-sample, rule out leakage, ask whether the target is trivially predictable, and compare against a proper baseline — then look at RMSE in real units.

What’s being tested

Scepticism toward good news. Interviewers deliberately offer a flattering number to see whether you interrogate it.
