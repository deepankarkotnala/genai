ML Interview Landscape & Study Setup

This module is not itself interviewed. Its job is to make the other 24 efficient — by telling you what is actually being scored.

Your interview goal

Know which rounds you will face, how your target role weights these modules, how to pace a spoken answer, and how to present a project that survives cross-examination.

1 · From the ground up

🧩
In plain words

An ML interview is not a quiz about definitions. It is five short conversations, each testing a different thing, and in every one the interviewer is really asking the same underlying question: if I hand you an ambiguous problem and a messy dataset, will you make good decisions and notice when you are wrong? Everything in this module is about making that judgment visible in the small amount of time you get.

Why the format looks the way it does

A company cannot observe six months of your work in 45 minutes, so it looks for proxies. The proxies it has settled on are: can you reason about a model rather than recall it, can you write code that runs, can you scope an ambiguous system, and can you work with people. That is the whole reason there are separate rounds — each one is a different proxy, and each is scored by different people against a different rubric.

So what: a great answer in the wrong register still scores poorly. A design round does not want a derivation; a theory round does not want a Kubernetes diagram.

What actually changed by 2026

Code generation became free. Writing a working train_test_split from memory used to be a mild signal of competence; now anyone's editor does it. So the questions moved to the part that models are still bad at unsupervised: choosing. Which loss, which metric, which split, which threshold, which trade-off — and what breaks after deployment. Two more shifts follow from the same cause: nearly every loop now has a GenAI or LLM component even for classical-ML roles, and production debugging has become its own round because more candidates than ever can build a model and fewer can keep one alive.

So what: practise saying why out loud. The bank on this site is deliberately almost all “why” questions, because that is what is left to test.

The unit of scoring is a sentence, not an essay

Interviewers take notes. What lands in those notes is one compressed sentence per question — “knew calibration matters for the cost decision”, or “listed assumptions but could not say which mattered”. Long answers do not produce better notes; they produce fewer of them, because you spent the time. So the shape that works is a ladder: a crisp claim first, then mechanism, then the trade-off, and let the follow-ups pull you deeper rather than pushing everything out at once.

The five rounds, and what each one is really measuring

The loop is not five chances to prove the same thing — it is five different rubrics. Diagnose which round you are in from the first question, and answer in that register.

Build the answer as a ladder, not a wall

Three rungs, and you only climb when invited. Rung one, ~30 seconds: the claim and the reason. Rung two, ~2 minutes: the mechanism, and the case where your answer would change. Rung three, the whiteboard: derivation, numbers, edge cases. Starting at rung three is the most common self-inflicted wound in an ML interview: it burns your clock, it hides your judgment behind algebra, and it makes it impossible for the interviewer to tell whether you can prioritise.

So what: for every question in this curriculum, rehearse the 30-second rung first. Every answer on this site includes one for exactly that reason.

Your projects will be cross-examined, so pre-build the answers

Any project you list invites four questions: what was the baseline, what metric moved and by how much, what did you try that failed, and what broke in production. If you cannot answer those, the project actively hurts you — it reads as something you followed rather than something you decided. One project you can defend to that depth beats five you can only describe.

Analogy — a driving test does not ask you to recite the highway code. It puts you on a road and watches whether you check the mirror before changing lane. The ML interview works the same way: the questions are the road, and “which metric, and why” is the mirror check. Interviewers are watching for the habits, not the recall.

3 · What changed in ML interviews by 2026

Internalise this before you start, because it changes how you should answer.

  1. Fundamentals got harder, not easier. Because LLMs produce working sklearn code instantly, interviewers stopped rewarding syntax recall and moved to reasoning: why this model, why this loss, why this metric, why this split, what breaks in production. Nearly every question in this curriculum is a “why” question.
  2. Tabular problems are still classical ML problems. Gradient-boosted trees remain the strong default. Expect to justify not reaching for a neural network.
  3. “Why not just use an LLM?” is now standard. You need a crisp, non-defensive answer covering cost, latency, determinism, calibration and auditability.
  4. Evaluation and data quality outweigh algorithm trivia. A candidate who spots leakage beats one who recites the XGBoost gain formula.
  5. Deployment literacy is assumed at every level — training/serving skew, monitoring, retraining cadence, rollback.
  6. Interpretability and fairness became compliance, not nice-to-haves, in regulated domains.

5 · The answer ladder

Answer every conceptual question in three escalating layers. Give layer 1, then stop and read the interviewer. Answering layer 3 to a layer 1 question reads as poor communication and eats your time.

1

30 seconds

The direct answer: one sentence of mechanism, one sentence of consequence.

2

2 minutes

The mechanism properly — formula, trade-off, and when it breaks.

3

Whiteboard

The derivation, or a worked numeric example.

🧭
Worked example — “What is regularization?”

L1: “It’s a penalty on model complexity added to the loss, so the optimizer trades a little training fit for better generalization.”

L2: “Concretely, minimize loss + λ·penalty. L2 shrinks all coefficients smoothly and handles correlated features; L1 drives some to exactly zero, giving feature selection. λ moves you along the bias–variance curve.”

L3: Write the ridge closed form, show (XᵀX + λI) is always invertible, and sketch the constraint-region diagram explaining why L1’s corners produce sparsity.

6 · Hands-on drill

Write a one-page brief for each of your two portfolio projects containing exactly these fields. If you cannot fill every field, the project is not interview-ready — it is a liability, not an asset.

  1. The business objective, in one sentence.
  2. The target definition — and the label’s delay.
  3. The baseline you measured against.
  4. The chosen metric, and why that one.
  5. The split strategy.
  6. The final number versus baseline.
  7. The biggest weakness.
  8. What you would do with two more weeks.
⚠️
Gotchas that fail candidates
  • Answering layer 3 to a layer 1 question.
  • Not asking what the business cares about in an applied round.
  • A portfolio project you cannot defend. If you cannot say why you chose that metric and what the baseline was, do not bring it.
  • No baseline. Every project has one: the current process, the majority class, or last month’s average.

7 · Interview questions

3 questions
01Walk me through an ML project you owned end to end.appliedownershipcommunication＋
Strong answer

Use a fixed skeleton so you never ramble: problem and business metric → data and its problems → target definition → baseline → why this model class → evaluation design → result versus baseline → what shipped → what you’d change. Keep it to three minutes and land on a quantified delta against a stated baseline. Volunteer one genuine weakness before being asked — it converts a cross-examination into a discussion.

30-second version

Problem, data, target, baseline, model choice, evaluation, result versus baseline, what shipped. Three minutes, ending on a number.

What’s being tested

Ownership, structured communication, and whether you measured against a baseline at all. Most candidates fail here by describing the model and never the problem.

Likely follow-up: “What was your baseline?” — if the answer is “we didn’t have one”, the round is already lost.
02This is a tabular churn prediction problem. Why not just use an LLM?appliedjudgmenttrap＋
Strong answer

Frame it as fitness for purpose, not scepticism. Tabular supervised learning with plentiful labels is exactly the regime where gradient-boosted trees dominate: they learn from your actual historical label distribution, produce scores you can threshold against a business cost, cost microseconds per prediction, are deterministic and auditable, and retrain in minutes.

An LLM has no access to your label signal beyond what fits in a prompt, costs orders of magnitude more per prediction, adds latency, and gives poorly calibrated confidence.

Then state where the LLM does earn its place: turning unstructured columns — support tickets, call transcripts, free-text notes — into embeddings or extracted features that feed the tree model, and generating candidate feature hypotheses for a human to validate.

30-second version

Tabular plus plenty of labels is GBDT territory — cheaper, deterministic, calibrated, auditable. The LLM’s real job here is turning free-text columns into features for that tree.

What’s being tested

Judgment and resistance to hype. Interviewers use this to separate engineers who choose tools from engineers who follow trends.

Likely follow-up: “When would you flip to a neural approach?” — very high-cardinality categorical interactions, genuinely multimodal inputs, transfer learning from a large pretrained model, or a target with little tabular signal but rich text or image signal.
03How do you keep learning in a field moving this fast?behaviouralprocess＋
Strong answer

Name a concrete mechanism, not a sentiment: a fixed weekly slot for one paper plus one reimplementation from scratch; following three or four primary sources rather than aggregators; reproducing a competition solution each quarter. Then give a specific recent example of something you learned and — importantly — what you concluded about when it does and does not apply.

What’s being tested

Whether you have a process. Vague enthusiasm scores zero; the “when it does not apply” half is what signals judgment rather than novelty-chasing.

2026 additions. These reflect how loops changed in the 2025–2026 cycle: an LLM component now appears even in classical-ML roles, and interviewers ask directly how you work with AI tooling.
04Code generation is essentially free now. What do you think we are testing instead?2026metajudgment＋
Strong answer

Decisions and verification. Generating a training script is no longer evidence of anything, so what is left is the reasoning a model cannot do for me without my problem context: framing a fuzzy business ask as an ML problem with a defined target and unit of prediction; choosing the loss, the metric, the split and the threshold from the actual cost of each error; knowing what would falsify my own approach, which is really the discipline of designing the validation before the model; and debugging production, where the failure is usually in data or plumbing rather than in the model.

The second half of the answer is verification. If I use generated code, the value I add is catching the things it gets confidently wrong — a scaler fitted before the split, a random split on time-ordered data, resampling applied outside the fold, a metric that quietly ignores class imbalance. Those are exactly the bugs that pass tests, produce a beautiful offline number and fail in production.

30-second version

Framing, choosing and verifying. Generation handles the syntax; what is left is defining the target, picking the metric from the error costs, designing the validation before the model, and catching the leakage-shaped bugs that generated code produces confidently.

What’s being tested

Self-awareness about your own value-add. It also functions as an honesty check — candidates who claim not to use AI tooling read as either dishonest or incurious.

Likely follow-up: “Show me. Here is a generated pipeline — what is wrong with it?” Have the leakage checklist ready: fit order relative to the split, time-awareness of the split, whether resampling and feature selection sit inside the fold, target encoding computed out-of-fold, and any feature that would not exist at prediction time.
05How do you use AI assistants day to day, and where do you deliberately not?2026toolinghonesty＋
Strong answer

Heavily for the mechanical layer: boilerplate, plotting, refactors, unfamiliar library syntax, docstrings, converting a notebook into a module, and as a rubber duck for “what am I not considering here”. That is a genuine multiple on throughput and there is no reason to pretend otherwise.

Deliberately not for three things. Choosing the metric and the validation design — those depend on business cost and data-generating process that only I have; an assistant will confidently hand me a defensible-looking default and defaults are exactly where this goes wrong. Accepting anything about data splitting or leakage without reading it, because that is the highest-frequency silent bug. And the final judgment on whether a model is good enough to ship, which is a risk decision, not a code decision.

The frame I would state explicitly: I treat generated code the way I treat a competent colleague's pull request — useful by default, reviewed always, and trusted least in exactly the areas where it is most fluent.

30-second version

Heavily for boilerplate, plots, refactors and unfamiliar APIs. Not for metric choice, validation design, or the ship decision — those need business context it does not have. I review generated data-splitting code most carefully, because that is where confident, silent bugs live.

What’s being tested

Whether you are productive with modern tooling and whether you know its failure boundary. Both extremes score badly: refusing to use it, or trusting it to make modelling decisions.

Likely follow-up: “Tell me about a time it was wrong and you caught it.” Have one real example. The best ones are subtle and plausible — a nested-CV loop that tuned on the outer fold, or a target encoder fitted on the full training set — rather than “it hallucinated a function that does not exist”.
06This is a classical ML role. Why should you expect LLM questions anyway?2026GenAIscope＋
Strong answer

Because in 2026 almost no production ML system is purely classical any more, and the boundary work falls on whoever owns the model. Three concrete overlaps come up. LLMs as feature generators — embeddings or extracted attributes from free text feeding a gradient-boosted model, which is the hybrid architecture that actually wins on tabular-plus-text problems. LLMs as evaluators, scoring outputs where no ground truth label exists, which immediately raises the question of how you validate the judge. And routing and cost decisions — when a cheap classifier is enough and when a call to a model is justified.

The good news is that the fundamentals transfer exactly, and saying so is the point of the answer: benchmark contamination is data leakage, pretraining is maximum likelihood estimation, temperature is an exploration–exploitation dial, RAG-versus-fine-tuning is a bias–variance and data-freshness trade-off, and a hallucination rate is a precision problem with a threshold behind it. I would rather show the mapping than pretend to LLM-internals expertise I do not have.

30-second version

Because production systems are hybrid now — LLMs generate features, act as evaluators, and sit behind routing decisions. And the fundamentals map directly: contamination is leakage, pretraining is MLE, RAG versus fine-tuning is a bias–variance and freshness trade-off.

What’s being tested

Whether you can be dropped into a modern stack. Mapping classical concepts onto LLM problems is far stronger than reciting model names, and it is what Module 23 is built to give you.

Likely follow-up: “How would you evaluate a system with no single correct answer?” — define the quality dimensions first, then combine a small human-labelled gold set, an LLM judge validated for agreement against that gold set, and behavioural checks with deterministic answers. Report agreement rates, and treat the judge itself as a model that can drift.
