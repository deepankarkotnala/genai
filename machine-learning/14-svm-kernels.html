Support Vector Machines & Kernels

Rarely the production choice in 2026, but asked about constantly — because the margin idea and the kernel trick test whether you can think geometrically.

Prerequisites · Modules 01, 07, 09Study time · 8 h6 interview questionsTHEORY · MATH
🎯
Your interview goal

State the margin as a generalization argument, explain the kernel trick without saying “maps to higher dimensions”, and know the cost model that ended SVMs’ dominance.

1 · From the ground up

🧩
In plain words

You have dots of two colours and you want a line that keeps them apart. Usually many lines would work, so the real question is which one to pick. An SVM picks the line with the widest empty gap on either side — the one that stays as far as it can from both groups. The rest of the idea is a trick that lets the same method draw a curved boundary without changing anything.

🎯
How far you need to go for a GenAI interview

This section is enough. SVMs come up as a concept check — can you explain the margin, support vectors, and the kernel trick in plain words — not as something you will build. The algebra in the later sections is there for classical-ML specialist roles; you can skip it and still answer every SVM question a GenAI panel is likely to ask.

Start with the problem, not the formula

Two colours of dots on a page, clearly apart. Draw a boundary between them. Now notice something awkward: if the dots really are separable, lots of different lines get every training point right. You can slide and tilt your line quite a bit and still score 100%.

So training accuracy cannot choose between them. It rates them all perfect.

So what: the whole idea starts from a question you only notice once you spot the tie. Not "how do I separate these?" — that is easy — but "which of these equally-perfect lines should I trust on data I have not seen?"

Pick the line with the most room

Imagine a boundary that passes within a hair of one blue dot. On your training data: perfect. But the next blue dot will be slightly different, and if it lands a hair the other way you get it wrong.

So prefer room. Push the boundary until it is as far as possible from the nearest dot of either colour. The empty strip you carve out is the margin, and the boundary runs down its middle. A wider margin means more room to be slightly wrong about the world.

So what: this is the line to say out loud — the margin is a bet that new points will land near old ones of the same colour. That is what "the margin is a generalisation argument" means, and it is the answer to the most likely SVM question you will get.

Watch the gap open up

Below are three boundaries. All three get every training dot right. Two of them skim past the data, so their gaps are slivers. The third is the SVM's answer — pushed out until both walls touch a dot. The dots they touch are ringed.

Slide any of the three boundaries a little and training accuracy does not change — which is exactly why accuracy cannot pick one. The margin is the tie-breaker, and it is a statement about the data you have not seen yet.

Only the dots on the walls matter

Take a dot far from the boundary and drag it further away. The boundary does not move. Delete it entirely — still no movement. The answer is held in place only by the few dots the walls rest against, and those are the support vectors.

So what: two consequences worth saying. The trained model only needs those few points, so it stays small. And it is relaxed about far-away outliers but very sensitive to a mislabelled point sitting near the boundary — the opposite of what most people assume.

Real data overlaps, so allow mistakes for a price

Everything so far assumed a clean gap exists. Put one blue dot deep in the green crowd and the strict version has no answer at all. And one mislabelled point near the edge can drag a good boundary badly off course.

So relax it. Let points break the rule, and charge for it. The setting that fixes the price is C, and this "soft margin" is what every real implementation uses.

So what: C runs backwards from what people expect. Large C = mistakes are expensive = insist on being right = narrow gap, more overfitting. Small C = mistakes are cheap = a wide gap that tolerates some errors = more regularisation. Getting that direction the wrong way round is the single most common SVM slip.
C is a dial between a wide corridor and a correct one

Same data, same algorithm, one changed number. Neither picture is "the right answer" — the left generalises better on noisy data, the right on genuinely clean and separable data. Cross-validation picks, and because C interacts with the RBF gamma, it is picked as a 2-D grid rather than one axis at a time.

When no straight line can ever work

Now the hard case: a green blob in the middle with a blue ring around it. No straight line separates those, ever.

But invent a new feature — distance from the centre. Inner dots have a small distance, outer dots a large one. Plot that new feature and a flat boundary works perfectly.

So what: the boundary is still straight. It is just straight in a space you built rather than the one you were handed. Straight there looks curved back here — and nothing about the method had to change.

The kernel trick, in one sentence

Here is the part worth getting right, because it is the question people actually ask.

When you solve an SVM, the maths only ever needs to know how similar each pair of points is. It never needs their actual coordinates. So if you have a shortcut that returns the similarity two points would have had in that richer space, you can hand over the shortcut and never build the space at all. That shortcut is a kernel, and it usually costs one line of arithmetic.

So what: say it as "the maths only needs pairwise similarities, so I swap in a kernel and the mapping never actually happens" — not "it maps the data to higher dimensions". For the popular RBF kernel that space is infinitely large, so nothing could ever be mapped into it, and the kernel is still a single exp call. That is the whole trick.

Why you will not ship one

Kernel SVMs need to compare every pair of training points, so the work grows roughly with the square of your row count. Ten thousand rows is comfortable. A million is not.

That, plus the fact that gradient-boosted trees handle mixed columns and missing values without complaint, is why SVMs faded from production while staying popular in interviews.

So what: if you are asked when to use one — small dataset, many features, and you want a clean margin-based boundary. Text classification with a linear kernel is the case that still holds up. For anything large or tabular, say gradient boosting and explain the cost.
A curved boundary is a straight one, measured differently

The right-hand picture is what the kernel gives you access to — except that the kernel never actually plots it. It returns the inner products that picture would have produced, which is all the optimiser ever asked for. That is the whole trick, and it is why gamma in the RBF kernel is a similarity range: large gamma means "only very close points count as related", which lets the boundary wrap tightly around individual points and overfit.

Analogy — you are laying a road between two villages and it must not touch either. Many routes stay outside both, but you build the widest road that still fits, because a wide road survives a van drifting a metre off course. Once it is built, look at which buildings its kerbs actually run against: move any other building and the road is unaffected, but nudge one of those few and the whole route shifts. Those are the support vectors. And if the villages are tangled together so no straight road fits, stop mapping by position and map by height instead — a perfectly straight road in that map comes out curved on the ground.

3 · Core intuitions

Maximizing the margin is a generalization argument

Many hyperplanes can separate the training data; they are not equally good. The one with the widest buffer to the nearest points of each class is most robust to perturbation — a small shift in a test point is least likely to flip its classification. This is the geometric expression of what regularization expresses algebraically, and indeed maximizing the margin is equivalent to minimizing ‖w‖ subject to correct classification with a unit functional margin.

Only the support vectors matter

The solution depends solely on the points on or inside the margin boundary. Delete every other training point and refit — you get the identical hyperplane. This has two consequences: SVMs are sparse in the examples (as opposed to lasso’s sparsity in features), and they are relatively insensitive to outliers sitting far from the boundary on the correct side. Being able to state the “delete the rest and nothing changes” fact is the fastest way to prove you understand the model.

C is inverse regularization, controlling the margin/violation trade

Soft margin allows points to violate the margin, penalized by C·Σξᵢ. Large C makes violations expensive, so the model fits a narrow margin classifying training points strictly — low bias, high variance. Small C tolerates violations, giving a wide smooth margin — high bias, low variance. This is the same convention as sklearn’s LogisticRegression, and the same trap: increasing C regularizes less.

The kernel trick, stated correctly

In the dual formulation, the data appears only through inner products xᵢ·xⱼ. So if you want to work in some high-dimensional feature space φ(x), you only ever need φ(xᵢ)·φ(xⱼ). A kernel function K(xᵢ,xⱼ) computes that inner product directly, without ever constructing φ(x). That is the whole trick: you get the expressive power of a high-dimensional — for RBF, infinite-dimensional — space at the cost of evaluating a cheap function on the original inputs. The polynomial kernel (xᵢ·xⱼ + c)^d corresponds to all monomials up to degree d, which for d=3 on 1,000 features would be an astronomically large explicit expansion.

gamma in the RBF kernel is a locality dial

K(x,x') = exp(-γ‖x-x'‖²). Large γ makes the kernel decay fast, so each support vector influences only a tiny neighbourhood — the boundary becomes wiggly and can memorize individual points, i.e. high variance. Small γ makes influence broad and the boundary smooth, approaching linear behaviour. Because C and γ both control effective complexity they interact, which is why RBF-SVM tuning is the textbook example of a 2D grid search over log-spaced values.

Hinge loss versus log loss explains the calibration problem

Hinge loss is max(0, 1 - y·f(x)): exactly zero for points correctly classified beyond the margin. So the model stops caring about a point once it is comfortably right — which is what produces the sparsity in support vectors. Log loss never reaches zero, so logistic regression keeps adjusting for every point and consequently estimates a calibrated probability. The price the SVM pays: its decision_function output is a signed distance, not a probability.

Why SVMs faded

Training cost scales roughly between O(n²) and O(n³) for kernel SVMs, because the kernel matrix is n × n. At a million rows that matrix alone is 8 TB in float64. Gradient-boosted trees handle the same data in minutes, need no scaling, handle mixed types and missing values, and usually win on accuracy for tabular problems. Linear SVMs remain practical at scale and are still competitive on high-dimensional sparse data such as text.

5 · Hands-on drill

  1. Fit a linear SVM on 2D separable data. Plot the hyperplane, both margin boundaries, and circle the support vectors. Then delete all non-support-vector points, refit, and confirm the boundary is identical — that is your visual for the support-vector question.
  2. Sweep C from 0.01 to 1000 on non-separable data and plot how the boundary and the number of support vectors change.
  3. Fit an RBF-SVM on concentric-circles data (which a linear model cannot separate) and sweep gamma to see under- and over-fitting boundaries.
  4. Run a full 2D log-spaced grid search over (C, gamma) and plot the validation-score heatmap. The diagonal ridge in that heatmap is worth describing in interviews.
  5. Time SVC(kernel='rbf') at n = 1,000 / 5,000 / 20,000 and confirm the super-linear scaling yourself, then compare against LGBMClassifier on the same data.
⚠️
Gotchas that fail candidates
  • Not scaling. An unscaled feature dominates the kernel entirely.
  • Getting C’s direction wrong. Large C = less regularization.
  • Saying the kernel trick “maps data to higher dimensions”. It computes inner products as if you had — the point is that you never build φ(x).
  • Claiming SVMs give probabilities. They give a signed distance; probabilities need Platt scaling.
  • Recommending an RBF-SVM for a million rows. Know the O(n²)–O(n³) cost.
  • Not knowing hinge loss. It is the whole reason support vectors are sparse.

6 · Interview questions

6 questions
01What is an SVM trying to do, and what is a support vector?margingeometryfundamentals＋
Strong answer

It finds the hyperplane separating the classes with the maximum margin — the widest possible buffer between the boundary and the nearest points of each class. The reasoning is a generalization argument: among infinitely many separating hyperplanes, the one furthest from both classes is most robust to perturbation, so a slightly shifted test point is least likely to be misclassified. Geometrically the margin width is 2/‖w‖, so maximizing the margin is minimizing ‖w‖ subject to all points being correctly classified with a functional margin of at least 1.

A support vector is a training point lying on the margin boundary or violating it — equivalently, one with a non-zero dual coefficient. These are the only points that matter: delete every other training point, refit, and you get exactly the same hyperplane. That makes the model sparse in examples and relatively robust to outliers sitting far from the boundary on the correct side.

30-second version

It maximizes the margin — the widest buffer to the nearest points of each class — because that is most robust to perturbation. Support vectors are the points on or violating the margin; delete all others and the hyperplane is unchanged.

What’s being tested

Geometric intuition. The “delete the rest and nothing changes” statement is the most efficient way to demonstrate real understanding.

02Explain the kernel trick precisely.kernel trickMercertrap＋
Strong answer

In the dual formulation of the SVM, the training data appears only through inner products xᵢ·xⱼ — never through the individual vectors. So to work in a higher-dimensional feature space via some mapping φ, you would only ever need φ(xᵢ)·φ(xⱼ). A kernel function K(xᵢ,xⱼ) computes that inner product directly from the original inputs, without ever constructing or storing φ(x).

That is the trick, and the emphasis matters: it is not “map the data to higher dimensions” — you get the effect of the mapping while never performing it.

Example: the polynomial kernel (xᵢ·xⱼ + c)^d corresponds to a feature map containing all monomials up to degree d, which for d=3 on 1,000 features would be hundreds of millions of explicit terms; the kernel computes it as one dot product plus a power. The RBF kernel corresponds to an infinite-dimensional space, which you obviously could never construct explicitly. Any function satisfying Mercer’s condition — producing a positive semi-definite Gram matrix — is a valid kernel, which is what guarantees the dual problem stays convex.

30-second version

The dual depends on the data only through inner products, so a kernel computes φ(xᵢ)·φ(xⱼ) directly from the original inputs — you never construct φ. Mercer’s condition guarantees validity and convexity.

What’s being tested

Whether you understand the mechanism or just the slogan. Saying “you never construct φ” and naming Mercer’s condition are the two markers.

Likely follow-up: “So why doesn’t everyone use kernels?” — the kernel matrix is n × n, so memory is O(n²) and training roughly O(n²)–O(n³). Fine at thousands of rows, impossible at millions — which is why kernel methods lost to boosted trees and neural networks on large datasets.
03What do C and gamma do, and how do you tune them?hyperparametersRBFgrid search＋
Strong answer

C is inverse regularization, controlling the penalty on margin violations. Large C makes violations expensive, producing a narrow margin that classifies training points strictly — low bias, high variance. Small C tolerates violations, giving a wide smooth margin — high bias, low variance.

gamma (RBF only) controls the kernel’s locality: K = exp(-γ‖x-x'‖²), so large γ means influence decays quickly and each support vector affects only a small neighbourhood, giving a wiggly high-variance boundary; small γ means broad influence and a smooth boundary approaching linear behaviour.

Because both control effective complexity they interact, so tune them jointly on a 2D log-spaced grid — typically C ∈ {10⁻², …, 10³} and γ ∈ {10⁻⁴, …, 10¹}, or gamma='scale' as a sensible start since it adapts to feature variance. The validation heatmap usually shows a diagonal ridge of good combinations rather than a single optimum, which is itself informative: high C with low γ and low C with high γ can perform similarly.

30-second version

C is inverse regularization trading margin width against violations; gamma is RBF locality, large meaning wiggly and local. They interact, so tune jointly on a log-spaced 2D grid — expect a diagonal ridge, not a point.

What’s being tested

Whether you know both parameters, their directions, and that they must be tuned together. The diagonal-ridge observation is a nice practitioner detail.

04SVM vs logistic regression. When would you pick each?comparisonhinge losscalibration＋
Strong answer

Both are linear classifiers; they differ in loss and therefore in behaviour. The SVM uses hinge loss, exactly zero once a point is correctly classified beyond the margin — so the model ignores comfortable points, depends only on support vectors, and optimizes a geometric margin. Logistic regression uses log loss, never zero, so every point contributes and the model estimates a calibrated probability.

Pick logistic regression when you need probabilities for expected-cost thresholds or pricing, when you want interpretable coefficients and odds ratios, when n is large since it scales linearly and supports SGD, or when you need a fast easily-monitored production model.

Pick an SVM when you need a non-linear boundary and n is small-to-moderate (RBF kernel), when the data is high-dimensional and sparse — text with a linear kernel is a classic strong case — or when the margin’s robustness to distant outliers is desirable.

In 2026 practice, for tabular problems you would usually pick logistic regression as the interpretable baseline and gradient boosting as the accurate model, with kernel SVMs occupying a narrow niche.

30-second version

Hinge loss ignores comfortably-correct points, giving support-vector sparsity but no probabilities; log loss uses every point and yields calibrated probabilities. So logistic regression when you need probabilities or scale, SVM for non-linear boundaries on small-to-moderate data.

What’s being tested

Whether you can connect a loss function to practical model properties. Calibration is the key axis, and noting that hinge loss creates support-vector sparsity shows the two ideas are connected in your head.

05Does an SVM output probabilities?calibrationPlatt scalingsklearn＋
Strong answer

Not natively. decision_function returns a signed distance from the hyperplane — larger magnitude means further from the boundary, but it is on an arbitrary scale and is not a probability.

To get probabilities you fit a post-hoc calibration map, which is exactly what sklearn’s probability=True does: it runs Platt scaling, fitting a one-dimensional logistic regression on cross-validated decision-function values.

Two caveats worth raising: it is expensive, because it requires internal 5-fold cross-validation during fit; and the resulting predict_proba can be mildly inconsistent with predict, since the two are computed differently. If probabilities are central to your application, that is a reason to prefer logistic regression or a calibrated boosted model in the first place.

30-second version

No — decision_function gives a signed distance on an arbitrary scale. probability=True applies Platt scaling via internal cross-validation, which is slow and can disagree with predict.

What’s being tested

Awareness that a decision score is not a probability — a distinction mattering for the whole Module 10 metric discussion. The probability=True cost and inconsistency detail proves practical experience.

06You have 2 million rows and 300 features. Would you use an RBF-SVM?scalecostNystromtrap＋
Strong answer

No. A kernel SVM requires the n × n kernel matrix, so memory is O(n²) — at 2 million rows that is on the order of terabytes — and training scales roughly O(n²)–O(n³). It is simply infeasible.

Realistic options: gradient-boosted trees (LightGBM or XGBoost), which handle this scale in minutes, need no scaling, tolerate mixed types and missing values, and will most likely be more accurate on tabular data anyway; a linear SVM via LinearSVC or SGDClassifier(loss='hinge'), which avoids the kernel matrix and scales linearly, if you specifically want hinge-loss margin behaviour; logistic regression with SGD for a fast calibrated baseline; or, if you genuinely need the RBF’s non-linearity, an explicit kernel approximation such as Nyström or random Fourier features (RBFSampler), which approximates the kernel in a few thousand explicit dimensions at linear cost, followed by a linear model.

I would start with LightGBM and only explore the others if there were a specific reason.

30-second version

No — the n×n kernel matrix is terabytes at that scale and training is O(n²)–O(n³). Use gradient-boosted trees, a linear SVM, or approximate the kernel with Nyström or random Fourier features plus a linear model.

What’s being tested

Whether you know the cost model and have a real alternative ready. Naming Nyström or random Fourier features is a genuine differentiator — few candidates know the kernel-approximation route exists.
