Module 16 · Part D Trees & Ensembles

Bagging & Random Forest

Guaranteed in interviews, and the bagging-versus-boosting question is near-universal. The variance identity below is the single highest-value thing to memorise here.

Prerequisites · Modules 05, 15Study time · 6 h6 interview questionsHighest priority
🎯
Your interview goal

Write the correlated-average variance identity, explain feature subsampling as reducing rho rather than “adding randomness”, derive the 36.8% out-of-bag fraction, and answer the n_estimators overfitting contrast perfectly.

1 · From the ground up

🧩
In plain words

One deep tree is roughly right on average but wildly unstable — change a few rows and it redraws itself. So build hundreds of trees on slightly different samples and average their answers. The individual mistakes are partly random, so they partly cancel out. Random Forest then adds the trick that is its real contribution: it forces the trees to disagree with each other, because trees that agree cannot cancel anything.

🎯
How far you need to go for a GenAI interview

Learn the one formula in step 3 and the bagging-versus-boosting contrast. That is the most-asked classical-ML question in GenAI interviews, because it tests whether you understand bias and variance rather than whether you have memorised an API. Everything else here is context.

Start with the problem, not the formula

The last module ended on a specific complaint: a fully grown tree gets the general shape right but is unstable. Resample your data and you get a visibly different tree, because one flipped split near the top redraws everything below it.

That is a good problem to have, because averaging fixes instability and leaves the general shape alone. Average many estimates of the same thing and the wobble shrinks while the centre stays put. That is the oldest trick in statistics, and it is the entire plan.

So what: this is why bagging uses deep, unpruned trees. Averaging a set of shallow, similarly-wrong models leaves you equally wrong. You want each tree overfitting a little, in a different direction, so the errors have something to cancel.

Getting different trees out of one dataset

Averaging needs several trees and you have one dataset. So manufacture variety: draw a sample the same size as your data, but with replacement, so some rows appear twice and others not at all. Train a tree on that. Repeat. This is bagging — short for bootstrap aggregating.

A handy fact falls out: each tree ends up seeing about 63% of your distinct rows and missing about 37%.

So what: those missed rows are free validation data. Score each row using only the trees that never saw it and you get an out-of-bag estimate — a held-out score without holding anything out, and without cross-validation.

The one formula worth memorising

Now be precise about what averaging actually buys, because "it reduces variance" is only half true. For n estimates that are each a bit wobbly and are correlated with each other by an amount ρ:

variance of the average = ρσ² + (1 − ρ)σ² / n

Look at the two pieces. The second shrinks away as you add trees. The first does not move at all. So adding trees forever does not take the wobble to zero — it flattens out at ρσ², a floor set purely by how much your trees agree with each other.

So what: this is the highest-value thing in the module. It says adding trees has shrinking and eventually zero return, and that the only way to lower the floor is to make the trees disagree more. Everything Random Forest does differently from plain bagging is an attack on ρ.

See the floor

Below is that formula plotted for three levels of agreement. Follow any line to the right and watch it stop improving. Where it stops is ρ.

ρ = 0ρ = 0.2ρ = 0.6the floor ρσ²

All three curves start at the same place, because with one tree there is nothing to average. What differs is where they stop falling. This is why "how many trees?" is the boring hyperparameter — past a few hundred it does almost nothing — while max_features, which sets ρ, is the one that matters.

Why bagging alone leaves the trees too similar

Bootstrapping changes the rows, but every tree still gets to look at every column. So if one feature is much more predictive than the rest, every tree finds that same feature to be the best first split — and once the tops match, the trees look alike all the way down.

Similar trees make similar mistakes on the same rows. That is what high ρ means, and the formula above says a high floor follows.

So what: bagged trees end up correlated through the strong features, not through the row sampling. That observation is the whole reason Random Forest exists, and stating it is what a good answer sounds like.

The actual trick: hide most of the columns at every split

Random Forest keeps the bootstrap and adds one rule. At every single split — not once per tree — it picks a random handful of columns and may only choose from those. The usual default is around the square root of your column count.

Now the dominant feature is simply unavailable at many nodes, so those nodes have to find the second- or third-best structure instead. The trees come out genuinely different, ρ drops, and the floor drops with it.

So what: this is a deliberate trade. Each individual tree gets slightly worse — you sometimes hid its best split — but the average gets better, because the floor fell further than the individual quality did. Accepting a worse part to get a better whole is the idea to say out loud.
Bagging correlates through strong features; Random Forest breaks it

Only the root labels differ, and that is the entire point: a different first question makes a structurally different tree. Set max_features to all p columns and a Random Forest collapses back into plain bagging — which is the cleanest one-line statement of the difference between them.

What can and cannot go wrong

Adding more trees cannot overfit. The prediction is an average settling toward a fixed answer, so extra trees buy accuracy that flattens out and then just costs compute. It is a rare setting you can turn up and stop thinking about.

Everything else can still go wrong. Hide too many columns and each tree is starved of good splits. Very noisy data with fully grown trees can still memorise. And the forest inherits two limits from the single tree that averaging does not repair: it cannot predict outside its training range, and its built-in feature importances stay biased toward high-cardinality features — use permutation importance instead (Module 21).

So what: the precise version is "more trees never overfits, but a forest is not immune to overfitting". And the two inherited limits are the follow-up that catches people who learned forests as a black box.
Analogy — you ask a hundred forecasters instead of one, because their individual mistakes partly cancel. But if all hundred read the same morning briefing, their consensus is barely better than one of them — they are wrong together, and hiring more forecasters does not help. That shared briefing is ρ. Random Forest is the rule that each forecaster may only read a random few of the available sources. Everyone becomes slightly less informed, and the consensus gets sharper, because now their mistakes are finally independent enough to cancel.

3 · Core intuitions

Averaging independent estimates reduces variance

If you have B independent estimators each with variance σ², the variance of their mean is σ²/B. That is the entire idea behind bagging: fit many high-variance low-bias models and average them. Bias is essentially unchanged, since each model is approximately unbiased and the average of unbiased estimators is unbiased — so bagging buys variance reduction almost for free.

But bagged trees are not independent, and this is the key insight

They are all fitted on samples from the same dataset, so their errors are correlated. The variance of the average of B estimators with pairwise correlation ρ is:

bagging_variance_identity.txt
Var = rho * sigma²  +  (1 - rho) * sigma² / B
      ------------        --------------------
      irreducible         -> 0 as B -> infinity
      correlation
      FLOOR

The second term vanishes as you add trees, but the first does not. So there is a hard floor on how much averaging can help, set by ρ. Adding the 500th tree to a bagged ensemble barely helps because you have already exhausted the (1-ρ)σ²/B term. The only way to improve further is to reduce ρ — and that is exactly what Random Forest does.

Random feature subsampling decorrelates the trees

At each split, a Random Forest considers only a random subset of features — sqrt(p) by default for classification. This means a single dominant feature cannot be used at the root of every tree — sometimes it is not even available — so different trees are forced to explore different structures and their errors become less correlated.

You pay a small price in individual tree quality (each tree is slightly worse than it could be, so bias rises slightly) in exchange for a large reduction in ρ, which lowers the variance floor. That trade is the entire innovation of Random Forest over plain bagging, and being able to state it in those terms — with the variance identity — is one of the highest-value answers in this course.

OOB error is free cross-validation

Each bootstrap sample omits about 36.8% of the rows, since the probability a given row is never picked in n draws with replacement is (1 - 1/n)ⁿ → 1/e ≈ 0.368. So for every training row there is a subset of trees that never saw it, and you can predict that row using only those trees. Aggregating over all rows gives an out-of-bag error estimate roughly comparable to k-fold CV — at no extra training cost.

Caveats worth mentioning: it is less reliable with few trees, and it assumes rows are i.i.d., so it is invalid under temporal or group structure, where you still need a proper time- or group-aware split.

More trees never meaningfully overfit a Random Forest

Each tree is fitted independently, and prediction is an average. Adding trees reduces the (1-ρ)σ²/B term monotonically and then plateaus; it does not push the ensemble toward the training data. So n_estimators is a compute-and-latency decision, not a regularization decision — set it as high as your budget allows and stop when the OOB curve flattens. This is diametrically opposite to boosting, where n_estimators is the primary overfitting risk. That contrast is the single most-asked ensemble question.

Extra Trees push randomization further

Instead of searching for the best threshold on each candidate feature, Extra Trees pick thresholds at random and take the best among those random candidates; they also typically use the whole dataset rather than bootstrap samples. This further reduces correlation and is much faster (no threshold search), at the cost of slightly higher bias per tree. On noisy data they sometimes beat Random Forests, and they are worth naming as a cheap thing to try.

6 · Hands-on drill

  1. Verify the 63.2% result empirically: bootstrap-sample repeatedly and count unique rows.
  2. Implement bagging over your Module 15 tree from scratch. Plot test error against B and watch it plateau — that plateau is the ρσ² floor.
  3. Add random feature subsampling to get a Random Forest. Plot test error against max_features from 1 to p and find the interior optimum. This plot is the empirical proof of the decorrelation argument and is worth describing verbally in interviews.
  4. Compute the average pairwise correlation of tree predictions for bagging versus RF and confirm RF’s is lower.
  5. Compare OOB error against 5-fold CV error on the same data; note how close they are and how much less compute OOB cost.
  6. Compare feature_importances_ with permutation_importance on a dataset containing a high-cardinality ID column. The ID will rank absurdly high on MDI and near zero on permutation importance — that contrast is the answer to the feature-importance question.
⚠️
Gotchas that fail candidates
  • Saying Random Forest is just bagging. The feature subsampling is the point.
  • Not being able to explain why feature subsampling helps. “Decorrelation” plus the variance identity is the answer.
  • Getting the n_estimators overfitting comparison wrong.
  • Trusting feature_importances_ uncritically. Know the high-cardinality bias.
  • Not knowing the 63.2% / 36.8% bootstrap result.
  • Using OOB error on time-series data. It assumes i.i.d. rows.
  • Saying RF handles missing values natively in sklearn. It does not — you must impute, unlike XGBoost and LightGBM.

7 · Interview questions

6 questions
01Bagging vs boosting. Explain the difference completely.ensemblesbaggingboosting＋
Strong answer

They are opposite strategies against opposite problems.

Bagging trains many models independently in parallel, each on a bootstrap resample, then averages or votes. Base learners are deliberately low-bias and high-variance — typically fully grown trees — and averaging cancels their uncorrelated errors, so bagging primarily reduces variance while leaving bias roughly unchanged. It is trivially parallelizable, robust to noise, forgiving of hyperparameters, and adding estimators plateaus rather than overfits.

Boosting trains models sequentially, each focused on what the current ensemble gets wrong — via reweighted samples in AdaBoost or by fitting the negative gradient of the loss in gradient boosting. Base learners are deliberately weak and high-bias — shallow trees or stumps — and the sequence progressively reduces bias. It is not parallelizable across trees, is more sensitive to noise and mislabeled points because it keeps chasing hard examples, needs careful tuning, and will overfit as trees accumulate, so it requires early stopping.

Practically: bagging if you want a strong robust model with minimal tuning; boosting if you want maximum accuracy on tabular data and can afford to tune and validate properly.

30-second version

Bagging trains in parallel on bootstrap samples with deep learners to cut variance, and plateaus rather than overfits. Boosting trains sequentially with weak learners to cut bias, is noise-sensitive, and needs early stopping.

What’s being tested

The single most common ensemble question. A complete answer covers training order, base-learner strength, which error component is reduced, parallelizability, and overfitting behaviour. Cover all five and you are in the top band.

02Random Forest is bagging plus what, and why does that addition matter?decorrelationvariance identitytrap＋
Strong answer

Plus random feature subsampling at every split: each split considers only a random subset of features — √p by default for classification, p/3 for regression — rather than all of them.

It matters because of the variance identity for an average of correlated estimators: Var = ρσ² + (1−ρ)σ²/B. The second term goes to zero as you add trees, but the first — set by the pairwise correlation ρ between trees — is a hard floor no amount of averaging can beat.

Plain bagged trees are strongly correlated, because if one feature is dominantly predictive, nearly every tree splits on it at the root and the trees end up structurally similar. Feature subsampling prevents that: sometimes the dominant feature is not even in the candidate set, so trees are forced to explore different structures and ρ drops, lowering the variance floor.

The cost is that each individual tree is slightly worse — bias rises a little — but the reduction in ρ more than compensates. So Random Forest deliberately makes each model worse to make the ensemble better, which is the elegant idea at its core.

30-second version

Plus random feature subsampling per split. Since Var = ρσ² + (1−ρ)σ²/B, adding trees only kills the second term — the ρσ² floor remains. Subsampling decorrelates the trees, lowering ρ and therefore the floor.

What’s being tested

Whether you can produce the variance identity and the decorrelation argument. The highest-signal answer in the module — most candidates say “it adds randomness” and stop.

Likely follow-up: “Why √p specifically?” — an empirical default from Breiman’s work balancing decorrelation against individual tree quality: too few features and each tree is badly handicapped, too many and correlation stays high. It is worth tuning, and the optimum is usually an interior point you can find by plotting test error against max_features.
03What is out-of-bag error and why is it useful?OOBderivationvalidation＋
Strong answer

A bootstrap sample of size n drawn with replacement omits each particular row with probability (1 − 1/n)ⁿ, which converges to 1/e ≈ 0.368 — so about 36.8% of rows are out-of-bag for each tree and about 63.2% are in-bag.

For any training row, the subset of trees that never saw it can be used to predict it; aggregating those predictions across all rows gives the out-of-bag error. It is useful because it is essentially free validation: no separate holdout, no extra model fits, and it is generally comparable to k-fold cross-validation, so you can use it for model selection and to decide when the OOB curve has flattened with respect to n_estimators.

Caveats: it is noisy with few trees; it can be mildly pessimistic since each row is predicted by only ~37% of the ensemble; and crucially it assumes rows are i.i.d., so it is invalid under temporal or group structure, where you still need a time-based or GroupKFold split.

30-second version

Each bootstrap omits about 36.8% of rows — (1−1/n)ⁿ → 1/e — so every row can be predicted by the trees that never saw it. That gives free validation comparable to k-fold, but it assumes i.i.d. rows.

What’s being tested

Whether you can derive the 36.8% and know the i.i.d. caveat. The caveat is the part that distinguishes a practitioner.

04Can you overfit a Random Forest?overfittingn_estimatorstrap＋
Strong answer

Not by adding trees — but yes, in other ways, and the distinction matters.

Adding trees is safe: each is fitted independently and prediction is an average, so more trees monotonically reduce the (1−ρ)σ²/B variance term and then plateau. Test error flattens; it does not rise. So n_estimators is a compute and latency choice, not a regularization one.

You can still overfit a Random Forest by: growing trees fully on a small or noisy dataset, where each leaf holds one or two samples and the ensemble memorizes label noise; setting max_features too high, which raises ρ and undermines the whole mechanism; or having far more features than samples, where even decorrelated trees find spurious splits. Controls: min_samples_leaf, max_depth, and a lower max_features.

The contrast with boosting is the point of the question — there, n_estimators is the primary overfitting risk and early stopping is mandatory.

30-second version

Not via n_estimators — trees are independent and averaged, so error plateaus. But yes via fully grown trees on small or noisy data, too high a max_features, or d ≫ n.

What’s being tested

Precision. The complete answer is “not via n_estimators, but yes via tree complexity” — candidates who answer a flat “no, RF cannot overfit” are overstating it.

05How do you interpret feature_importances_ from a Random Forest?feature importanceMDIpermutationtrap＋
Strong answer

Carefully, because sklearn’s default is mean decrease in impurity (MDI) — total impurity reduction attributable to each feature, averaged over trees — and it has three known biases.

  1. It favours high-cardinality and continuous features, because more candidate split points mean more chances to reduce impurity; a random unique-ID column can score high while carrying zero signal.
  2. It is computed on training data, so it reflects what the model used to fit, including noise — not what generalizes.
  3. With correlated features it splits credit arbitrarily among them, so an important feature can look unimportant because a correlated twin absorbed the attribution.

The better tool is permutation importance on a held-out set: shuffle one feature’s values and measure the drop in validation performance, which directly measures contribution to generalization and is model-agnostic — though it too is distorted by correlated features, since a twin can substitute for the shuffled one. For real interpretation work, SHAP gives consistent per-prediction attributions (Module 21).

And the most important caveat regardless of method: importance is not causation — a feature can be important because it proxies for an unmeasured cause.

30-second version

The default is MDI, which is biased toward high-cardinality features, computed on training data, and splits credit arbitrarily among correlated features. Prefer permutation importance on held-out data — and never read it as causal.

What’s being tested

Whether you know the default is untrustworthy. Naming MDI versus permutation importance and the correlated-feature caveat is the strong answer.

06When would you choose Random Forest over XGBoost in 2026?appliedmodel selectionnoisy labels＋
Strong answer

Several legitimate cases. (1) Minimal tuning budget — RF’s defaults are strong and forgiving, whereas boosted trees need learning rate, tree count, depth and regularization tuned to reach their potential; with hours rather than days, RF often gets closer to its ceiling. (2) Noisy labels — boosting keeps focusing on hard examples so it chases mislabeled points, while bagging averages them out; RF is meaningfully more robust here. (3) Free validation via OOB when data is limited and you want an honest estimate without spending rows on a holdout. (4) Trivial parallelism — trees train independently, so wall-clock scales with cores, which matters for frequent retraining. (5) A robust baseline establishing what is achievable before investing in tuning. (6) Very high-dimensional data with many irrelevant features, where feature subsampling provides useful implicit selection.

That said, I would be honest that on most tabular problems a well-tuned LightGBM or XGBoost model wins on accuracy and trains faster on large data, so RF is typically my strong baseline rather than my final model.

30-second version

Minimal tuning budget, noisy labels where boosting chases mislabeled points, free OOB validation on limited data, trivial parallelism, and as the baseline. But a tuned GBDT usually wins on accuracy.

What’s being tested

Whether you can defend the simpler ensemble on real grounds. The noisy-labels point is the most technically interesting and least commonly given.
