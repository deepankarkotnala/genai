Clustering & Dimensionality Reduction

What to do when there are no labels — and why unsupervised results should be treated as hypotheses to validate rather than answers to ship.

Your interview goal

Enumerate K-Means's assumptions and attach each to a failure mode, cite the spectral theorem for PCA orthogonality, explain why variance is not importance, and refuse to over-read a t-SNE plot.

1 · From the ground up

🧩
In plain words

Everything so far had labels to learn from. Here there are none. Two different jobs live in this module: clustering groups the rows — which customers belong together — and dimensionality reduction shrinks the columns — turning fifty features into five. What makes both awkward is the same thing: with no labels there is no right answer to check against, so you cannot score them the way you score a classifier. That single fact changes how you use them and how you talk about them.

Start with the problem, not the formula

You have a table of customers and no labels at all. Someone asks for "our customer segments". Run any clustering algorithm and it will happily hand you five groups. Run a different one and you get five different groups. Both are valid outputs.

Nobody can tell you which is correct, because there is no correct answer sitting in the data waiting to be found. The groups are something you impose, not something you discover.

So what: a clustering result is a hypothesis, not a finding. The right test is not "is it right?" — that question has no answer — but "is it stable, and is it useful?" Do you get roughly the same groups on a different sample, and can the business actually do something different for each group? Saying this is what separates someone who has shipped a segmentation from someone who has only run one.

K-Means is four lines, and every quirk follows from them

The algorithm is small enough to state completely: pick k starting centres. Assign every point to its nearest centre. Move each centre to the average of the points assigned to it. Repeat until nothing moves.

That is all of it. And what it is quietly minimising is the total squared distance from each point to its centre.

So what: because it is squared distance to a single centre point, K-Means can only ever draw round clusters of roughly equal size. Every limitation below is that one sentence showing up in a different disguise. Squared distance also means it is dragged by outliers, and distance means you must scale your features first — same rule as KNN.

The panels below show the same algorithm on two datasets. On round blobs it does exactly what you hoped. On two curved shapes it produces a confident, tidy, completely wrong answer — because a curve is not round, and no amount of tuning k will fix a shape assumption.

You only know it is wrong because you can see the shape — which you cannot do in fifty dimensions. That is the real argument for plotting your data before trusting a clustering.

Choosing k, honestly

Two standard tools. The elbow method plots the within-cluster sum of squares against k: it always falls as k rises, so you look for the bend where extra clusters stop paying. The silhouette score asks, for each point, how much closer it is to its own cluster than to the next nearest — it ranges from −1 to 1 and you take the k with the highest average.

Both are useful. Neither is proof. Elbows are often ambiguous, and silhouette quietly prefers round clusters — the same bias K-Means already has.

So what: if the marketing team has budget for exactly four campaigns, k = 4 is a better-justified answer than anything the elbow tells you. With no ground truth, "the number the business can act on" is a legitimate criterion, and often the strongest one.

The two alternatives worth knowing

DBSCAN groups by density rather than distance-to-a-centre. It grows a cluster wherever points are packed closely enough, so it handles any shape, does not need you to pick k, and labels genuinely isolated points as noise instead of forcing them into a group. The price: you must choose eps (how close counts as close) and min_samples, and it struggles when one cluster is dense and another is sparse, because a single eps cannot suit both.

Gaussian Mixture Models keep the centre idea but allow each cluster to be a stretched ellipse rather than a circle, and give every point a probability of belonging to each cluster rather than a hard assignment.

So what: a neat way to hold all three together — K-Means is a Gaussian Mixture where every cluster is forced to be a circle of the same size and every point must pick one. Relax the shape and you get GMM; abandon centres entirely and go by crowding and you get DBSCAN.

PCA is the other job: fewer columns, not fewer rows

Now the second half of the module. You have fifty features, many of them saying overlapping things. PCA finds a new set of axes: first the single direction along which the data is most spread out, then the direction of most remaining spread that is at right angles to the first, and so on.

Why insist on right angles? Because a new axis that partly repeated the old one would be telling you something you already knew. Perpendicular guarantees each component carries fresh information.

So what: the components come out ordered by how much of the spread they capture, so you keep the first few and drop the rest. And because PCA chases variance, you must scale first — otherwise a feature measured in rupees will dominate component 1 purely because its numbers are large.
PCA finds the direction of most spread, then turns 90° for the next

The bars always fall, and they always sum to 100%. The usual rule is to keep enough components to reach 90–95%, though if you are reducing purely to plot something, you keep two whatever they add up to — and you say so, because two components covering 40% is a very different picture from two covering 95%.

What PCA takes away from you

Every component is a blend of all your original features. Component 1 is not "income" — it is 0.4 × income plus 0.3 × age minus 0.2 × tenure and forty more terms. So the moment you use PCA, you have traded away the ability to say "this prediction happened because of income".

There is a second, sharper cost. PCA never looks at your labels. It chases spread, and spread is not the same as usefulness. If the direction that separates your two classes happens to be a narrow one, PCA will rank it low and you may discard the only thing that mattered.

So what: use PCA to fight the curse of dimensionality, collinearity, or plotting — not as a routine step. When separating known classes is the goal, LDA looks at the labels and PCA does not (Module 13), and that is the whole difference.

t-SNE and UMAP are for looking, not for measuring

These make beautiful two-dimensional pictures of high-dimensional data, and they are widely misread. They work by preserving which points are near each other locally, and they deliberately sacrifice everything else to do it.

So in a t-SNE plot: the size of a blob means nothing, the distance between two blobs means nothing, and empty space means nothing. Change the perplexity setting and the picture rearranges itself.

So what: never measure anything off a t-SNE plot, never cluster on t-SNE coordinates, and never feed them to a model — the axes have no units and the geometry is not real. Use it to notice that groups exist, then go back to the original features to describe them.
Analogy — clustering is being handed a room of strangers and told to arrange them into groups, with nobody to tell you afterwards whether you got it right. Sort by age and you get one sensible answer; sort by profession and you get a completely different, equally sensible answer. The question "which grouping is correct?" has no answer — only "which grouping helps me do my job?" And PCA is photographing a chair. A chair is three-dimensional, but one well-chosen angle tells you almost everything about it, and a second angle at right angles fills in most of the rest. You have lost real information, and you kept the part that mattered — which is exactly the trade PCA makes.

3 · Core intuitions

K-Means minimizes within-cluster sum of squares by alternating two steps

Assign each point to its nearest centroid; move each centroid to the mean of its assigned points; repeat. Each step monotonically decreases the objective, so it always converges — but to a local optimum, since the objective is non-convex over assignments. That is why you run it multiple times from different initializations (n_init) and keep the best, and why K-Means++ exists: it seeds centroids far apart probabilistically, which reliably improves both the final objective and convergence speed.

K-Means bakes in strong geometric assumptions

Because it assigns by Euclidean distance to a centroid, its implied cluster shape is a sphere — more precisely, the partition is a Voronoi tessellation, so boundaries are linear. It also implicitly prefers clusters of similar size and density, since a large cluster’s centroid gets pulled toward wherever the mass is. So it fails on: elongated or crescent-shaped clusters, clusters of very different sizes, clusters of different densities, and any data where Euclidean distance is not meaningful. When you see those, reach for DBSCAN/HDBSCAN (arbitrary shapes, density-based) or a GMM (elliptical clusters with per-component covariance).

The elbow method is a heuristic, not an answer

Plot within-cluster sum of squares against k; it decreases monotonically, and you look for the bend where additional clusters stop paying. The problem is that real data often has no clear bend, and the reading is subjective. Silhouette score is better — it measures how much closer each point is to its own cluster than to the next nearest, on a −1 to +1 scale, and has an actual maximum you can select. But the honest senior answer is that k is usually a business decision: if the marketing team can run four campaigns, k = 4 regardless of what the elbow plot says, and your job becomes checking whether four clusters are stable and interpretable.

DBSCAN’s real advantages are shape-freedom and explicit noise

It grows clusters from dense neighbourhoods: a point with at least min_samples neighbours within eps is a core point, points reachable from core points join the cluster, and points in no dense region are labelled noise. So it finds arbitrarily shaped clusters, does not need k in advance, and — uniquely among the common methods — refuses to assign outliers, which is genuinely useful. The costs: it is very sensitive to eps, it struggles when clusters have very different densities (one eps cannot suit both), and it degrades in high dimensions like every distance-based method. HDBSCAN removes most of the eps sensitivity by building a cluster hierarchy across density levels, and is the better default in 2026.

PCA finds orthogonal directions of maximum variance, and it is an eigenproblem

Standardize the data, compute the covariance matrix, and take its eigenvectors: those are the principal components, and each eigenvalue is the variance captured along its component. Because the covariance matrix is real and symmetric, the spectral theorem guarantees an orthogonal eigenbasis — that is why components are uncorrelated, and it is a better answer than asserting it. Equivalently, PCA is the truncated SVD of the centred data matrix, which is how it is actually computed for numerical stability and to handle d > n. It also has a dual characterization: the top-k components are simultaneously the directions of maximum variance and the k-dimensional subspace minimizing reconstruction error.

Variance is not importance — the sharpest PCA caveat

PCA is unsupervised, so it has no idea what you are predicting. The direction of greatest variance can be one along which your classes are completely mixed, while a low-variance direction carries all the discriminative signal. So PCA before a supervised model can discard exactly the information you needed. Use it for decorrelation, visualization, noise reduction and compression — and if you want a supervised projection, use LDA or partial least squares instead.

t-SNE and UMAP plots must be read carefully

They preserve local neighbourhood structure, so tight groups in the plot really are similar points. But global geometry is not preserved: the distances between clusters are not meaningful, cluster sizes in the plot do not reflect real cluster sizes, and both methods are stochastic so different runs give different-looking pictures. They are also strongly hyperparameter-dependent (t-SNE’s perplexity, UMAP’s n_neighbors) and can manufacture apparent clusters in random data at some settings. Use them for exploration and communication, never as evidence, and never as a preprocessing step feeding a supervised model — UMAP is at least deterministic with a fixed seed and has a transform for new data, which t-SNE fundamentally lacks.

4 · Whiteboard formulas

clustering_pca.txt
=== K-Means ===
objective (WCSS)   J = SUM_k SUM_{x in C_k} ||x - mu_k||²
assign step        C_k = { x : k = argmin_j ||x - mu_j|| }
update step        mu_k = mean of points in C_k
converges to a LOCAL optimum; each step decreases J monotonically
cost               O(n * k * d * iters)

silhouette for point i:   s(i) = (b(i) - a(i)) / max(a(i), b(i))     in [-1, 1]
   a(i) = mean distance to own-cluster points
   b(i) = mean distance to nearest other cluster

=== PCA ===
1. standardize X (zero mean, unit variance)
2. covariance     Sigma = (1/(n-1)) X'X
3. eigendecompose Sigma = V L V'      (V orthogonal since Sigma is symmetric)
4. components     columns of V, ordered by eigenvalue
5. project        Z = X * V_k         (k largest components)
explained variance ratio for component i:  lambda_i / SUM_j lambda_j
equivalently: truncated SVD of centred X = U S V'   (preferred numerically)

=== GMM / EM ===
model      p(x) = SUM_k pi_k * N(x | mu_k, Sigma_k)
E-step     responsibilities gamma_ik = pi_k N(x_i|mu_k,S_k) / SUM_j pi_j N(x_i|mu_j,S_j)
M-step     update pi_k, mu_k, Sigma_k as gamma-weighted estimates
K-Means is the limit of GMM with shared spherical Sigma -> 0, hard assignment

=== DBSCAN ===
core point: >= min_samples points within eps
cluster: maximal set of density-connected points
noise: not reachable from any core point -> labelled -1

5 · Hands-on drill

  1. Implement K-Means from scratch including K-Means++ initialization. Run it 20 times on the same data from random inits and record the spread in final WCSS — that spread is your evidence for the local-optimum answer.
  2. Generate three datasets: spherical blobs, two crescents, and clusters of very different densities. Run K-Means, DBSCAN and GMM on all three and plot the nine results. This 3×3 grid is the single best preparation for “when does K-Means fail?”
  3. Produce elbow and silhouette plots for k from 2 to 15 on real data and note whether they agree.
  4. Implement PCA from scratch via both eigendecomposition and SVD; verify they match sklearn.
  5. Construct a labelled dataset where the top principal component is not the discriminative direction, then compare a classifier on PCA features versus LDA features. That is your evidence for the variance-is-not-importance answer.
  6. Run t-SNE on the same data with perplexity 5, 30 and 100 and with different seeds. The visual instability is what you should describe when asked about it.
⚠️
Gotchas that fail candidates
  • Not scaling before K-Means or PCA.
  • Saying K-Means always converges to the global optimum. Local only — use n_init and K-Means++.
  • Presenting the elbow method as definitive.
  • Claiming PCA components are interpretable features. They are linear combinations of everything.
  • Saying PCA is feature selection. It is feature extraction — it builds new features from all the old ones, so you cannot drop the original columns from your pipeline.
  • Reading cluster distances off a t-SNE plot.
  • Using t-SNE as a preprocessing step for a supervised model.
  • Presenting cluster labels as ground truth rather than as hypotheses to validate.

6 · Interview questions

6 questions
01Explain K-Means and its assumptions.K-MeansassumptionsVoronoi＋
Strong answer

K-Means partitions data into k clusters minimizing within-cluster sum of squares. It alternates two steps: assign each point to the nearest centroid, then move each centroid to the mean of its assigned points. Both steps monotonically decrease the objective, so it always converges — but only to a local optimum, since the objective is non-convex over assignments; hence multiple restarts (n_init) and K-Means++ seeding.

Its assumptions are strong and worth stating explicitly: clusters are roughly spherical — since assignment is by Euclidean distance to a centre, the partition is a Voronoi tessellation with linear boundaries — of roughly similar size and similar density, and k is known in advance. It also requires feature scaling, is sensitive to outliers because centroids are means, and assumes Euclidean distance is meaningful — which fails in high dimensions.

When those assumptions do not hold — elongated clusters, wildly different densities, unknown k, or outliers you want identified rather than absorbed — DBSCAN/HDBSCAN or a Gaussian mixture model are better choices.

30-second version

Alternate assign-to-nearest-centroid and move-centroid-to-mean; it converges but only locally. It assumes spherical, similar-size, similar-density clusters with k known, needs scaling, and is outlier-sensitive.

What’s being tested

Whether you can enumerate assumptions and connect each to a failure mode. The Voronoi / linear-boundary observation is a nice depth signal.

02How do you choose k?elbowsilhouettebusinesstrap＋
Strong answer

With a combination of statistical heuristics and, decisively, business context. The elbow method plots within-cluster sum of squares against k and looks for the bend — but WCSS decreases monotonically, real data often has no clear bend, and the reading is subjective. Silhouette score is better, since it measures how much closer points are to their own cluster than the next nearest and has an actual maximum; gap statistic and Davies–Bouldin are further options, and for a GMM you can use BIC/AIC, which properly penalize complexity.

But the honest answer is that in most real projects k is a business decision: if the marketing team can operate four distinct campaigns, k = 4 regardless of the elbow plot, and my job becomes verifying that four clusters are stable (consistent across resamples and seeds) and interpretable (each has a describable profile a stakeholder can act on).

I would also check whether cluster membership actually predicts anything downstream, since a segmentation nobody can act on is worthless no matter how good its silhouette score.

30-second version

Elbow is subjective; silhouette or BIC is better. But k is usually a business constraint — and then the real work is verifying the clusters are stable across seeds and interpretable enough to act on.

What’s being tested

Whether you will hide behind a metric or acknowledge that unsupervised problems are underdetermined. The stability-and-interpretability check is the senior move.

03Explain PCA. Why are the components orthogonal?PCAspectral theoremSVD＋
Strong answer

PCA finds a new orthogonal basis ordered by how much variance each direction captures. Standardize the features, compute the covariance matrix Σ = XᵀX/(n−1), and eigendecompose it — the eigenvectors are the principal components and each eigenvalue is the variance along its component, so ordering by eigenvalue gives the components by importance. Projecting onto the top k gives a k-dimensional representation.

The components are orthogonal because the covariance matrix is real and symmetric, and the spectral theorem guarantees a symmetric matrix has an orthogonal eigenbasis — so orthogonality is a mathematical consequence, not an imposed constraint, and it is why the resulting features are uncorrelated.

In practice PCA is computed via the SVD of the centred data matrix rather than by forming Σ explicitly, which is more numerically stable and handles d > n. It also has a dual characterization: the top-k components are simultaneously the directions of maximum variance and the k-dimensional subspace minimizing reconstruction error.

30-second version

Eigenvectors of the covariance matrix, ordered by eigenvalue. They are orthogonal because covariance is real and symmetric, and the spectral theorem guarantees a symmetric matrix has an orthogonal eigenbasis.

What’s being tested

Whether you know the linear algebra. Citing the spectral theorem for orthogonality, and knowing the SVD route, are the two markers of a strong answer.

Likely follow-up: “How many components do you keep?” — depends on the goal: enough for a target cumulative explained variance (90–95% conventionally) for compression; two or three for visualization; or, if PCA feeds a supervised model, treat the component count as a hyperparameter and cross-validate it against downstream performance rather than against explained variance.
04Should you always apply PCA before modelling?PCAvariance vs importancetrap＋
Strong answer

No, and it is often actively harmful. PCA is unsupervised, so it selects directions by variance with no knowledge of your target — and variance is not importance. The highest-variance direction can be one along which your classes are perfectly mixed, while a low-variance direction carries all the discriminative signal, so PCA can discard exactly what you needed.

Other costs: components are linear combinations of every original feature, so you lose interpretability and cannot drop any source column from your production pipeline; it assumes linear structure; it requires standardization; and for tree ensembles it is usually counterproductive, since trees handle irrelevant features well and PCA’s rotated axes destroy the axis-aligned structure that makes splits effective.

PCA earns its place for visualization, when features are severely collinear and you are using a linear model, when you need compression for memory or latency, for noise reduction by discarding low-variance components, or when d ≫ n. If you want a supervised projection, use LDA or partial least squares instead.

30-second version

No. PCA is unsupervised, so the top-variance direction can be one where classes are completely mixed. It also destroys interpretability and the axis-aligned structure trees rely on.

What’s being tested

Resistance to applying a standard step reflexively. The “variance is not importance” and “bad for trees” points are the two that land.

05K-Means vs DBSCAN vs GMM. When would you use each?clusteringcomparisonHDBSCAN＋
Strong answer

K-Means when clusters are roughly spherical and similarly sized, k is known or business-determined, and you need speed — it is O(nkd) per iteration and scales to very large data.

DBSCAN, or preferably HDBSCAN, when clusters have arbitrary shapes, you do not know k, and you want outliers explicitly identified rather than forced into a cluster — it finds density-connected regions and labels the rest as noise, which is uniquely useful for anomaly-adjacent problems. Its weaknesses are sensitivity to eps and trouble with clusters of differing densities, which is exactly what HDBSCAN improves by working across density levels.

GMM when clusters are elliptical rather than spherical, or when you want soft assignments — a probability of belonging to each cluster rather than a hard label, valuable when membership is genuinely ambiguous. Fitted by EM, it also gives a generative model you can sample from and score new points under, and BIC/AIC give a principled way to choose the number of components.

A useful connection: K-Means is the limiting case of a GMM with shared spherical covariance as the variance goes to zero and assignments become hard — so GMM is the strictly more general model, at the cost of more parameters and more data to fit them.

30-second version

K-Means for spherical, similar-sized clusters at scale. DBSCAN or HDBSCAN for arbitrary shapes, unknown k, and explicit outliers. GMM for elliptical clusters and soft assignments — and K-Means is its degenerate limit.

What’s being tested

Whether you can map data geometry to algorithm choice, and whether you see the K-Means/GMM relationship. Naming HDBSCAN shows currency.

06Your t-SNE plot shows two clusters far apart. What can you conclude?t-SNEvisualizationtrap＋
Strong answer

Very little from the distance. t-SNE optimizes preservation of local neighbourhood structure and explicitly does not preserve global geometry, so the distance between clusters is not meaningful — two clusters appearing far apart may be adjacent in the original space, and vice versa. Nor are the apparent cluster sizes meaningful, since t-SNE expands dense regions and contracts sparse ones.

Additional caveats: it is stochastic, so different seeds give visibly different plots; it is very sensitive to perplexity, and at some settings it will manufacture apparent clusters in pure random data; and it has no transform method for new points, since it optimizes an embedding of the specific input set rather than learning a mapping.

What I can conclude is that points within each apparent group have similar local neighbourhoods — a hypothesis worth testing. To validate it I would check whether the groups differ on interpretable features, run a proper clustering algorithm in the original space, and confirm stability across seeds and hyperparameters. If I needed a projection preserving more global structure and able to transform new data, I would use UMAP — though it shares the “do not over-read the geometry” caveat.

30-second version

Almost nothing from the distance — t-SNE preserves local neighbourhoods, not global geometry, so between-cluster distances and apparent sizes are meaningless. Treat the grouping as a hypothesis and validate it in the original space.

What’s being tested

Whether you over-interpret visualizations. A deliberate trap, and one of the most common misuses in real data-science work.
