Module 10 · Part C Evaluation

Classification Metrics & the Confusion Matrix

If you master one module beyond logistic regression, make it this one. Interviewers use metric questions to test business judgment, and a wrong metric invalidates an otherwise good project.

Prerequisites · Modules 03, 05, 07Study time · 10 h9 interview questionsHighest priority
🎯
Your interview goal

Draw the confusion matrix from memory, never confuse precision with recall, know exactly when ROC-AUC lies, and derive the optimal threshold from a stated cost matrix.

1 · From the ground up

🧩
In plain words

A classifier can be right in two ways and wrong in two ways. Those four counts are the whole story. Every metric you have heard of is just two of those counts in a fraction. Which fraction you should care about depends on which mistake hurts your business more. And the model does not really output a class — it outputs a score. The cutoff that turns that score into a yes or no is a dial you control.

Start with the problem, not the formula

You have 1,000 emails. Sixty of them are spam. Now build the laziest possible classifier: say "not spam" every time. It looks at nothing. It scores 94% accuracy.

Now build a real model. It catches 40 of the 60 spam emails, and wrongly flags 10 good ones. Accuracy: 97%. Only three points better than the model that does nothing.

So what: accuracy counts every row equally, so the bigger class decides the score. When one class is rare — which is true for almost anything worth detecting — accuracy stops telling you anything useful.

The four numbers everything is built from

Sort your predictions by two things: what you said, and whether you were right. That gives four counts. The names look confusing but follow a simple rule — the second word is what you predicted, the first word says whether that was correct.

  • True Positive — you said positive, and it was. A catch.
  • False Positive — you said positive, but it was not. A false alarm.
  • False Negative — you said negative, but it was positive. A miss.
  • True Negative — you said negative, and it was.
So what: draw this 2×2 grid before you answer any metric question. Every formula below is two of these cells in a fraction. People who rebuild the formula from the grid never mix up precision and recall. People who memorised the formulas often do.

Precision and recall differ only in what you divide by

Both have TP on top. The only difference is the bottom of the fraction — and that is what makes them answer different questions.

Precision = TP / (TP + FP). You divide by everything you flagged. So it asks: when I raise an alarm, how often am I right?

Recall = TP / (TP + FN). You divide by everything that was actually positive. So it asks: of all the real cases out there, how many did I find?

So what: remember it as "precision divides by what I predicted, recall divides by what was true". Once you can say that, you cannot swap them. It also shows why neither one is better — they answer different questions.
Same four cells — precision reads a column, recall reads a row

Look at the two rings and the confusion goes away for good: precision's ring stands up, recall's ring lies flat. Also watch what accuracy does. It moves from 0.940 to 0.970 — a change too small to notice on a dashboard — while the thing you actually wanted went from zero to two-thirds.

The cutoff is yours, not the model's

A classifier does not hand you a class. It hands you a score, ideally a probability. Something then compares that score to a cutoff and calls it yes or no. That cutoff of 0.5 is just a habit. It is rarely the right business choice.

Lower the cutoff and you catch more real cases, but you also raise more false alarms: recall goes up, precision goes down. Raise it and the opposite happens. The model never changes. You are only choosing how much proof you want before you act.

So what: precision and recall do not belong to a model. They belong to a model plus a cutoff. Quoting them without saying the cutoff is half an answer. And "my precision is too low" is often a cutoff problem, not a model problem.

positivesnegativesthe cutoff you chose

Precision swings from 0.29 to 0.89. Recall swings from 0.95 to 0.52. And the model never changed. The two classes are drawn on separate scales because there are 940 of one and 60 of the other — and that gap is exactly why the false alarms pile up so fast at a low cutoff.

F1 is a choice, and its default is an assumption

F1 combines precision and recall into one number: 2PR / (P + R). It uses a harmonic mean, not a plain average, so a lopsided pair cannot hide. Precision 1.0 with recall 0.02 gives a plain average of 0.51 — but an F1 of 0.039. It demands that both be decent.

But look at what F1 quietly assumes: that precision and recall matter equally. That is a claim about your business, and it is usually wrong. Fβ exists for this. Use β > 1 when a miss is worse, β < 1 when a false alarm is worse.

So what: grabbing F1 by default is the same mistake as grabbing accuracy by default — just harder to spot. If you cannot say why a false alarm and a miss cost the same here, F1 is not a measurement. It is a guess wearing a formula.

Why ROC-AUC looks great when positives are rare

ROC plots recall against the false positive rate, FP / (FP + TN). Look at what is on the bottom: every negative in your data. When negatives hugely outnumber positives, that number is enormous — so even a flood of false alarms barely moves it.

Here is a concrete case. Ten positives, 9,990 negatives. Your model finds all ten and raises 100 false alarms. FPR is 100 / 9990 ≈ 1%, which draws a beautiful ROC curve. But precision is 10 / 110 ≈ 9%. Ninety-one out of every hundred alerts are wrong. The team handling those alerts will stop trusting the system within a week.

So what: the precision–recall curve uses precision, and precision divides by what you flagged — not by the whole haystack. So a big negative class cannot water it down. Rare positives → report PR-AUC. This is the most useful metric decision in the module.

Sorting well is not the same as being right about probabilities

ROC-AUC, PR-AUC and precision-at-k only care about order. A model that gives 0.9 to every positive and 0.8 to every negative sorts perfectly and scores an AUC of 1.0 — while every probability it reports is wrong.

Calibration is the separate question: does a score of 0.7 really mean 70% of those cases turn out positive? Check it with a reliability curve. Fix it with Platt scaling or isotonic regression, always fitted on data the model has not seen.

So what: if the score just sorts a work queue, good ordering is enough. If the number gets multiplied by something — an expected loss, a price, a dose — you need calibration, and AUC will not tell you whether you have it.

Where the cutoff should actually come from

Stop guessing it. Say a miss costs C_FN and a false alarm costs C_FP. Acting on a case with predicted probability p is worth it when acting costs less than not acting:

(1 − p) · C_FP < p · C_FN  →  p > C_FP / (C_FP + C_FN)

So the best cutoff is C_FP / (C_FP + C_FN). Notice it depends only on the ratio of the two costs. If a miss is nine times worse than a false alarm, the cutoff is 1/10 = 0.10 — nowhere near 0.5.

So what: this is the strongest answer to "how would you pick the threshold?". It also needs calibrated probabilities to work, which is why the last step matters. And it sums up the whole module: both the metric and the cutoff come from the cost of being wrong, not from the model.
Analogy — airport security. Turn the scanner up and you catch every real threat, but you also stop hundreds of people carrying nothing but keys. Recall is near perfect, precision is awful, and the queue reaches the car park. Turn it down and the queue disappears, along with your ability to find anything. Nobody sets that dial by looking at accuracy — almost everyone is harmless, so a scanner that is switched off scores nearly 100%. You set it by deciding what a miss costs compared to a pointless search. That is the cost ratio above, and it is why the decision belongs to people, not to the machine.

3 · Draw the matrix, always

In any metric question, draw it first. It anchors the discussion and prevents the precision/recall mix-up that ends interviews.

🧭
The mnemonic that survives interview pressure

Precision is a column — it divides by what you predicted positive. Recall is a row — it divides by what actually is positive.

4 · Core intuitions

Precision protects the user from false alarms; recall protects the business from misses

Every metric question reduces to: which error is more expensive here? Missing a cancer diagnosis (FN) is catastrophic; a false cancer alert (FP) costs an unnecessary biopsy — so recall dominates. Wrongly blocking a legitimate customer’s card (FP) causes churn; missing a small fraudulent charge (FN) costs the transaction value — so the balance depends on relative amounts, and the right answer is an expected-cost calculation, not a metric name.

The threshold, not the model, moves you along the trade-off

Lower the threshold and you flag more things: recall rises, precision falls. Raise it and the reverse. A single trained model gives you an entire curve of operating points, which is why threshold-free metrics (AUC) measure ranking quality while threshold-dependent metrics (precision, recall, F1) measure a specific operating point. This distinction is the backbone of the module.

ROC-AUC has a clean probabilistic meaning

It equals the probability that a randomly chosen positive is ranked above a randomly chosen negative. 0.5 is random, 1.0 is perfect ranking, and below 0.5 means your scores are inverted. It is threshold-free and invariant to class balance — that last property is both its strength and its trap.

Why ROC-AUC misleads under severe imbalance

ROC plots TPR against FPR, and FPR has the large negative count in its denominator. With 1,000,000 negatives and 1,000 positives:

False positivesFPRPrecision
1,0000.0010.50
11,0000.0110.08

That FPR shift from 0.001 to 0.011 is visually negligible on the ROC curve — while precision has collapsed from 0.50 to about 0.08 and your alert queue has become 92% junk. The PR curve, whose precision axis has the predicted-positive count in its denominator, shows it immediately. Hence: for rare-positive problems, use PR-AUC / average precision. Note also that the PR baseline is the positive rate — 0.001 here, not 0.5 — so a PR-AUC of 0.15 can be an excellent model.

F1 is a default, not a principle

It is the harmonic mean of precision and recall, so it punishes a large imbalance between them — you cannot get a good F1 by sacrificing one entirely. But it implicitly asserts precision and recall matter equally, which is rarely true. If recall is twice as important use F2; if precision matters more, F0.5. If you can quantify costs at all, skip F-beta and minimize expected cost directly. F1 also ignores TN entirely — usually appropriate for rare-positive problems, inappropriate when both classes matter.

Discrimination and calibration are different things

A model outputting p/1000 for every case has identical ranking to one outputting p — the same AUC — but is uselessly miscalibrated. If a human or a rule consumes the number, you need calibration; if only the order matters, you need discrimination. Say which one the application needs.

MCC is the most honest single number for a binary confusion matrix

It uses all four cells, returns −1 to +1 with 0 as random, and unlike F1 it cannot be inflated by a degenerate predict-everything-positive strategy on imbalanced data. It is underused, and naming it scores well.

5 · Whiteboard formulas

classification_metrics.txt
Accuracy          = (TP+TN) / (TP+TN+FP+FN)
Precision (PPV)   = TP / (TP+FP)
Recall/TPR/Sens.  = TP / (TP+FN)
Specificity/TNR   = TN / (TN+FP)
FPR               = FP / (FP+TN)  = 1 - specificity
NPV               = TN / (TN+FN)

F1                = 2·P·R / (P+R)                  (harmonic mean)
F-beta            = (1+b²)·P·R / (b²·P + R)        b>1 favours recall
Balanced accuracy = (Recall + Specificity)/2

MCC = (TP·TN - FP·FN) / sqrt((TP+FP)(TP+FN)(TN+FP)(TN+FN))     in [-1, 1]

Log-loss  = -(1/n) S [ y log p + (1-y) log(1-p) ]
Brier     =  (1/n) S (p - y)²

ROC    : TPR (y) vs FPR (x), swept over thresholds. Baseline = diagonal.
         AUC = P(score of random positive > score of random negative)
PR     : Precision (y) vs Recall (x). Baseline = POSITIVE CLASS RATE.

Expected cost at threshold t:
   E[cost](t) = FP(t)·C_FP + FN(t)·C_FN        <- minimize this over t
Optimal threshold for a CALIBRATED model:
   t* = C_FP / (C_FP + C_FN)
🧭
Memorize that last line

If a false positive costs £10 and a false negative costs £190, then t* = 10/200 = 0.05 — flag anything above a 5% predicted probability. Producing that formula in an applied round is a standout moment.

7 · Hands-on drill

  1. Compute every metric above from a raw confusion matrix by hand, on paper, for TP=40, FP=60, FN=10, TN=9890. Then reproduce them with sklearn and check yourself.
  2. On an imbalanced dataset, plot the ROC curve and the PR curve side by side for the same model. Then add 10× more negatives and re-plot both. The ROC curve will barely move; the PR curve will collapse. This single experiment is the best possible preparation for the ROC-versus-PR question.
  3. Sweep the threshold from 0 to 1 and plot precision, recall and F1 against it. Locate the F1-maximal threshold and note how far it is from 0.5.
  4. Define a cost matrix (C_FP = 10, C_FN = 190), compute expected cost across thresholds, find the minimizing threshold empirically, and confirm it lands near the C_FP/(C_FP+C_FN) prediction for a calibrated model.
  5. Plot reliability diagrams for logistic regression, an SVM decision function, and untuned XGBoost. Then apply Platt scaling and isotonic regression to the latter two and re-plot.
⚠️
Gotchas that fail candidates
  • Reporting accuracy on an imbalanced problem without flagging it. An instant negative signal.
  • Mixing up precision and recall under pressure. Use the row/column mnemonic.
  • Defaulting to ROC-AUC for a 0.1%-positive problem.
  • Reporting F1 without saying which class it is for, or macro/micro without saying which.
  • Not knowing that AUC is threshold-free and therefore says nothing about your operating point.
  • Tuning the threshold on the test set. Tune it on validation.
  • Confusing calibration with accuracy.
  • Forgetting the PR baseline is not 0.5. A PR-AUC of 0.3 on a 1%-positive problem is 30× baseline.

8 · Interview questions

9 questions
01Draw the confusion matrix and define precision and recall.fundamentalsconfusion matrix＋
Strong answer

Draw a 2×2 with actual on rows and predicted on columns: TP and FN on the positive-actual row, FP and TN on the negative-actual row.

Precision = TP/(TP+FP) — of everything I predicted positive, what fraction really was. It is computed down the predicted-positive column and answers “can I trust my alerts?” Recall = TP/(TP+FN) — of all the actual positives, what fraction I caught. It is computed across the actual-positive row and answers “am I missing cases?”

They trade off through the threshold: lowering it flags more, raising recall and lowering precision.

30-second version

Precision is TP/(TP+FP), down the predicted-positive column — can I trust my alerts. Recall is TP/(TP+FN), across the actual-positive row — am I missing cases. The threshold trades one for the other.

What’s being tested

Absolute fundamentals. Any hesitation here is costly — practise until it is reflexive, and always draw the matrix.

Likely follow-up: “What is specificity, and where is it used?” — TN/(TN+FP), recall for the negative class. It is the standard companion to sensitivity in medical testing, and 1 − specificity is the FPR forming the ROC curve’s x-axis.
02Your fraud model has 99.9% accuracy. Are you happy?accuracy paradoximbalancetrap＋
Strong answer

No, and I would be suspicious. If fraud is 0.1% of transactions, a model predicting “never fraud” for everything achieves exactly 99.9% accuracy while catching zero fraud — so the number is entirely consistent with a completely useless model. That is the accuracy paradox: on imbalanced data, accuracy is dominated by the majority class and carries almost no information.

What I would ask for instead: the full confusion matrix; recall, for what fraction of fraud we catch; precision, since a human team has to work the alert queue; PR-AUC as the threshold-free summary; and the expected cost given the real cost of a missed fraud versus a false decline. Then I would set the threshold by minimizing that expected cost rather than defaulting to 0.5.

30-second version

No — predicting “never fraud” scores 99.9% on a 0.1% base rate while catching nothing. I would want the confusion matrix, recall, precision, PR-AUC and a cost-tuned threshold.

What’s being tested

Whether you spot the trap immediately. Probably the single most frequently asked ML interview question of the last decade, and still standard in 2026 — the expected-cost extension is what makes the answer strong rather than merely correct.

03ROC-AUC vs PR-AUC. When must you use PR?ROCPR curveimbalancetrap＋
Strong answer

Use PR when the positive class is rare and you care about the quality of your positive predictions. The reason is in the denominators: FPR is FP/(FP+TN), so with an enormous negative count even a large absolute increase in false positives barely moves FPR — the ROC curve stays high and looks great. Precision is TP/(TP+FP), whose denominator is the predicted-positive set, so the same increase visibly destroys it.

Concretely with 1,000 positives and 1,000,000 negatives: going from 1,000 to 11,000 false positives shifts FPR from 0.001 to 0.011 — invisible on ROC — while precision falls from 0.50 to 0.08.

Two additional points: ROC-AUC is invariant to class balance, which makes it good for comparing models across datasets with different base rates but bad for judging real-world usability; and the PR curve’s baseline is the positive rate, not 0.5, so PR-AUC must be read relative to that base rate.

30-second version

When positives are rare. FPR’s huge negative denominator hides false-positive growth, while precision’s predicted-positive denominator exposes it — 1,000 to 11,000 FPs moves FPR by 0.01 but drops precision from 0.50 to 0.08.

What’s being tested

Whether you can explain the mechanism with numbers rather than just state the rule. The worked example is what makes this answer memorable.

04How do you choose the classification threshold?thresholdexpected costapplied＋
Strong answer

From business cost, not from the model. If the costs of a false positive and false negative are quantifiable, minimize expected cost E[cost](t) = FP(t)·C_FP + FN(t)·C_FN, swept over t on a validation set. For a well-calibrated model this has a clean closed form: t* = C_FP/(C_FP + C_FN) — so if a false negative is 19× as expensive as a false positive, the optimal threshold is 0.05, not 0.5.

If costs are not quantifiable, use an operational constraint instead: fix the alert volume your review team can handle and take the top-k by score, or fix a minimum acceptable precision and maximize recall subject to it.

Two disciplines matter: tune the threshold on validation data, never on test; and re-check it after deployment, because the optimal threshold shifts when the base rate drifts.

30-second version

From cost, not the model. Minimize FP·C_FP + FN·C_FN on validation; for a calibrated model that is t* = C_FP/(C_FP+C_FN). Absent costs, fix the reviewable alert volume and take the top-k.

What’s being tested

Whether you understand that 0.5 is an arbitrary default. The closed-form threshold and the “re-check after drift” point are both strong differentiators.

Likely follow-up: “What if the model is not calibrated?” — the closed form does not apply; calibrate first with Platt or isotonic on held-out data, or just find the empirical cost-minimizing threshold by direct sweep, which works regardless of calibration.
05Why is F1 the harmonic mean rather than the arithmetic mean?F1metric design＋
Strong answer

Because the harmonic mean is dominated by the smaller value, which is exactly the behaviour you want. Take precision 1.0 and recall 0.01 — a model that flags one thing, correctly, and misses 99% of positives. The arithmetic mean is 0.505, which flatters a useless model. The harmonic mean is 2(1×0.01)/1.01 ≈ 0.02, correctly near-zero.

So F1 can only be high when both precision and recall are decent, preventing you from gaming it by collapsing one. Two caveats: F1 assumes they matter equally, so use F-beta when they do not; and F1 ignores TN entirely, which is appropriate for rare-positive detection and inappropriate when correctly identifying negatives has value.

30-second version

Because the harmonic mean is dominated by the smaller value. Precision 1.0 with recall 0.01 gives an arithmetic mean of 0.505 but an F1 of 0.02 — so you cannot game it by collapsing one side.

What’s being tested

Whether you understand why a metric is constructed the way it is. The numeric example is the fastest way to demonstrate it.

06Pick a metric for: cancer screening, spam filtering, credit default, and a recommendation feed.appliedmetric selectionranking＋
Strong answer

Cancer screening — recall dominates, since a missed cancer is potentially fatal while a false positive costs a follow-up test. Maximize recall subject to a precision floor keeping the follow-up burden feasible; report sensitivity at a fixed specificity, which is the convention clinicians use.

Spam filtering — precision dominates, because a legitimate email sent to spam is far worse for the user than one spam message reaching the inbox. Maximize recall subject to a very high precision constraint, and consider a three-way outcome (inbox / quarantine / spam) rather than a binary decision.

Credit default — costs are directly monetizable, so use expected cost with the actual loss-given-default versus the foregone-interest cost of a wrongly declined applicant. Require calibrated probabilities, because the score feeds pricing and capital calculations, plus interpretability for regulatory reasons.

Recommendation feed — this is a ranking problem, not a classification one, so use top-k metrics: precision@k, NDCG, MAP — because users only see the top few — and validate against an online engagement metric via A/B test, since offline ranking metrics correlate imperfectly with real behaviour.

30-second version

Screening: recall with a precision floor. Spam: precision with a recall floor. Credit: expected cost plus calibration. Feed: it is ranking, so precision@k and NDCG, validated by A/B test.

What’s being tested

Whether you can map business context to metric choice fluently. Recognizing the feed as ranking rather than classification is a specific differentiator.

07What is calibration, how do you measure it, and how do you fix it?calibrationPlattisotonic＋
Strong answer

Calibrated means predicted probabilities match observed frequencies — among cases scored 0.7, about 70% are positive. It is distinct from discrimination: a model can rank perfectly (AUC 1.0) and still be badly calibrated, and vice versa.

Measure with a reliability diagram (bin predictions, plot mean predicted probability against observed positive rate; the diagonal is perfect), expected calibration error (the average bin-wise gap), or the Brier score (which combines calibration and discrimination).

Fix with a post-hoc mapping fitted on held-out data. Platt scaling fits a one-dimensional logistic regression on the model’s scores — parametric, works with little data, assumes a sigmoid-shaped distortion. Isotonic regression fits a non-parametric monotone mapping — more flexible, corrects any monotone distortion, needs more data and can overfit small sets.

Which models need it: SVMs badly, since hinge loss does not estimate probabilities at all; boosted trees usually, since they tend toward over-confidence; Random Forests mildly, typically under-confident at the extremes because of averaging; logistic regression usually not, since it is trained on a proper scoring rule.

30-second version

Predicted probabilities matching observed frequencies — distinct from ranking ability. Measure with a reliability diagram, ECE or Brier; fix with Platt or isotonic regression on held-out data.

What’s being tested

A topic much more prominent in 2026 loops because scores increasingly drive automated cost-based decisions. Knowing which models are miscalibrated in which direction is a strong practitioner signal.

08Macro vs micro vs weighted averaging in multi-class problems.multiclassaveraging＋
Strong answer

Macro computes the metric per class and takes an unweighted mean, so every class counts equally regardless of size — use it when rare classes matter as much as common ones, which is usually the case when evaluating fairness across categories.

Micro pools all TP/FP/FN across classes and computes one global metric, so it is dominated by frequent classes; for single-label multi-class classification, micro-F1 equals overall accuracy.

Weighted averages the per-class metric weighted by class support — a middle ground that reflects the population but can still hide poor performance on rare classes.

Rule of thumb: report macro when class importance is uniform, micro or accuracy when you care about overall correct-decision rate, and always report the per-class breakdown alongside, because a single averaged number can conceal one class sitting at zero recall.

30-second version

Macro weights classes equally; micro pools counts and equals accuracy for single-label multi-class; weighted uses support. Always show the per-class breakdown too.

What’s being tested

Precision on a routinely fudged topic. The “micro-F1 equals accuracy” identity is a good detail.

09What is MCC and why might you prefer it?MCCimbalancebreadth＋
Strong answer

The Matthews correlation coefficient is essentially the Pearson correlation between predicted and actual binary labels: (TP·TN − FP·FN)/√((TP+FP)(TP+FN)(TN+FP)(TN+FN)), ranging from −1 (perfectly wrong) through 0 (random) to +1 (perfect).

Its advantage is that it uses all four cells of the confusion matrix, so unlike F1 — which ignores TN — it cannot be inflated by a degenerate strategy on imbalanced data. A model predicting all-positive on a 1%-positive dataset gets a non-trivial F1 but an MCC of approximately 0.

It is therefore the most trustworthy single-number summary for imbalanced binary problems, and it is symmetric under swapping which class you call positive — which F1 is not.

30-second version

The correlation between predicted and actual labels, from −1 to +1. It uses all four confusion-matrix cells, so unlike F1 it cannot be inflated by predicting everything positive on imbalanced data.

What’s being tested

Breadth beyond the standard four metrics. Naming MCC unprompted, with the “uses all four cells” justification, is a clear positive signal.
