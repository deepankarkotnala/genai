Interpretability & Explainability

Rising sharply in interviews — regulated domains and the EU AI Act made this a compliance topic rather than a nice-to-have. The single most important caveat: explanations describe the model, not the world.

Prerequisites · Modules 06, 07, 16, 18Study time · 6 h5 interview questionsRising fast
🎯
Your interview goal

Explain SHAP's additivity and its non-causality, distinguish global from local explanations when a regulated decision is at stake, and reframe “make the model fair” as the ill-posed request it is.

1 · From the ground up

🧩
In plain words

Two questions get muddled together here, and they need different tools. "Why did the model decide this about this one person?" is a local question. "What does the model rely on in general?" is a global one. On top of that sits a warning that applies to every technique in this module: these tools tell you what the model does. They do not tell you what is true about the world. Feed a model correlations and it will explain itself in correlations, very convincingly.

Start with the problem, not the formula

A loan application is declined. The customer asks why. You cannot say "the gradient boosting model returned 0.31" — that is not an explanation, it is a number. In many countries it is also not legally sufficient.

So you need to turn a model's output into a sentence a person can act on: "your application was declined mainly because of your existing debt level relative to income." Getting from the first thing to the second is the whole subject.

So what: interpretability is not an academic nicety bolted on at the end. In credit, hiring, insurance and healthcare, an unexplainable model is often an unshippable model — which is exactly why a simpler model that can be explained sometimes beats a stronger one that cannot.

Sort out which question you are answering

Almost all confusion in this area comes from mixing the two up.

  • Global — "what does this model use, overall?" Answered by feature importance, permutation importance, and partial dependence. Useful for sanity-checking the model and for talking to stakeholders.
  • Local — "why this specific prediction?" Answered by SHAP, LIME and counterfactuals. This is what the declined customer needs.
So what: a global answer cannot serve a local need. "Income is the most important feature overall" tells the declined customer nothing about their own case — their decision may have turned entirely on something that ranks fifth globally.

The built-in importance number is not trustworthy

Every tree model gives you feature_importances_ for free, and it is measured by how much each feature reduced impurity across all splits. It has two biases that matter.

First, it favours features with many distinct values. A continuous feature offers hundreds of possible thresholds, so it gets hundreds of chances to look good; a yes/no feature gets one. Customer ID would score well and mean nothing.

Second, when two features say almost the same thing, the tree picks one arbitrarily at each split and the credit gets split unevenly between them.

So what: prefer permutation importance — shuffle one column, see how much the score gets worse, put it back. It measures what you actually care about (does the model need this?) and works for any model. But it has its own trap: with two correlated features, shuffling one leaves the other intact, so both can look unimportant. No importance measure survives correlated features cleanly.

SHAP: splitting the credit fairly

SHAP borrows an old idea from game theory. A team scores ten goals — how much did each player contribute? The fair answer is: for each player, measure how much the score improves when they join the team, averaged across every possible order in which the team could have been assembled.

Swap players for features and goals for a prediction, and that is SHAP. It starts from the average prediction across your whole dataset and hands out the difference between that average and this row's prediction, one feature at a time.

So what: the contributions add up exactly to the gap between the average prediction and this one. Nothing is left over and nothing is double-counted, and that property — not the plots — is what makes SHAP trustworthy enough to show a regulator.

This is the shape of an answer you can give a customer: "you started at our average; your income was the strongest point in your favour, and your existing debt was the biggest thing working against it." And because the numbers close exactly, you can prove no hidden fifth factor is doing the real work.

What SHAP does not tell you — and this is the important one

SHAP explains the model. It says nothing about cause and effect in the world. If postcode happens to correlate with income and the model leaned on postcode, SHAP will faithfully report postcode as the driver. That report is correct about the model and tells you nothing about whether postcode causes anything.

So this sentence is wrong: "SHAP shows that raising this customer's income by 10% would improve their score by 0.05." That is a claim about an intervention, and you built a correlational tool.

So what: the honest phrasing is "the model's output is most influenced by X", never "X causes Y". A SHAP plot that shows the model relying on something absurd is a genuinely useful finding — use it to audit the model, not to make policy about the world.

Partial dependence averages, and averages hide things

A partial dependence plot sweeps one feature across its range, re-predicts every row at each value, and averages. The result is one clean curve showing that feature's effect.

The clean curve is the problem. Averaging destroys anything that goes in opposite directions for different groups. If price rises demand for one segment and kills it for another, the average is flat — and you conclude price does not matter.

So what: plot the individual curves too — that is an ICE plot. If the individual lines fan out in different directions, the average curve is a lie, and you have discovered an interaction worth modelling explicitly.
Two groups, opposite effects — and a partial dependence plot showing nothing
group Agroup Bwhat the PDP shows you

The arithmetic is exact: two equal-sized groups moving by the same amount in opposite directions average to a horizontal line. The PDP is not broken — it is answering "what happens on average", and the answer really is "nothing". The question you wanted was "what happens to whom", and only the individual curves can answer that.

Better than explaining afterwards: constrain it upfront

All of the above describes a model after it is built. There is a stronger option: build the requirement in from the start.

Monotonic constraints let you tell XGBoost or LightGBM "risk may never go down as debt goes up". The model then cannot learn a wiggle where more debt looks safer — no matter what quirk in the training data suggested it.

So what: this is worth more than any explanation, because it is a guarantee rather than a description. A SHAP plot says "the model happened to behave sensibly on the rows I checked". A monotonic constraint says "the model cannot behave otherwise, on any row, ever" — and that is what a regulator or a risk committee actually wants to hear.

Fairness definitions contradict each other, provably

Three reasonable-sounding definitions of a fair model:

  • Equal approval rates — each group gets approved at the same rate.
  • Equal error rates — each group suffers false positives and false negatives at the same rate.
  • Equally calibrated — a score of 0.7 means 70% within every group.

All three sound like things a fair system should do. It has been mathematically proven that whenever the underlying rates genuinely differ between groups, you cannot satisfy all three at once. Not with a better model, not with more data. It is an impossibility result.

So what: so "is the model fair?" has no technical answer. You must choose which definition applies to your situation and be able to defend the choice — and knowing that the trade-off is unavoidable, rather than a gap in your effort, is what makes that conversation honest.
Analogy — a doctor tells you your test result is abnormal. "The machine printed 0.31" is useless to you. "Your cholesterol is the main driver, and your exercise level partly offsets it" is something you can act on — that is a local explanation. "Across all our patients, cholesterol matters most" is a global one, and no comfort if your own result turned on something else entirely. And the causal warning is in the analogy too: if the clinic happens to serve one neighbourhood, the machine may have learned that postcode predicts illness. It would be reporting that honestly, and moving house would not make you healthier.

2 · What to learn

  1. Interpretability versus explainability; global versus local; intrinsic versus post-hoc.
  2. Intrinsically interpretable models: linear and logistic coefficients, shallow trees, rule lists, GAMs and EBMs.
  3. Permutation importance — the mechanism, and its correlated-feature caveat.
  4. SHAP: Shapley values from cooperative game theory, the additivity property, TreeSHAP, and the standard plots (beeswarm, waterfall, dependence).
  5. LIME: local surrogate models, and why it is less stable than SHAP.
  6. Partial dependence plots and ICE plots; how PDPs mislead under correlation and interactions.
  7. Counterfactual and contrastive explanations — “what would need to change?” — and why they are often the most actionable.
  8. Correlation versus causation in explanations — the single most important caveat.
  9. Monotonic constraints as a way to buy interpretability structurally.
  10. Fairness and bias: group fairness definitions (demographic parity, equal opportunity, equalized odds), their mathematical incompatibility, and disparate-impact auditing.
  11. Documentation practice: model cards, datasheets, and what regulated deployment requires.
  12. The accuracy–interpretability trade-off, and why it is smaller than people assume.

3 · Core intuitions

Distinguish global from local

Global explanations describe the model’s overall behaviour — “income is the strongest driver, and risk rises monotonically as it falls”. Local explanations describe one prediction — “this application was declined mainly because of a 14-month credit history and three recent enquiries”. Regulated decisions usually require local explanations, because the affected individual is entitled to know why their case was decided that way. Feature importance is global; SHAP gives both.

SHAP computes each feature’s fair share of the prediction

Shapley values come from cooperative game theory: they allocate a payout among players by averaging each player’s marginal contribution across all possible orderings in which players could join the coalition. Applied to ML, the “payout” is the difference between this prediction and the average prediction, and the “players” are the features.

This gives SHAP its key additivity property: the SHAP values for one prediction sum exactly to prediction − baseline, so the explanation is complete and consistent, with no contribution unaccounted for. Exact computation is exponential in features, but TreeSHAP computes it in polynomial time for tree ensembles — which is why SHAP is practical for exactly the models (XGBoost, LightGBM, Random Forest) that most need explaining.

Permutation importance measures what the model uses to generalize

Shuffle one feature’s values in a held-out set, breaking its relationship with the target, and measure the drop in performance. A large drop means the model relied on it. Advantages over impurity-based importance: model-agnostic, computed on held-out data so it reflects generalization rather than fit, and no high-cardinality bias. Its main caveat is shared with most methods: with two correlated features, shuffling one leaves the other available as a substitute, so both look unimportant even though the information is critical.

PDPs show the average marginal effect — and “average” is the operative word

A partial dependence plot varies one feature across its range while marginalizing the others, showing the average predicted response. Two ways it misleads: if the feature is correlated with others, the plot evaluates the model at combinations that never occur in real data, so the curve is partly fiction; and if the feature’s effect is heterogeneous — increasing for one subgroup, decreasing for another — the average can be flat and hide both. ICE plots solve the second problem by drawing one line per instance instead of the average, and disagreeing line shapes are a direct visual signal of interaction.

The most important caveat: explanations are about the model, not the world

SHAP tells you what drove the model’s prediction. It does not tell you what causes the outcome. If ZIP code is highly predictive of default, SHAP will show ZIP code as important — but ZIP code does not cause default, it proxies for unmeasured socioeconomic factors and possibly encodes protected characteristics.

Acting on an explanation as if it were causal is how organizations make expensive mistakes: “SHAP says support calls predict churn, so let us reduce support calls” inverts the causality entirely. State this limitation whenever you present an explanation — it is a strong signal of maturity, and it is the answer interviewers most often reward in this module.

Group fairness definitions are mathematically incompatible — a theorem, not an inconvenience

Demographic parity requires equal positive-prediction rates across groups; equal opportunity requires equal true-positive rates; predictive parity requires equal precision. Except in degenerate cases — equal base rates, or a perfect classifier — you cannot satisfy all of them at once; this is the impossibility result that emerged from the COMPAS debate. So “make the model fair” is not a well-posed technical request. The correct response is to ask which fairness criterion the context requires and to be explicit about what is being traded away.

Monotonic constraints buy interpretability structurally

XGBoost, LightGBM and CatBoost all let you constrain a feature’s effect to be monotonically increasing or decreasing. That encodes domain knowledge — “higher income must never increase predicted risk” — makes the model’s behaviour defensible to a regulator, often costs little accuracy, and can even improve generalization by ruling out noise-driven non-monotonicity. It is an underused technique and naming it is a good differentiator.

5 · Hands-on drill

  1. Train XGBoost on a tabular dataset. Produce MDI importance, permutation importance and SHAP summary plots. Compare the rankings and explain every disagreement you find.
  2. Add a random high-cardinality ID column and re-run all three. MDI will rank it high; the others will not. That contrast is your evidence for the feature-importance question.
  3. Generate SHAP waterfall plots for three individual predictions — one confident positive, one confident negative, one borderline — and write the one-paragraph explanation you would give a customer.
  4. Produce a PDP and the corresponding ICE plot for a feature with a known interaction. Observe how the PDP flattens what the ICE plot reveals.
  5. Duplicate an important feature (perfect correlation) and re-run permutation importance. Watch both copies drop to near zero — that result is the correlated-feature caveat made concrete.
  6. Fit the same model with and without a monotonic constraint on one feature; compare accuracy and plot both response curves.
⚠️
Gotchas that fail candidates
  • Treating feature importance or SHAP as causal. The defining error of this module.
  • Using feature_importances_ without mentioning its biases.
  • Not distinguishing global from local when the question implies a regulated individual decision.
  • Saying SHAP is “just feature importance”. It is per-prediction, signed, and additive.
  • Reading a flat PDP as “no effect” when heterogeneous effects may be cancelling.
  • Claiming a model can satisfy all fairness definitions.
  • Claiming interpretable models are always much less accurate. On tabular data, GAMs/EBMs and well-featured logistic regression are often close.

6 · Interview questions

5 questions
01What is SHAP and why is it preferred over plain feature importance?SHAPShapleyadditivity＋
Strong answer

SHAP assigns each feature a contribution to an individual prediction, based on Shapley values from cooperative game theory: a feature’s value is its average marginal contribution to the prediction across all possible orderings in which features could be added.

Three properties make it valuable. Additivity / local accuracy — for any single prediction the SHAP values sum exactly to prediction − baseline, so the explanation is complete with nothing unattributed. Locality — it explains each prediction individually, whereas conventional feature importance gives one global number; this matters enormously for regulated decisions, where the affected individual needs to know why their case went that way. Consistency — it satisfies formal axioms (a feature that always contributes more never receives a lower attribution), unlike impurity-based importance, which is biased toward high-cardinality features and computed on training data.

SHAP values are also signed, so you see direction as well as magnitude, and they aggregate upward into a global importance ranking — giving both views from one computation. Exact Shapley computation is exponential, but TreeSHAP makes it polynomial for tree ensembles.

The caveat I would always state: SHAP explains the model, not the world — the attributions are not causal effects.

30-second version

Shapley values allocating each feature its average marginal contribution. Additive (they sum exactly to prediction minus baseline), local, signed and axiomatically consistent — with TreeSHAP making it tractable for tree ensembles. But not causal.

What’s being tested

Whether you know the theory behind SHAP and its limits. Additivity, locality and the non-causality caveat are the three things to land.

02Your SHAP analysis shows number of support calls is the top driver of churn. The business wants to cut support calls. What do you say?causationjudgmentexperimenttrap＋
Strong answer

I would stop that decision, because it inverts the causality. SHAP tells me the model uses support-call count to predict churn — it does not tell me support calls cause churn. The far more plausible causal story is that customers already having problems both call support and churn, so call volume is a symptom and a leading indicator, not a cause.

Reducing calls by making support harder to reach would remove the signal while leaving the underlying dissatisfaction untouched — and would very likely increase churn.

What I would propose instead: treat the feature as an early-warning trigger rather than a lever, routing high-call-volume customers to proactive retention outreach; dig into what the calls are about, since the root cause is what is actionable; and if the business wants a causal estimate of an intervention, run an experiment — randomize the retention treatment among flagged customers — because that is the only way to establish causality.

I would also check whether the feature is genuinely available at prediction time in production or is partly post-hoc, since a symptom this strong is worth checking for leakage.

30-second version

That inverts the causality — calls are a symptom of dissatisfaction, not its cause, so suppressing them would remove the signal and likely worsen churn. Use it as an early-warning trigger, and run an experiment if you need a causal estimate.

What’s being tested

The most important judgment call in this module. Interviewers use this scenario specifically to see whether you will let a stakeholder act on a correlation. Proposing an experiment is the answer that closes it.

03Permutation importance vs impurity-based importance.feature importanceMDIpermutation＋
Strong answer

Impurity-based (MDI) — sklearn’s feature_importances_ — sums each feature’s total impurity reduction across all splits, averaged over trees. It is free, being a by-product of training, but has three biases: it favours high-cardinality and continuous features because more candidate split points give more opportunities to reduce impurity (a random unique-ID column can rank top); it is computed on training data, so it reflects what the model fitted, including noise, rather than what generalizes; and with correlated features it splits credit arbitrarily among them.

Permutation importance shuffles one feature’s values in a held-out set and measures the resulting drop in performance. It is model-agnostic, computed on held-out data so it measures contribution to generalization, and has no cardinality bias — at the cost of one re-scoring pass per feature per repeat.

Its own caveat is the correlated-feature problem in a different form: if two features are correlated, shuffling one leaves the other as a substitute, so both appear unimportant even though the information is essential.

Practical guidance: never rely on MDI alone; use permutation importance on validation data for a trustworthy global ranking, and SHAP when you need per-prediction explanations or direction of effect.

30-second version

MDI is free but biased toward high-cardinality features, computed on training data, and splits credit among correlated features. Permutation importance is model-agnostic and measures generalization — but correlated twins still mask each other.

What’s being tested

Whether you know the default is untrustworthy and why. The three specific MDI biases are the substance of the answer.

04You’re deploying a credit model. What does interpretability require of you?regulatedmonotonic constraintsmodel cardsapplied＋
Strong answer

Several distinct things, and I would separate them.

Local explanations per decision — a declined applicant is typically entitled to the principal reasons, which means per-prediction attributions (SHAP or a reason-code mapping), not a global importance chart.

Structural defensibility — I would use monotonic constraints so that, for example, higher income can never increase predicted risk. A regulator will ask about non-monotonic behaviour, and constraints make the answer trivial while usually costing little accuracy.

Model choice — a well-featured logistic regression or a GAM/EBM may be the right final model even at a small accuracy cost, because odds ratios are the accepted currency in credit and the model is auditable end to end. A common pattern is to use boosting to discover which features matter, then ship a constrained interpretable model.

Fairness auditing — test for disparate impact across protected groups, decide explicitly which fairness criterion applies since they are mutually incompatible, and check for proxy variables such as ZIP code that encode protected characteristics indirectly.

Documentation — a model card covering intended use, training data, performance by segment, known limitations and monitoring plan. With the EU AI Act’s high-risk obligations phasing in through 2026, credit scoring sits squarely in scope, so documentation, human oversight and record-keeping are compliance requirements rather than good practice.

Monitoring — track score distribution, feature drift and outcome rates by segment, with alerting.

30-second version

Local per-decision explanations, monotonic constraints for structural defensibility, possibly an intrinsically interpretable final model, fairness and proxy auditing, a model card, and segment-level monitoring.

What’s being tested

Whether you understand that regulated deployment is a different engineering problem. Distinguishing local from global, and naming monotonic constraints and the fairness incompatibility, are the differentiators.

05Can a model be fair on all definitions simultaneously?fairnessimpossibilitytrap＋
Strong answer

No, and this is a proven impossibility rather than an engineering shortfall. The common group-fairness criteria are mutually incompatible except in degenerate cases. Demographic parity requires equal positive-prediction rates across groups. Equal opportunity requires equal true-positive rates. Predictive parity requires equal precision. If the groups have different base rates, you cannot satisfy calibration/predictive parity and equalized error rates at the same time — the impossibility result that emerged from the COMPAS recidivism debate.

The practical consequence is that “make the model fair” is not a well-posed technical request. The correct response is to ask which criterion the context and regulation require, implement that one, measure and report the others so the trade-off is visible rather than hidden, and document the choice and its rationale.

Beyond the group definitions, I would also raise that fairness is not only a metric question: it depends on whether the historical labels themselves encode past discrimination, whether the protected attribute is being proxied by something like ZIP code, and whether the decision process around the model provides recourse — and none of those are fixed by choosing a different fairness metric.

30-second version

No — it is an impossibility theorem. With different base rates you cannot have predictive parity and equalized error rates together. So ask which criterion the context requires, implement it, and report the trade-off explicitly.

What’s being tested

Whether you know this is an impossibility theorem, and whether you can reframe an ill-posed request. Both are strong senior signals, and this question has become considerably more common as regulation tightened.
