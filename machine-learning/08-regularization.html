Regularization: Ridge, Lasso & Elastic Net

Constant across interviews — and the L1-sparsity question is a favourite, because most candidates know the picture but not the mechanism.

Prerequisites · Modules 05–07Study time · 6 h5 interview questionsTHEORY · MATH
🎯
Your interview goal

Give both arguments for L1 sparsity, explain why ridge is specifically right under collinearity, derive the Bayesian equivalence, and name five regularizers that are not a penalty term.

1 · From the ground up

🧩
In plain words

An unconstrained model will happily make one weight enormous if that squeezes out a fraction more training accuracy — and enormous weights are exactly how a model memorises noise. Regularisation adds a second term to the loss that charges the model rent on the size of its weights. It now has to justify every large weight with enough error reduction to cover the rent. That is the whole idea. The only interesting question left is how you charge — and squaring the weights versus taking their absolute value produces two genuinely different behaviours.

What a big weight actually means

A weight of 4,000 on a feature says “when this feature moves by 0.001, my prediction moves by 4”. That is an extraordinarily strong claim to have learned from finite data, and models make it constantly — most often when two features nearly duplicate each other, so the model discovers it can put +5,000 on one and −4,997 on the other and fit the training noise beautifully while predicting nonsense on anything new. Large weights are therefore not just a symptom of overfitting; they are a specific, detectable mechanism for it.

So what: constraining weight size attacks overfitting at its source, which is why regularisation works across so many model families.

The two rents, and the one difference that matters

You minimise error + λ · penalty(w). The multiplier λ is how expensive the rent is: λ = 0 is free and gives back the unregularised model; very large λ makes weights so expensive that the model gives up and predicts the mean.

  • L2 / Ridge — penalty is Σwⱼ². Because it squares, it punishes one huge weight far more than several medium ones, so it spreads weight across correlated features. Its gradient is 2λw, which shrinks proportionally: a weight of 10 gets pulled by 20λ, a weight of 0.01 by 0.02λ. As a weight approaches zero the pull vanishes, so nothing ever quite reaches zero.
  • L1 / Lasso — penalty is Σ|wⱼ|. Its gradient is λ·sign(w), a constant pull of the same size no matter how small the weight already is. A weight of 0.001 gets pulled just as hard as a weight of 10. So weak weights get pushed all the way to exactly zero and stay there — the model performs feature selection as a side effect of the penalty's shape.
So what: “why does L1 give exact zeros and L2 does not?” is answered in one sentence — constant pull versus proportional pull. This is the cleanest version of the answer and it needs no geometry.

Both animations start from the same weight and both shrink it. The L1 dot travels at constant speed and stops dead on zero; the L2 dot decelerates and asymptotes. That single difference is the entire sparsity story, and it explains why L1 needs a special optimiser — the penalty has a kink at zero where the derivative does not exist.

The geometric picture, for when they ask for it

Same fact, drawn instead of derived. Picture the unregularised loss as concentric ellipses around the OLS solution, and the penalty as a budget region you must stay inside. For L2 that region is a circle; for L1 it is a diamond with corners on the axes. The solution is where the smallest ellipse first touches the region. A circle has no special points, so contact happens anywhere and both coordinates are generally non-zero. A diamond has sharp corners that stick out, and corners lie exactly on the axes — so contact happens at a corner disproportionately often, and a corner means one coordinate is exactly zero.

So what: have both the geometric and the gradient argument. Interviewers often accept the diamond picture and then ask “but why really?”, and the constant-pull answer is the one that satisfies them.

Why ridge specifically fixes multicollinearity

Write X = UΣVᵀ. Ridge scales each direction of the data by σᵢ²/(σᵢ² + λ). Directions with large singular values — the well-determined ones — have that factor near 1 and are barely touched. Directions with tiny singular values — precisely the near-collinear ones, where coefficient variance explodes — get crushed. Ridge is not a blunt shrinkage of everything; it is targeted damping of exactly the unstable directions. And because XᵀX + λI shifts every eigenvalue up by λ, it is invertible for any λ > 0, which is why ridge also rescues the d > n case where OLS has no unique solution at all.

The Bayesian reading, and the practical rules

Regularisation is a prior. Maximising the posterior with a Gaussian prior centred at zero gives you exactly ridge; a Laplace prior — sharply peaked at zero — gives you exactly lasso. The Laplace's spike at zero is the probabilistic version of the diamond's corner, which is a satisfying way to tie the three arguments together.

Two rules that must be said out loud, because forgetting either is a standard trap. Always scale your features first — the penalty compares raw weight magnitudes, so a feature measured in rupees gets a naturally tiny weight and is under-penalised relative to one measured in years. Never penalise the intercept: shrinking it would drag every prediction toward zero rather than toward the data's mean, which is not a bias–variance trade-off, just damage.

Analogy — you are packing a suitcase with a weight limit. Ridge is a per-item excess charge proportional to the square of its weight: you keep everything but bring smaller versions of each thing. Lasso is a flat fee per item you bring at all: now the sensible move is to leave the marginal items behind entirely, and you arrive with fewer, fully-sized items. Elastic net charges both, so you drop the pointless items but keep both halves of a matching pair rather than arbitrarily discarding one.

2 · What to learn

  1. Regularization as the bias–variance dial, applied through the loss function.
  2. Ridge (L2): J = loss + λ‖w‖², closed form (XᵀX + λI)⁻¹Xᵀy.
  3. Lasso (L1): J = loss + λ‖w‖₁, no closed form, produces exact zeros.
  4. Elastic Net: J = loss + λ(α‖w‖₁ + (1-α)‖w‖²).
  5. Why L1 produces sparsity — the geometric argument and the subgradient argument. Know both.
  6. The SVD view of ridge: shrinkage factor σᵢ²/(σᵢ²+λ) per singular direction.
  7. The Bayesian view: L2 = Gaussian prior, L1 = Laplace prior, both as MAP estimation.
  8. Why the intercept is not penalized, and why features must be standardized first.
  9. Choosing λ by cross-validation; the regularization path; RidgeCV and LassoCV.
  10. Behaviour under correlated features: lasso picks arbitrarily, ridge spreads weight, elastic net groups them.
  11. Other regularization families: early stopping, dropout, data augmentation, ensembling, explicit capacity limits.
  12. Group lasso and monotonic constraints, briefly.

3 · Core intuitions

A penalty is a price on complexity

Unpenalized fitting chases every wiggle in the training sample. Adding λ‖w‖² makes large coefficients expensive, so the optimizer only “buys” a large coefficient when the loss reduction justifies it. As λ → 0 you recover OLS; as λ → ∞ all coefficients go to zero and you predict the mean. Cross-validation finds the sweet spot in between.

Why L1 gives exact zeros and L2 does not — geometry

Think of it as minimizing the loss subject to a budget on the coefficients. The L2 budget region ‖w‖₂ ≤ t is a circle or sphere — smooth, no corners. The L1 region ‖w‖₁ ≤ t is a diamond whose vertices lie exactly on the axes, where some coordinates are zero. The solution is where the expanding elliptical loss contours first touch the budget region, and a convex region with pointy corners is very likely to be touched at a corner — which means a coefficient of exactly zero. A sphere has no corners, so contact happens at a generic point where every coefficient is small but non-zero.

Why L1 gives exact zeros — calculus (the mechanism)

The L2 penalty’s derivative 2λw_j vanishes as w_j → 0, so the shrinkage force fades exactly where it would need to be strongest to reach zero — asymptotic shrinkage, never arrival. The L1 subgradient is λ·sign(w_j), which has constant magnitude λ regardless of how small w_j is. So there is a persistent push toward zero, and any feature whose loss-gradient contribution is smaller than λ gets pinned at exactly zero. This is visible directly in the soft-thresholding update of coordinate descent, where the max(·,0) is literally the mechanism creating the zero:

soft_thresholding.txt
wⱼ ← sign(zⱼ)·max(|zⱼ| - λ, 0)

Ridge shrinks the unstable directions hardest

Via SVD, ridge multiplies each singular direction’s contribution by σᵢ²/(σᵢ²+λ). Directions with large σᵢ — well-determined by the data — are barely affected. Directions with tiny σᵢ — the collinear, high-variance directions where OLS coefficients explode — get crushed. This is the precise reason ridge is the right tool for multicollinearity, and it is a better answer than “it shrinks coefficients”.

Regularization is a prior

Maximize the posterior instead of the likelihood: log p(w|D) ∝ log p(D|w) + log p(w). A zero-mean Gaussian prior contributes -‖w‖²/2τ², i.e. L2. A Laplace prior contributes -‖w‖₁/b, i.e. L1 — and the Laplace density’s sharp peak at zero is the probabilistic counterpart of the geometric corner. So λ encodes how strongly you believe coefficients are small before seeing data.

Standardize first, and do not penalize the intercept

The penalty treats all coefficients with the same λ, so a feature measured in millimetres and one in kilometres get wildly unfair treatment unless you standardize. And penalizing the intercept would shrink predictions toward zero rather than toward the target mean, which is not what you want — so every sane implementation excludes it.

The direction trap

Ridge and Lasso take alpha, where bigger means more regularization. LogisticRegression and SVC take C = 1/λ, where bigger means less. Getting this backwards in a live coding round is a common and very visible mistake.

6 · Hands-on drill

  1. Generate data with 100 features where only 5 matter. Fit OLS, ridge and lasso across a λ grid, then plot the regularization paths — coefficient value versus λ — for all three. Confirm you can see lasso coefficients hitting exactly zero while ridge’s only asymptote toward it.
  2. Count how many of the 5 true features lasso recovers, and how many false ones it keeps.
  3. Duplicate one of the 5 true features (perfect correlation) and re-fit. Observe that lasso splits or arbitrarily picks between the twins while ridge splits the weight evenly and elastic net keeps both. This experiment is the answer to the correlated-features question.
  4. Implement soft-thresholding coordinate descent for lasso from scratch and confirm it produces exact zeros.
⚠️
Gotchas that fail candidates
  • Not standardizing before regularizing. The penalty becomes arbitrary.
  • Only giving the geometric sparsity argument. Give the subgradient one too — it is the mechanism.
  • Saying lasso is always better because it selects features. With correlated predictors its selection is unstable across resamples.
  • Getting the alpha versus C direction wrong.
  • Penalizing the intercept.
  • Thinking regularization only means an L1/L2 penalty. Early stopping, subsampling, dropout, bagging, min_samples_leaf and more data are all regularization.

7 · Interview questions

5 questions
01Why does L1 produce exactly zero coefficients while L2 does not?L1sparsitysubgradienttrap＋
Strong answer

Two complementary arguments.

Geometric: view it as minimizing the loss subject to a coefficient budget. The L1 constraint region ‖w‖₁ ≤ t is a diamond whose vertices sit on the coordinate axes, where some coefficients are exactly zero; the L2 region is a smooth sphere. The optimum is where the loss’s elliptical contours first touch the region, and a region with sharp axis-aligned corners is very likely to be touched at a corner — producing exact zeros. A sphere has no corners, so contact is at a generic point with all coefficients small but non-zero.

Analytic (the mechanism): the L2 gradient is 2λwⱼ, which vanishes as wⱼ → 0, so the shrinkage force disappears precisely where it would need to be strongest — asymptotic shrinkage without arrival. The L1 subgradient is λ·sign(wⱼ), constant in magnitude no matter how small wⱼ is, so there is a persistent constant push to zero and any coefficient whose loss gradient is smaller than λ is pinned at zero. You see this explicitly in lasso’s coordinate-descent update wⱼ ← sign(zⱼ)·max(|zⱼ|−λ, 0), where max(·,0) is the zeroing operation.

30-second version

Geometrically, L1’s diamond has corners on the axes where coefficients are zero, and contours tend to touch corners. Mechanically, L1’s subgradient is constant λ·sign(w) so the push to zero never fades, whereas L2’s 2λw vanishes as w → 0.

What’s being tested

Depth. Nearly every candidate gives the diamond picture; the subgradient and soft-threshold mechanism is what distinguishes a strong answer, because it explains why the geometry works.

02Ridge or lasso: how do you choose?model selectioncorrelated features＋
Strong answer

Start from what you believe about the signal. If you expect many features each contributing a little — the dense case — ridge is better, because it keeps them all and shrinks proportionally. If you expect a few strong predictors among many irrelevant ones — the sparse case — lasso is better and hands you feature selection for free.

Then check correlation structure: with groups of correlated predictors, lasso arbitrarily keeps one and zeroes the others, which is unstable across resamples and can be actively misleading for interpretation, whereas ridge spreads weight across the group and elastic net selects the group as a unit — so elastic net is usually the pragmatic default for wide correlated data.

Practically: cross-validate all three over a λ grid, and also weigh the operational factor that a sparse model is cheaper to serve and easier to maintain, which can justify a small accuracy loss.

30-second version

Dense signal with many small effects favours ridge; a few strong predictors favours lasso. With correlated groups, lasso’s choice is unstable — so elastic net is usually the pragmatic default. Then cross-validate all three.

What’s being tested

Whether you reason from the data’s structure rather than reciting “L1 selects features”.

Likely follow-up: “Why is lasso’s selection unstable with correlated features?” — among near-duplicates the loss is nearly flat with respect to which one carries the weight, so tiny sampling changes flip the choice. For stable selection use elastic net, or stability selection: bootstrap lasso and keep features chosen in most resamples.
03Show the Bayesian interpretation of ridge regression.BayesianMAPderivation＋
Strong answer

Ridge is the MAP estimate under a Gaussian prior. Assume y|X,w ~ N(Xw, σ²I) and a prior w ~ N(0, τ²I). The log posterior is log p(y|X,w) + log p(w) + const, which equals -‖y-Xw‖²/(2σ²) - ‖w‖²/(2τ²) + const.

Maximizing it is minimizing ‖y-Xw‖² + (σ²/τ²)‖w‖² — exactly ridge with λ = σ²/τ². The interpretation is illuminating: λ is the ratio of noise variance to prior variance, so noisy data or a tight prior belief that coefficients are small implies large λ and heavy shrinkage.

Substituting a Laplace prior in place of the Gaussian yields the L1 penalty and lasso, and the Laplace density’s sharp spike at zero is the probabilistic counterpart of the L1 diamond’s corner.

30-second version

Ridge is MAP with a Gaussian prior on w: the log posterior gives loss + (σ²/τ²)‖w‖², so λ = σ²/τ² — the ratio of noise variance to prior variance. A Laplace prior gives lasso.

What’s being tested

Whether you see regularization as principled inference rather than a hyperparameter hack. Deriving λ = σ²/τ² and interpreting it is the strong finish.

04Name five forms of regularization that are not an L1/L2 penalty.breadthregularization＋
Strong answer

(1) Early stopping — halt training when validation loss stops improving; in boosting and neural nets this is the primary complexity control, and for gradient descent on a convex problem it is provably related to L2 shrinkage. (2) Ensembling / bagging — averaging decorrelated models reduces variance, which is regularization by another route. (3) Data augmentation, or simply more data — the most effective variance reducer when available. (4) Explicit capacity limits — max_depth, min_samples_leaf, max_leaf_nodes, fewer features, lower polynomial degree. (5) Stochasticity during training — dropout in neural networks, and subsample / colsample_bytree in gradient boosting, both preventing reliance on any single path or feature.

Two more worth naming: noise injection on inputs or labels (label smoothing), and structural constraints such as monotonicity constraints in XGBoost or LightGBM, which restrict the hypothesis space using domain knowledge and are increasingly used in regulated settings.

30-second version

Early stopping, ensembling, more data or augmentation, explicit capacity limits, and training stochasticity like dropout or subsampling — plus noise injection and monotonic constraints.

What’s being tested

Breadth. Candidates who equate regularization with alpha reveal a narrow mental model. Also sets up Modules 17 and 18.

05You have 200 samples and 5,000 features. What do you do?appliedhigh dimensionalleakagetrap＋
Strong answer

d ≫ n, so XᵀX is singular and unregularized OLS has no unique solution — regularization is mandatory, not optional.

(1) Expect huge variance, so evaluate with repeated or nested cross-validation and report spread; a single holdout of 40 rows tells you almost nothing. (2) Start with strongly regularized linear models: lasso or elastic net for sparsity and interpretability, ridge if you believe many small effects. (3) Reduce dimensionality where sensible — remove near-zero-variance features, deduplicate perfectly correlated ones, consider PCA or a univariate filter — but fit any selection inside the CV folds, because selecting features on all the data is leakage and is spectacularly optimistic in this regime. (4) Use domain knowledge to prune; in the d≫n regime priors are worth more than algorithms. (5) Be very sceptical of complex models — a boosted ensemble on 200 rows will overfit. (6) If possible, get more data; the honest answer is that 200 rows bounds what any method can deliver.

30-second version

Regularization is mandatory since XᵀX is singular. Lasso or elastic net, repeated or nested CV reporting spread, dimensionality reduction fitted inside the folds — and honesty that 200 rows caps what is achievable.

What’s being tested

Whether you recognize the regime and the leakage trap in feature selection. The “select inside the CV fold” point is the one most candidates miss.

2026 additions. Regularisation is now asked as a feature-selection and stability problem, and interviewers connect it to the regularisation you find inside boosting and neural networks.
06You used lasso to select 40 features from 800, and a stakeholder wants to build a strategy on that list. Any concerns?2026feature selectionstability＋
Strong answer

Several, and the biggest is that the list is less stable than it looks. Among a group of correlated features, lasso keeps one and zeros the rest — and which one it keeps is close to arbitrary, decided by small sample accidents. Resample the data and a different member of the group survives. So “feature X matters and feature Y does not” is not a conclusion the method can support; the honest statement is “something in this correlated group matters”.

Three further caveats. Selection is not importance — a feature can survive with a small coefficient while a zeroed one was nearly as useful, and the coefficient magnitudes are not comparable unless the features were standardised. The count of 40 is an artefact of λ, not a property of the problem: nudge λ and you get 25 or 70, so any narrative built on “the 40 features that matter” is really a narrative about one cross-validation result. And none of this is causal — a selected feature may be a proxy for the real driver, so a strategy that intervenes on it can do nothing at all.

What I would do instead. Run stability selection: fit lasso on many bootstrap resamples and report each feature's selection frequency. Features chosen in 90% of resamples are a real finding; ones chosen 40% of the time are a coin flip and should be reported as such. Switch to elastic net, which keeps correlated groups together rather than picking one at random, so the output is interpretable at the group level. Cluster the correlated features first and select over clusters, then let the domain expert choose the representative. And cross-check with a method that has different biases — permutation importance or SHAP on a boosted model — because agreement across methods is much stronger evidence than any single ranking.

30-second version

Lasso picks one feature from each correlated group essentially arbitrarily, so the list is unstable across resamples — and the count of 40 is an artefact of λ. I would run stability selection over bootstraps and report selection frequencies, switch to elastic net so correlated groups stay together, and cross-check against SHAP or permutation importance. And I would be explicit that selection is not causation.

What’s being tested

Whether you trust your own tools appropriately. Stability selection is the answer that marks someone who has been burned by this; naming the arbitrariness within correlated groups is the minimum bar.

Likely follow-up: “Why does elastic net keep the group?” — the L2 component is strictly convex, so it prefers to split weight evenly among identical features rather than concentrate it, while the L1 component still zeros genuinely useless ones. That combination — the grouping effect — is exactly the problem elastic net was introduced to solve.
07Where is regularisation hiding in a gradient-boosted model, and in a neural network?2026connectionsboosting＋
Strong answer

Almost everywhere, which is the point — once you recognise regularisation as “anything that trades a little training fit for less variance”, most hyperparameters in both families turn out to be regularisers, and that reframing is what the question is testing.

In XGBoost and its relatives, the objective literally contains the penalties: λ is L2 on the leaf weights, α is L1 on them, and γ charges a fixed cost per leaf, so a split must reduce loss by more than γ to be kept — that is pre-pruning expressed as a penalty. Then a second layer of regularisers that are not written as penalties but act as them: the learning rate (shrinkage — every tree's contribution is scaled down, so no single tree can dominate), max_depth and min_child_weight (capacity limits per tree), subsample and colsample (stochastic noise injection, the same mechanism as bagging), and early stopping, which is the most important of them all because it caps the number of trees at the point where validation loss stops improving.

In neural networks: weight decay is L2 by another name — although in Adam the two differ, which is exactly what AdamW decouples and is worth naming. Dropout is closest to an ensemble-and-noise regulariser rather than a weight penalty. Early stopping again, and in the linear case it is provably related to L2 shrinkage. Batch normalisation and data augmentation both regularise as side effects. And the deepest one: SGD's implicit bias toward minimum-norm solutions regularises without any term in the loss at all, which is a large part of why over-parameterised networks generalise.

The unifying sentence I would end on: a penalty on weights, a limit on capacity, injected noise, and stopping early are four different implementations of the same trade — accept more bias to remove more variance.

30-second version

In XGBoost the objective has λ for L2 and α for L1 on leaf weights, plus γ as a per-leaf cost — and the learning rate, depth, subsampling and early stopping all regularise without being written as penalties. In networks: weight decay, dropout, early stopping, batch norm, augmentation, and SGD's implicit minimum-norm bias. Penalties, capacity caps, injected noise and early stopping are four implementations of one trade.

What’s being tested

Whether regularisation is a concept for you or a pair of formulas. This is a favourite because it reveals immediately whether a candidate can transfer an idea across model families.

Likely follow-up: “Which XGBoost regulariser would you tune first?” — the learning rate together with the number of trees via early stopping, because they interact directly and dominate everything else: a lower learning rate with more trees is almost always better if you can afford the compute. Then max_depth and min_child_weight for capacity, then subsampling, and λ, α and γ last — they are usually the least sensitive knobs in practice.
08How do you choose λ — and what if the cross-validation curve is almost flat across two orders of magnitude?tuningcross-validationjudgment＋
Strong answer

By cross-validation over a logarithmic grid, because λ acts multiplicatively — the meaningful steps are 0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, not evenly spaced values. And with features standardised inside the fold, since the penalty compares raw weight magnitudes and an unscaled feature is effectively unpenalised.

A flat curve is not a problem, it is information: it says performance is insensitive to λ across that range, so I get to choose on other grounds. The principled choice is the one-standard-error rule — take the largest λ whose mean CV score is still within one standard error of the best. That deliberately picks a simpler model than the outright optimum, on the reasoning that the outright optimum is the maximum of many noisy estimates and is therefore partly an artefact of fold noise. With lasso that simpler model is also more sparse, cheaper to serve and easier to explain, so the flat region is a gift.

Two further things I would check before accepting the result. Look at the shape of the curve, not just its argmax: a sharp spike is a warning that the estimate is unstable and probably fold-specific, whereas a broad basin is trustworthy. And confirm the grid's edges are not the winner — if the best λ sits at the smallest or largest value I tried, the optimum is outside my grid and I should extend it rather than report a boundary value.

30-second version

Cross-validation on a log grid, with scaling done inside the fold. A flat curve means λ does not matter much, so I use the one-standard-error rule: the largest λ still within one standard error of the best, which gives a simpler, sparser, cheaper model and guards against picking the maximum of noisy estimates. I would also check that the winner is not at the edge of my grid.

What’s being tested

Whether you know that the argmax of a noisy curve is itself a noisy quantity. The one-standard-error rule is the specific answer being listened for, and “log grid” plus “scale inside the fold” are the hygiene markers.

Likely follow-up: “Your best λ is essentially zero. What does that tell you?” — that the data is not asking for regularisation: you probably have far more rows than features, low collinearity and little noise, so the unregularised fit is already low-variance. I would sanity-check for leakage, since a suspiciously easy problem is often a contaminated one, and I would keep a small non-zero λ anyway for numerical stability and to guard against the separation pathology if it is a logistic model.
