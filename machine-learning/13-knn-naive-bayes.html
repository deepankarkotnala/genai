KNN, Naive Bayes & Discriminant Analysis

Rarely your production model, but these three carry concepts you will be tested on: lazy learning, the curse of dimensionality, and generative classification.

Prerequisites · Modules 01, 02, 04Study time · 6 h6 interview questionsTHEORY · MATH
🎯
Your interview goal

Explain distance concentration geometrically, say why Naive Bayes classifies well while estimating probabilities badly, and hold LDA, PCA and logistic regression cleanly apart.

1 · From the ground up

🧩
In plain words

These are two of the simplest algorithms in machine learning, and neither is here because you will ship it. Each one teaches a lesson bigger than itself. KNN does no learning at all — it stores the data and looks up the closest rows — which makes it the cleanest possible place to see how distance stops working in high dimensions. Naive Bayes is built on an assumption everyone knows is false, and it works anyway, which teaches you that a wrong assumption is not automatically a fatal one.

KNN: the model with no training step

Training a KNN is just storing your data. That is the entire fit. To predict a new row you measure its distance to every stored row, take the k closest, and let them vote.

So there is no equation, no weights, nothing learned. This is why it is called a lazy learner — it postpones all the work until you ask it a question.

So what: the cost is upside-down compared with every other model. Training is instant; prediction is slow and grows with your dataset, because every prediction scans everything you stored. That alone rules it out for most production serving.

k is the same bias–variance dial as everywhere else

Set k = 1 and every training point is its own neighbour, so training accuracy is a perfect 100% and the boundary is a jagged shape that traces every last piece of noise. Classic high variance.

Set k to the size of the dataset and every prediction is just the majority class, everywhere. Classic high bias.

So what: small k = wiggly and overfit, large k = smooth and underfit. It is the same dial as tree depth or SVM's C, wearing different clothes — and seeing that it is the same dial is more valuable than remembering the algorithm.

Scaling is not optional here — it decides the answer

Distance adds up the difference on every feature. Now put salary and age in the same table. Two people differ by 40 years of age and by 1,000 rupees of salary. The distance between them is √(40² + 1000²) ≈ 1000.8.

Age contributed 0.8 of that. In effect, the model ignored age completely.

So what: unscaled KNN is KNN on whichever feature happens to have the biggest numbers. Always standardise first. This is also the sharpest way to remember which models need scaling — anything that measures distance does; trees, which only compare one feature to a threshold, do not.

The real lesson: in high dimensions nothing is nearby

KNN rests on one assumption: that the closest rows are genuinely similar. In two or three dimensions that is obviously true. Add features and it quietly stops being true.

Here is a way to see it exactly. Imagine your data spread evenly through a cube with sides of length 1. You want a small box around your query point holding 10% of the data. How long must that box's sides be? The answer is 0.1^(1/d), where d is the number of features — and the numbers are startling.

So what: in 100 dimensions your "local neighbourhood" has to stretch across 98% of every single axis. It is not a neighbourhood at all — it is nearly the whole dataset. The word "nearest" has quietly lost its meaning.
How much of each axis you must cover to reach just 10% of the data

This is the curse of dimensionality, and it is not a vague warning — it is arithmetic. As features pile up, every point drifts roughly the same distance from every other point, so the gap between the nearest and the furthest neighbour shrinks toward nothing. Any method that relies on distance degrades: KNN, k-means, DBSCAN, RBF kernels. The usual fix is to cut the dimensions first with PCA or feature selection.

Naive Bayes: making an impossible sum possible

You want P(spam | the words in this email). Bayes' rule flips it into something you can count: P(words | spam) · P(spam).

Except you still cannot count it. P(words | spam) means the probability of that exact whole combination of words. With a vocabulary of ten thousand words, the number of possible combinations is larger than any dataset will ever be. You would need to have seen this precise email before.

So Naive Bayes makes one sweeping assumption: given the class, the features are independent of each other. Then the joint probability becomes a simple product — P(w₁|spam) × P(w₂|spam) × … — and each of those is just counting one word at a time.

So what: the "naive" assumption is not a modelling nicety, it is the thing that turns an impossible count into an easy one. That is why it survives despite being false.

Why being wrong does not stop it working

The assumption is plainly false. "Free" and "money" turn up together far more often than chance. So the probabilities Naive Bayes reports are wrong, and often wildly wrong — it counts the same evidence several times over and drives its outputs to 0.999 or 0.001.

But look at what a classifier actually has to do: pick which class scores highest. It never has to get the number right. Correlated features exaggerate the score for both classes at once, so the ranking usually survives even when the magnitude does not.

So what: say it as "Naive Bayes is a good classifier and a bad probability estimator". Use its predictions; do not use its predict_proba as a real probability, and never feed it into an expected-cost calculation without calibrating first.

Every Naive Bayes value has been shoved to an extreme — email C's honest 0.55 is reported as 0.91. Yet every one of the five decisions is identical, because all that matters is which side of the line a point lands on. That gap between "the decision is right" and "the number is right" is the whole point of the algorithm.

One zero would destroy the whole product

Naive Bayes multiplies probabilities together. So if a single word never appeared in any spam email during training, P(word | spam) = 0, and the entire product collapses to zero — no matter how obviously spam-like the other twenty words are.

The fix is Laplace smoothing: add 1 to every count before dividing. Nothing is ever exactly zero, so no single unseen word can veto the whole decision.

So what: this is not a patch, it is a prior — you are saying "I have not seen this word in spam, but I refuse to claim it is impossible." Two other practical notes: use log probabilities and add them instead of multiplying, or long documents underflow to zero; and pick the right variant — Multinomial for word counts, Bernoulli for present/absent, Gaussian for continuous features.

LDA and PCA look similar and answer different questions

Both find new directions to project your data onto, and both are often used to reduce dimensions. The difference is what they optimise.

PCA looks for the directions with the most spread, and never looks at the labels. LDA looks for the directions that best separate the classes, and needs the labels to do it.

So what: PCA is unsupervised, LDA is supervised — that one line answers the question. The consequence: PCA can happily discard the very direction that separates your classes, if that direction happens to have low variance. LDA cannot, because separation is the only thing it is looking for.
Analogy — KNN is asking your five nearest neighbours how they voted, and assuming you will vote the same way. Reasonable on a real street. Now imagine a street defined by a hundred different characteristics — age, income, job, diet, commute, and so on. Once you demand "similar on all hundred", your five nearest neighbours are not close on anything in particular; they are just the least-far people in a very empty place. That is the curse of dimensionality. Naive Bayes is a detective who treats every clue as unrelated to every other. Fingerprints and a matching shoe size are counted as two independent shocks, when really they point at the same person. The detective is far too confident, and still names the right suspect — because they only have to rank the suspects, not state the true odds.

2 · What to learn

KNN

  1. The algorithm: store everything, then at prediction time find the k nearest training points and vote (classification) or average (regression).
  2. Lazy learning: no training cost, expensive inference — the opposite profile from everything else in this curriculum.
  3. Distance metrics: Euclidean, Manhattan, Minkowski, cosine, Hamming, Gower for mixed types.
  4. Choosing k and its bias–variance meaning; why odd k for binary problems.
  5. Distance weighting, and why scaling is mandatory.
  6. The curse of dimensionality — the central concept here.
  7. Efficient search: KD-trees, ball trees, and why they fail in high dimensions; approximate nearest neighbours (HNSW, IVF-PQ) — the direct bridge to vector databases.

Naive Bayes

  1. Bayes’ theorem applied to classification, and the “naive” conditional-independence assumption.
  2. Variants: Gaussian, Multinomial, Bernoulli, Complement — and which data each suits.
  3. Laplace / additive smoothing and the zero-frequency problem.
  4. Why it works well on text despite an obviously false assumption.
  5. Why its probability estimates are poorly calibrated even when its classification is good.

Discriminant analysis

  1. LDA: shared covariance, linear boundary, and its dual life as a supervised dimensionality reducer.
  2. QDA: per-class covariance, quadratic boundary, more parameters.
  3. LDA versus PCA (supervised versus unsupervised), and LDA versus logistic regression (generative versus discriminative).

3 · Core intuitions

KNN is the purest form of “similar inputs have similar outputs”

It makes no global assumption about the function’s form — it just trusts locality. That makes it a genuinely non-parametric learner with zero bias in the limit, and also the model most exposed to bad distance geometry.

The curse of dimensionality is why KNN fails on wide data

As dimensions grow, three things happen together. Volume grows exponentially, so any fixed number of samples becomes vanishingly sparse — maintaining the same local density would need exponentially more data. Distances concentrate: the ratio between the nearest and farthest neighbour distances tends toward 1, so “nearest” stops being meaningfully different from “farthest” and the whole notion of a neighbourhood degrades. And almost all the volume of a high-dimensional ball sits near its surface, so every point is effectively on the boundary with no interior to interpolate within.

The consequence: in high dimensions your k nearest neighbours are not actually near, and KNN’s predictions become noise. This affects every distance-based method — KNN, K-Means, RBF kernels — and it is why dimensionality reduction or learned embeddings precede them in practice.

k is the bias–variance dial, and it is inverted from what beginners expect

k = 1 gives zero training error and a jagged, high-variance boundary that memorizes noise. Large k averages over a big neighbourhood, smoothing the boundary — more bias, less variance. At k = n you always predict the global majority. So increasing k regularizes.

Naive Bayes assumes conditional independence, which is essentially always false — and it still works

The reason is that classification only needs the argmax of the posterior to be right, not the posterior itself. The independence assumption badly distorts the magnitude of the probabilities — correlated features effectively get their evidence counted multiple times, driving estimates toward 0 and 1 — but it frequently leaves the ordering of classes intact.

So Naive Bayes is often a decent classifier and a poor probability estimator, which is exactly the distinction from Module 10’s calibration discussion. It also has a real practical virtue: with d features and K classes it estimates O(dK) parameters instead of a joint distribution, so it trains in one pass, needs very little data, and remains a strong text baseline.

LDA is logistic regression’s generative twin

Both produce a linear boundary. LDA gets there by assuming Gaussian class-conditionals with a shared covariance and estimating means, the pooled covariance and the priors; logistic regression gets there by directly fitting the log-odds with no distributional assumption. When the Gaussian assumption holds, LDA is more statistically efficient — it needs less data. When it does not, logistic regression is more robust.

LDA also doubles as a supervised dimensionality reducer, projecting to at most K−1 dimensions — the key contrast with PCA. PCA maximizes total variance ignoring labels; LDA maximizes between-class separation relative to within-class scatter, using them.

5 · Hands-on drill

  1. Implement KNN from scratch. Plot test accuracy against k from 1 to 50 and identify the U-curve.
  2. Demonstrate the curse of dimensionality directly: for d from 2 to 1,000, sample random points and plot the ratio of mean farthest-neighbour distance to mean nearest-neighbour distance. Watch it approach 1. This plot is the best possible answer to the curse-of-dimensionality question — describe it in interviews.
  3. Run KNN with and without scaling on a dataset with mixed units and record the accuracy gap.
  4. Implement Multinomial Naive Bayes for text with Laplace smoothing and compare against logistic regression on TF-IDF features. Then compare their calibration curves — NB will be far worse calibrated while remaining competitive on accuracy.
  5. Fit LDA and PCA on the same labelled dataset, project to 2D, and plot both. The visual difference between “maximum variance” and “maximum class separation” makes the contrast permanent.
⚠️
Gotchas that fail candidates
  • Not scaling before KNN. A feature in dollars will dominate one in years entirely.
  • Getting the k bias–variance direction backwards.
  • Saying Naive Bayes fails because features are correlated. It often classifies well anyway — explain why.
  • Not knowing about Laplace smoothing. One unseen word gives a zero product and destroys the posterior.
  • Confusing LDA (Linear Discriminant Analysis) with LDA (Latent Dirichlet Allocation). Clarify which is meant if the context is ambiguous — a nice moment to show precision.
  • Saying PCA and LDA are interchangeable. One uses labels, the other does not.

6 · Interview questions

6 questions
01Explain the curse of dimensionality and its practical consequences.dimensionalitygeometryembeddings＋
Strong answer

As the number of features grows, the volume of the feature space grows exponentially, so a fixed sample size becomes exponentially sparser — maintaining the same local density requires exponentially more data.

Two geometric consequences follow. Distances concentrate: the ratio of farthest to nearest neighbour distance tends toward 1, so all points become roughly equidistant and “nearest neighbour” loses meaning. And nearly all the volume of a high-dimensional ball lies near its surface, so every point is effectively on the boundary with no interior to interpolate within.

Practical consequences: KNN, K-Means and RBF kernels degrade badly; overfitting becomes easy because there is always some hyperplane separating any labelling of sparse points; and distance-based outlier detection becomes unreliable. Mitigations: dimensionality reduction, learned embeddings that compress to a dense low-dimensional manifold, feature selection, models whose inductive bias tolerates high dimensions (regularized linear models, trees), and exploiting the fact that real data usually lies on a much lower-dimensional manifold than its ambient dimension suggests.

30-second version

Volume grows exponentially so data becomes sparse; distances concentrate so nearest and farthest converge; and all volume sits near the surface. Distance-based methods degrade, and overfitting becomes trivial.

What’s being tested

Whether you understand the geometry rather than just the phrase. Distance concentration and the manifold caveat are the two markers of a strong answer.

Likely follow-up: “Then why does KNN work in vector search over 1,536-dimensional embeddings?” — because embeddings are learned so semantic similarity corresponds to cosine proximity, the data occupies a low-dimensional manifold within the ambient space, the metric is usually cosine rather than raw Euclidean, and production systems use approximate methods (HNSW, IVF-PQ) tuned to that structure. The curse applies to arbitrary high-dimensional data, not to a well-trained embedding space.
02How does k in KNN affect bias and variance?KNNbias-variance＋
Strong answer

Small k means low bias and high variance: k=1 fits the training data perfectly, giving zero training error and a jagged boundary that memorizes label noise, so a single mislabeled point carves out its own region. Large k means higher bias and lower variance: predictions average over a wide neighbourhood, smoothing the boundary and potentially washing out genuine local structure; at k=n you always predict the global majority, which is maximum bias and zero variance. So increasing k is regularization. Choose it by cross-validation, use an odd k for binary problems to avoid ties, and consider distance weighting so nearer neighbours count more — which lets you use a larger k without over-smoothing.

30-second version

Small k means low bias, high variance and a jagged boundary memorizing noise. Large k smooths, adding bias and cutting variance. So increasing k regularizes.

What’s being tested

Whether you can apply the bias–variance frame to a specific hyperparameter. The direction is what people get wrong.

03Naive Bayes assumes feature independence, which is obviously false for text. Why does it work?Naive Bayesargmaxcalibrationtrap＋
Strong answer

Because classification needs only the argmax of the posterior to be correct, not the posterior’s numerical value. The independence assumption does damage the probability estimates — correlated features have their evidence effectively counted multiple times, pushing estimates toward 0 and 1 — but that distortion often preserves the ranking of classes, so the predicted label stays right.

Three additional reasons it does well on text specifically: the parameter count is tiny — O(dK) instead of a joint distribution — so it trains in one pass and works with very little data; bag-of-words features are individually weakly informative but jointly numerous, so accumulating many mildly-correlated log-probabilities is fairly robust; and high-dimensional sparse text data suits a model with strong assumptions and low variance.

The right summary is that Naive Bayes is often a good classifier and a poor probability estimator — which is exactly why you would calibrate it before using its outputs as probabilities.

30-second version

Because only the argmax needs to be right, not the probability. Independence distorts the magnitudes but usually preserves the class ranking — so it is a good classifier and a bad probability estimator.

What’s being tested

Whether you understand the argmax-versus-posterior distinction. The canonical answer, and it links directly to Module 10’s calibration material.

04What is Laplace smoothing and why is it necessary?smoothingDirichletderivation＋
Strong answer

Naive Bayes multiplies per-feature conditional probabilities, so a single term of zero makes the whole product zero. If a word never appeared with a given class in training, then P(word|class) = 0 and that class’s posterior is annihilated regardless of how much other evidence supports it — the zero-frequency problem.

Laplace (additive) smoothing fixes it with a pseudo-count: P(xⱼ=v|y) = (count + α)/(total + α·V), where V is the vocabulary size and α = 1 gives classic Laplace smoothing (α < 1 is Lidstone). This guarantees every probability is strictly positive, and it is equivalent to placing a Dirichlet prior on the categorical distribution and taking the MAP estimate — the same regularization-as-prior idea from Module 08.

Practically it also prevents unseen categories at serving time from breaking predictions, and you should work in log-space anyway to avoid numerical underflow from multiplying many small probabilities.

30-second version

Adding a pseudo-count so no conditional probability is zero — otherwise one unseen word zeroes the entire product. It is equivalent to a Dirichlet prior with a MAP estimate.

What’s being tested

Whether you know the mechanism and can connect it to Bayesian priors. The Dirichlet framing and the log-space note are the depth signals.

05LDA vs PCA vs logistic regression.LDAPCAgenerative＋
Strong answer

PCA is unsupervised: it finds orthogonal directions of maximum total variance, ignoring labels, and is used for compression, denoising and visualization. LDA is supervised: it finds the directions maximizing between-class separation relative to within-class scatter, so it preserves discriminability — but it produces at most K−1 components for K classes, and assumes Gaussian class-conditionals with shared covariance.

The practical contrast: PCA’s top component can be a direction along which the classes are completely mixed, if that is where the variance happens to be, while LDA’s is chosen to separate them.

Logistic regression and LDA both yield linear boundaries but arrive differently: LDA is generative, modelling P(x|y) and applying Bayes, which makes it more statistically efficient when the Gaussian assumption holds and lets it handle multi-class naturally; logistic regression is discriminative, fitting P(y|x) directly with no distributional assumption, which makes it more robust when the assumption fails and is why it is the more common default.

30-second version

PCA is unsupervised maximum variance; LDA is supervised maximum class separation, capped at K−1 components; logistic regression is LDA’s discriminative twin — same linear boundary, no Gaussian assumption.

What’s being tested

Whether you can hold three related methods apart cleanly. The “PCA component along which classes are mixed” example makes the supervised/unsupervised distinction concrete.

06KNN has no training phase. Is that an advantage?lazy learningcostvector search＋
Strong answer

It is a trade, and mostly a disadvantage in production. The advantages are real: adding new data needs no retraining, which suits rapidly-changing data; there is no model to fit, so no optimization to tune; and the decision boundary can be arbitrarily complex with no assumption about its form.

The costs are severe: prediction is O(nd) per query with brute-force search, so latency grows with your dataset; the entire training set must be held in memory at serving time; it is highly sensitive to scaling, irrelevant features and the curse of dimensionality; and there is no learned compact representation to inspect.

This “lazy” profile is exactly inverted from every other model in this curriculum, which pays a large one-off training cost for cheap inference — and cheap inference is what production usually needs. What makes it viable are spatial indexes (KD-trees, ball trees) in low dimensions and approximate nearest-neighbour indexes (HNSW, IVF-PQ) in high dimensions, which is precisely the technology behind vector databases in modern retrieval systems.

30-second version

A trade, usually unfavourable: no retraining cost, but O(nd) inference, the whole training set in memory, and acute scaling sensitivity. It is the inverse of every other model — and cheap inference is what production needs.

What’s being tested

Cost awareness across the train/serve boundary, and whether you can connect KNN to contemporary vector search — a common 2026 bridge question.
