Bias–Variance, Splits & Cross-Validation

Universal. Every hyperparameter question is secretly this question — which is why the bias-variance dial is the most reusable framing in the whole curriculum.

Prerequisites · Modules 01–04Study time · 8 h6 interview questionsUniversal
🎯
Your interview goal

Write the decomposition from memory, place any named hyperparameter on the dial instantly, know why random K-fold ruins time series, and explain why a tuned CV score is optimistically biased.

1 · From the ground up

🧩
In plain words

There are exactly two ways a model can be wrong. It can be too simple to represent the truth — it would still be wrong even with infinite data. Or it can be so flexible that it memorises the accidents in your particular sample — it would give a completely different answer on a different sample. The first is bias, the second is variance, and almost every hyperparameter in machine learning is a dial between them. Cross-validation is simply how you find where on that dial you are, without cheating.

Two errors, and a third you cannot touch

Imagine you could rerun your whole project many times, each time collecting a fresh sample of data and refitting. Now look at the predictions all those models make for one particular input.

  • Bias — their average prediction is off from the truth. All your models are wrong in the same direction, because the shape you chose cannot represent the real relationship. More data will not help. A straight line fitted to a curve has high bias.
  • Variance — their predictions are scattered widely around their own average. Each model chased the noise in its own sample. More data does help, because noise averages out. A deep unpruned tree has high variance.
  • Irreducible noise — the target genuinely is not fully determined by your features. No model of any kind can beat this, and mistaking it for bias is how people over-engineer for weeks.
So what: the diagnosis determines the cure, and the two cures are opposites. Treat variance as bias and you make things worse.

Read the diagnosis off two numbers

You do not need a formula. Compare training error with validation error.

  • Training error high, validation error similar → underfitting, dominated by bias. Add capacity: more features, interactions, a more flexible model, less regularisation.
  • Training error very low, validation error much worse → overfitting, dominated by variance. Remove capacity or add data: more regularisation, less depth, more samples, bagging, early stopping.
  • Both low and close → you are near the sweet spot. Stop.
  • Both high and close, and adding capacity does nothing → suspect irreducible noise, or a missing feature, or a mislabelled target.
So what: “my model has low bias and high variance, what do you do?” is a stock interview question, and the answer is this table.

Training error falling is never evidence of progress — it falls no matter what. Only the validation curve tells you anything, and the vertical gap between the two curves is your variance.

Why one split is not enough

Hold out 20% of your data, score on it, and you get a number. Do it again with a different random seed and you get a noticeably different number — sometimes a couple of points of AUC apart on small data. That spread is not a nuisance, it is information about how uncertain your estimate is, and a single split hides it from you entirely.

K-fold cross-validation fixes this: split into k parts, train on k−1 and score on the held-out one, rotate through all k. Every row is used for validation exactly once, and you end up with k scores whose mean is a better estimate and whose spread tells you how much to trust it. Higher k means more training data per fold — so less pessimistic bias — but more compute and more correlated folds. Five or ten is the standard compromise, and stratified folds, which preserve the class ratio in each fold, are the default for classification.

So what: report mean ± standard deviation across folds. A difference between two models smaller than the fold spread is not a difference.

In the top panel every part takes its turn as validation, which is what makes the estimate stable. In the bottom panel validation is always in the future relative to training — the shuffled version of this is the second most common leak in machine learning, right behind fitting a scaler before splitting.

The three ways cross-validation lies to you

K-fold is only valid if the rows are independent and interchangeable. Real data breaks that in three predictable ways, and each has a specific fix.

  • Time. Random folds train on the future to predict the past. Use a forward-chaining split — train on months 1–3, validate on 4; train on 1–4, validate on 5 — and add an embargo gap if labels take time to mature, so a label that only becomes known days later cannot contaminate the adjacent training window.
  • Groups. The same patient, user or device appearing in both the training and validation fold means you are measuring recognition, not generalisation. GroupKFold, keyed on the entity, and the entity should be whatever the model will not have seen in production.
  • Tuning on the same data you report. If you try 200 hyperparameter combinations and report the best CV score, that number is optimistically biased — you have effectively fitted the fold structure. This is the subtle one, and the fix is nested CV: an inner loop selects hyperparameters, an outer loop measures how well “the whole procedure including tuning” generalises.

The one place the classical U-curve is not the whole story

Worth knowing because interviewers now ask it. In heavily over-parameterised models — large neural networks — test error can fall, rise at the interpolation point where the model exactly fits the training data, and then fall again as capacity grows further. That is double descent, and it does not overturn the bias–variance trade-off so much as show that “capacity” and “effective complexity” are not the same thing, because implicit regularisation from the optimiser keeps the very large models' effective complexity in check. For classical tabular models the U-curve remains the operative picture, and saying exactly that is the well-calibrated answer.

Analogy — bias is a rifle with bent sights: tightly grouped shots, all in the wrong place, and more ammunition never helps. Variance is a shaky hand with true sights: shots scattered all around the bullseye, and averaging many shots — which is exactly what bagging does — brings you to the centre. The diagnosis dictates the cure: you fix bent sights by changing the instrument, and a shaky hand by taking more shots and averaging.

3 · Core intuitions

Bias is being wrong on average; variance is being unstable

Bias is error from the model being too rigid to represent the truth — a straight line fitted to a curve is wrong no matter how much data you give it. Variance is error from the model being so sensitive that a different training sample would produce a very different function. A depth-1 tree has high bias and low variance; a fully grown tree has low bias and high variance.

Every hyperparameter is a position on the bias–variance dial

This is the most reusable framing in the curriculum, because interviewers ask it in a hundred disguises.

HyperparameterIncrease it →
λ / alpha (regularization)more bias, less variance
C in SVMless bias, more variance (C is inverse regularization)
tree max_depthless bias, more variance
tree min_samples_leafmore bias, less variance
k in KNNmore bias, less variance
n_estimators in Random Forestvariance ↓, then flat — does not meaningfully overfit
n_estimators in boostingbias ↓, variance ↑ — will overfit, needs early stopping
learning_rate in boostinglarger → faster bias reduction, more variance
polynomial degreeless bias, more variance

Memorize the contrast between the two n_estimators rows. It is asked constantly and is the cleanest one-line proof that you understand bagging versus boosting.

Bagging attacks variance; boosting attacks bias

Bagging averages many low-bias, high-variance models fitted on bootstrap samples, so bias stays roughly the same and variance falls. Boosting adds many high-bias, low-variance models sequentially, each correcting the previous residuals, so bias falls and you must control the resulting variance with shrinkage and early stopping.

The test set is a one-shot resource

Train fits parameters. Validation selects hyperparameters and model families. Test estimates generalization once, at the end. Every time you look at the test set and change something, you leak information into your model choice and your final estimate becomes optimistic. This is why nested CV exists.

Read learning curves to decide what to do next

Plot training and validation error against training-set size. Both high and close together means underfitting — add capacity or better features; more data will not help. Low training error with a large persistent gap means overfitting — regularize, simplify, or add data; here more data will help, and the curve shows roughly how much.

Double descent, stated carefully

In heavily over-parameterized regimes — especially deep networks — test error can fall again after the interpolation threshold, producing a second descent beyond the classical U-curve. It is real and important in modern deep learning, but it is not a licence to ignore overfitting in the tabular models this curriculum covers, where the classical U-curve is what you will actually observe.

4 · Choosing a splitter — which one for which leak

Section 1 settled how many folds (five or ten, trading the estimate's bias against compute and fold correlation). This section settles the harder question: which splitter. Get it wrong and cross-validation still returns a confident number — it is just measuring the wrong thing, and it will be optimistic every time.

Three questions decide it, and you should ask them in this order before writing any cross_val_score:

Is there a time arrow?

If the rows are ordered in time and you will deploy on the future, any splitter that puts a later row in training and an earlier row in validation is training on the future. This overrides everything else — decide it first, because it eliminates most splitters outright.

Are rows independent, or do they cluster into entities?

Several rows per patient, per user, per device, per shop? Then rows are not exchangeable. A random fold will put the same entity on both sides, and the model gets credit for recognising the entity rather than learning the pattern.

Is the target balanced enough for random folds?

With a rare positive class, random folds vary wildly in how many positives they contain — some may contain none at all, which makes recall undefined and the fold spread meaningless.

Your data looks like…UseWhat plain KFold would do instead
Independent rows, roughly balanced targetKFold(shuffle=True)Nothing wrong — this is the base case.
Classification, especially imbalanced or smallStratifiedKFoldFold positive counts swing wildly; a fold with zero positives makes recall undefined and inflates the apparent variance.
Repeated rows per entity (patient, user, device, session)GroupKFold (pass groups=)The same entity lands in train and validation. The model memorises the entity; the score is optimistic and does not survive a new entity.
Imbalanced and groupedStratifiedGroupKFoldYou must have both guarantees. Choosing one splitter gives up the other — this is the case people most often get wrong.
Time-ordered, deployed on the futureTimeSeriesSplit, or walk-forwardTrains on the future to predict the past. The single most damaging split error, and it always flatters the model.
Time-ordered with slow-arriving labels or rolling featuresWalk-forward + embargoEven a correct train/validation ordering leaks if a feature window or a label delay straddles the boundary. The embargo is the gap that removes it.
Time-ordered and entity-grouped (panel data)Group-aware walk-forwardBoth leaks at once. Split by time, then confirm no entity crosses the boundary within the window.
Small n, estimate too noisy to act onRepeatedStratifiedKFoldA single K-fold run gives a mean whose own uncertainty you cannot see. Repeating with different seeds averages the fold assignment away.

The same twelve rows, split four ways — two leak, two do not

The two failures look nothing alike but share one property: both produce a higher score than the model deserves, so neither announces itself. That is why the splitter is chosen from the data's structure up front, and never adjusted afterwards because the number looked disappointing.

⚠️
The splitter is only half of it

Choosing GroupKFold and then fitting your scaler, imputer, encoder or feature selector on the full dataset re-introduces the leak you just removed. Every preprocessing step that learns from data must live inside a Pipeline so it is re-fitted on each training fold alone. Resampling for imbalance belongs inside the fold too — SMOTE applied before splitting synthesises validation rows out of training rows, which is the same crime in a different costume (Module 12).

🔑
The sentence to say in an interview

"Before I pick a splitter I ask whether there is a time arrow, whether rows cluster into entities, and whether the target is balanced enough for random folds. Time beats grouping beats stratification when they conflict, because training on the future is the error that flatters a model most — and whatever I choose, every fitted preprocessing step goes inside the pipeline so the fold boundary is real."

5 · Whiteboard formulas

bias_variance.txt
Expected squared error at a point x, over training sets:

  E[(y - f̂(x))²] = ( Bias[f̂(x)] )² + Var[f̂(x)] + σ²
                     ─────┬─────      ────┬────    ─┬─
              systematic error   sensitivity to    irreducible
              from wrong form     the sample       noise

  Bias[f̂(x)] = E[f̂(x)] - f(x)
  Var[f̂(x)]  = E[( f̂(x) - E[f̂(x)] )²]

σ² is the noise floor: no model, however good, can go below it.

Variance of an average of B correlated models (the bagging identity):
  Var = ρσ² + (1-ρ)·σ²/B
        ───     ────────
    irreducible   →0 as B→∞
    correlation
    floor

That last identity is the mathematical reason Random Forest randomizes features: raising B only helps down to the ρσ² floor, so improving further means reducing ρ by decorrelating the trees. Being able to state it earns real credit in Module 16.

6 · Hands-on drill

  1. Fit polynomial regressions of degree 1 to 15 on a small noisy sample; plot training and test error against degree and produce the U-curve yourself.
  2. Plot learning curves for a high-bias and a high-variance model on the same data; confirm you can identify which is which from the curves alone.
  3. Take a time-ordered dataset and score the same model with random K-fold and with TimeSeriesSplit. Record the gap — that number is your answer to the leakage question.
  4. Implement nested CV manually and compare its estimate against a single tuned-CV estimate on the same data. Note the optimism.
⚠️
Gotchas that fail candidates
  • Random K-fold on time series. Trains on the future to predict the past.
  • Not stratifying a K-fold on an imbalanced target, so some folds have too few positives for a stable estimate.
  • Tuning on the test set and then reporting the test score as unbiased.
  • Reporting a single CV number with no spread. 0.82 ± 0.09 is a very different result from 0.82 ± 0.01.
  • Claiming more data always helps. Under high bias, it does not.
  • Confusing the effect of n_estimators in Random Forest versus in boosting.

7 · Interview questions

6 questions
01Write and explain the bias–variance decomposition.derivationfundamentals＋
Strong answer

For squared error, expected error at a point decomposes as bias² + variance + irreducible noise. Bias is E[f̂(x)] - f(x), the systematic error from the model class being too restrictive to represent the truth. Variance is E[(f̂(x) - E[f̂(x)])²], the error from sensitivity to the particular training sample. σ² is noise in the data-generating process — a floor no model can beat. Crucially, the expectation is over training sets, which is the part candidates usually omit and the part that makes “variance” meaningful: it asks how much your fitted function would move if you had drawn a different sample of the same size.

30-second version

bias² + variance + irreducible noise. Bias is systematic error from too rigid a model class; variance is sensitivity to the sample; σ² is the noise floor — and the expectation is over training sets.

What’s being tested

Whether your understanding is mathematical or slogan-level. Saying “the expectation is over training sets” is the marker of a genuine answer.

Likely follow-up: “Does this hold for 0-1 classification loss?” — not in this clean additive form; the decomposition is exact for squared error. Analogues exist but are messier. The intuition transfers, the algebra does not.
02Your model has 99% train accuracy and 71% test accuracy. What do you do?overfittingdebuggingplaybook＋
Strong answer

A 28-point gap: high variance, overfitting. I would address it in order of cost. (1) Verify the evaluation first — is the split leaking or unrepresentative, is the test set too small to trust? (2) Reduce capacity: shallower trees, higher min_samples_leaf, fewer features, lower polynomial degree. (3) Add regularization: L1/L2, dropout, subsampling for boosting. (4) Early stopping on a validation set. (5) Ensemble to average out variance. (6) Get more training data if feasible. (7) Simplify features — aggressive feature counts on limited rows are a common cause.

I would plot the learning curve before choosing between (6) and the rest, because the curve distinguishes “needs data” from “needs less model”.

30-second version

High variance. Check the evaluation first, then reduce capacity, regularize, early-stop, ensemble, and consider more data — using a learning curve to decide whether data will actually help.

What’s being tested

Whether you have an ordered, cost-aware playbook, and whether you check the evaluation before changing the model.

03Does adding more trees to a Random Forest cause overfitting? What about gradient boosting?ensemblesbaggingboostingtrap＋
Strong answer

Random Forest: essentially no. Each tree is fitted independently on a bootstrap sample and prediction is an average, so adding trees reduces variance and then plateaus. Test error flattens; it does not rise meaningfully. So n_estimators is a compute and latency decision, not a regularization decision.

Gradient boosting: yes, definitively. Each tree is fitted to the current ensemble’s residuals, so the ensemble keeps reducing training error and will eventually fit noise. n_estimators in boosting is a genuine bias–variance knob and must be chosen by early stopping on a validation set, interacting with the learning rate — a lower rate needs more trees.

30-second version

Random Forest: no, it plateaus, because trees are independent and averaged. Boosting: yes, because each tree fits the current residuals — so early stopping is mandatory.

What’s being tested

The cleanest single discriminator between candidates who understand ensembles and candidates who have memorized names. Get this one perfect.

Likely follow-up: “Then why does Random Forest have a max_depth parameter?” — individual tree depth still controls each tree’s variance and the correlation between trees, and limiting it saves compute. But unlike boosting, fully grown trees are usually fine because averaging handles the variance.
04How do you cross-validate a time-series model?time seriesvalidationtrap＋
Strong answer

Never with random K-fold — that trains on future data to predict the past, which is temporal leakage and produces an estimate you cannot reproduce in production. Use forward-chaining or walk-forward validation: sort by time, train on [0..t] and validate on (t..t+h], sliding or expanding the window across several folds. sklearn’s TimeSeriesSplit implements the expanding-window version.

Three refinements that mark a strong answer: insert a gap or embargo between train and validation equal to your label-availability delay, so a label taking 30 days to materialize cannot be learned from data one day before validation; make the validation horizon match the real prediction horizon; and if entities also repeat over time, combine group and time constraints.

30-second version

Forward-chaining, never random K-fold. Train on the past, validate on the future — with an embargo gap matching your label delay, and a validation horizon matching the real prediction horizon.

What’s being tested

Whether you can handle non-i.i.d. data. The embargo point is the senior differentiator; most candidates stop at “use TimeSeriesSplit”.

05What is nested cross-validation and when do you need it?validationmodel selection＋
Strong answer

Two loops: the inner loop selects hyperparameters, the outer loop estimates generalization of the whole procedure including that selection. You need it whenever you want an unbiased performance estimate on a dataset too small to afford a separate untouched test set.

The reason is that selecting the best of 200 configurations by CV score means the winning score is partly luck — you took a maximum over noisy estimates — so it is optimistically biased. Nested CV re-runs the entire selection inside each outer fold, so the reported number reflects “how well does my procedure do”, not “how well did my luckiest configuration do”. Cost is the product of the two loops, so it is a small-data technique; with plenty of data a clean three-way split is simpler and adequate.

30-second version

An inner loop tunes, an outer loop scores the whole tuning procedure. Needed because picking the best of many noisy CV scores is itself optimistic — so the tuned CV score is not an unbiased estimate.

What’s being tested

Whether you understand that model selection itself overfits. A strong mid-to-senior signal.

06Cross-validation says 0.84 ± 0.11 across five folds. Is the model good?variancedebuggingtrap＋
Strong answer

You cannot tell yet, and the ±0.11 is the story. That spread means folds range roughly 0.73–0.95, so the estimate is too unstable to act on. Likely causes: the dataset is small; the target is imbalanced and folds are not stratified, so positive counts vary wildly per fold; there is group or temporal structure making some folds much harder; or a few high-leverage outliers dominate whichever fold they land in.

Next steps: switch to stratified and/or repeated K-fold to stabilize the estimate, inspect per-fold scores for a single bad fold versus uniform noise, check fold-wise class balance, and check whether the bad fold corresponds to a specific time period or entity group.

30-second version

Unknown — the ±0.11 means folds span roughly 0.73 to 0.95, which is too unstable to act on. Investigate stratification, group and temporal structure, and outlier leverage before judging the number.

What’s being tested

Whether you read uncertainty or just the headline number. Many candidates answer “0.84 is decent” and lose the round.

2026 additions. Validation-design questions have overtaken bias–variance definitions: interviewers hand you a leaky evaluation setup and score whether you spot it, and they now ask about double descent because of deep learning's influence.
07Design the validation scheme for “will this subscriber churn next month”. Assume you have three years of monthly snapshots.2026validation designtemporal＋
Strong answer

Three properties of this data decide everything, and I would name them before designing anything: it is temporal, it has repeated entities — one subscriber appears in 36 rows — and the label has a horizon, since “churns next month” is only observable a month later.

So the scheme is: forward-chaining splits by calendar time, with an embargo, grouped by subscriber. Concretely, train on months 1–24 and validate on month 26, then train on 1–25 and validate on 27, and so on — the one-month gap is the embargo, and it exists because a label for month 25 is not knowable at the end of month 25, so including month 25 in training would let a not-yet-observable outcome influence the model. On top of that, no subscriber may appear in both the training window and the validation window for the same fold; where a subscriber straddles the boundary I would drop their boundary rows rather than let the model recognise the individual.

The details that make it right rather than merely defensible. Fix the prediction horizon explicitly — every feature must be computed as of the snapshot date and no window may extend past it, which rules out any aggregate built with a naive full-table groupby. Match the fold's class balance to reality rather than stratifying, because in a temporal split the churn rate itself changes over time and that change is signal I want the evaluation to feel. Report a score per fold, not just the mean, because a downward trend across folds is concept drift showing up in the evaluation and is far more informative than the average. And keep the final period completely untouched as a single time-forward test set that nothing — no tuning, no feature selection, no threshold search — is allowed to see until the end.

30-second version

Forward-chaining by calendar month, never shuffled, with a one-month embargo because the label is not observable at the training cut-off, and grouped so a subscriber never spans train and validation. Every feature computed as of the snapshot date. Report per-fold scores to expose drift, and hold the final period back completely untouched.

What’s being tested

Whether you can compose the three constraints — time, groups, and label horizon — rather than applying just one. Most candidates get the temporal split and miss the embargo and the repeated-subscriber problem.

Likely follow-up: “Should the training window keep expanding, or slide?” — expanding uses all history and gives the most data, which is right when the relationship is stable. Sliding weights recency and adapts faster, which is right under concept drift. Decide it empirically: try both and compare per-fold scores. If a 12-month sliding window beats all three years, that is direct evidence of drift and it also tells you your retraining cadence.
08Deep networks with far more parameters than data points still generalise. Does that break the bias–variance trade-off?2026double descenttheory＋
Strong answer

It complicates the classical picture without invalidating the decomposition. What is observed is double descent: as capacity grows, test error falls, then rises to a peak right at the interpolation threshold — the point where the model has just enough capacity to fit the training data exactly — and then falls again as capacity grows well beyond that.

The reconciliation is that the number of parameters is a bad proxy for effective complexity. The bias–variance decomposition is a mathematical identity and remains true; what fails is the assumption that parameter count moves you monotonically along it. Two things explain the second descent. Near the interpolation threshold there is exactly one way to fit the data, and it is a badly behaved one — the fit is forced to contort, so variance spikes. Beyond it there are many ways to fit the data, and the optimiser does not pick one at random: gradient descent has an implicit bias towards minimum-norm, smoother solutions, so more capacity gives it more room to find a well-behaved interpolator rather than more room to misbehave.

The calibrated conclusion, which is the part that gets scored: for the tabular models this curriculum is about, the classical U-curve is the operative picture and I would still tune complexity against a validation curve. Double descent matters for over-parameterised neural networks, and its practical lesson is that early stopping on a validation curve remains correct while “fewer parameters is safer” does not generalise.

30-second version

No — the decomposition is an identity and stays true. What breaks is using parameter count as a proxy for effective complexity. Test error peaks at the interpolation threshold, where there is only one contorted way to fit the data, then falls again because the optimiser's implicit bias picks minimum-norm interpolators. For tabular boosting the classical U-curve still governs.

What’s being tested

Whether you can hold a modern result and a classical framework at once without over-claiming either. Candidates who say “bias–variance is outdated” fail; so do candidates who have never heard of double descent. Scoping it to deep nets is the calibrated answer.

Likely follow-up: “Practically, does this change how you train anything?” — for tabular work, no: I still use early stopping against a validation set, and I still expect a U. For deep models it argues against reflexively shrinking a network that overfits — more capacity plus regularisation often beats less capacity — and it makes the region near the interpolation threshold the one place you should not park your model.
09Cross-validation says 0.88, the untouched test set says 0.79. Same data, same code. Explain the gap.2026debuggingtuning bias＋
Strong answer

Several causes produce this pattern, and I would rank them by how often they are the real one.

  1. You tuned on the CV score. This is the most likely explanation and the most-missed. If you evaluated a few hundred hyperparameter combinations and reported the best mean CV score, that number is the maximum of many noisy estimates, so it is optimistically biased by construction — you have partially fitted the fold structure itself. The honest number requires nested CV, or exactly what you did: a truly untouched test set. In that reading, 0.79 is not the anomaly; 0.88 was never an unbiased estimate.
  2. The test set is not exchangeable with the training data. If it is a later time period — which it should be — then part of the drop is real drift, and that is useful information rather than a bug. Check by scoring the model on each time slice of the training period and looking for a trend.
  3. Leakage inside the CV but not the test. If a preprocessing step was fitted on the whole training set rather than inside each fold, the CV folds were mildly contaminated while the held-out test set was not — producing exactly this gap.
  4. Small test set. A 0.09 difference on 500 rows may be within sampling noise. Compute a confidence interval before treating it as a finding; the fold-to-fold standard deviation is your reference for what a real difference looks like.
  5. Group structure respected in one and not the other. If CV was plain k-fold on data with repeated entities and the test split was by entity, the CV number is inflated by recognition.

What I would say I do about it: report 0.79 as the expected performance, because a number from data that influenced no decision is the only trustworthy one, and use the gap itself as the diagnostic for which of the causes above applies.

30-second version

Most likely tuning bias — the best of many noisy CV scores is optimistically biased by construction, so 0.88 was never unbiased and 0.79 is the real number. Then check whether the test set is a later period, which makes part of the drop genuine drift; whether preprocessing was fitted outside the fold; whether the test set is simply too small; and whether group structure was handled the same way in both.

What’s being tested

Whether you know that a selected maximum is a biased estimator. This is the specific insight nested CV exists for, and it is the difference between a candidate who runs cross-validation and one who understands what it estimates.

Likely follow-up: “How much optimism should you expect from tuning?” — it scales with how many configurations you tried and how noisy each fold estimate is, and shrinks as the validation set grows. On small data with hundreds of trials it can easily be a few points; with a handful of trials on a large validation set it is negligible. Practical mitigations: search fewer, better-chosen configurations, prefer the simplest configuration within one standard error of the best rather than the outright best, and keep a locked test set.
