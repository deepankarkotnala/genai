Gradient Descent & Optimization

How models actually learn — and the bridge to deep learning. The function-space framing at the end of this module is the cleanest possible setup for boosting.

1 · From the ground up

🧩
In plain words

Most losses have no formula for their minimum, so you find it by walking. At your current weights, work out which direction makes the loss go up fastest — that is the gradient — and take a step the other way. Repeat. The two things that can go wrong are the two things interviewers ask about: how big a step (too small and you never arrive, too big and you bounce out), and how much data you look at per step (all of it is accurate but slow, one row is fast but noisy). Every optimiser from momentum to AdamW is an answer to one of those two questions.

Blindfolded on a hillside

You cannot see the valley floor, but you can feel the slope under your feet. So you feel around, find the steepest downhill direction, step that way, and feel again. That is the entire algorithm: w ← w − η∇J(w). The gradient ∇J is the steepest uphill direction, so you subtract it. The step size η — the learning rate — is how far you commit before feeling again.

One property makes this work at all: the gradient shrinks as you approach a minimum, because the surface flattens. So the algorithm naturally takes smaller steps as it converges, without anyone telling it to. At the exact minimum the gradient is zero and the update stops — which is the same condition Module 06 solved algebraically for linear regression.

So what: gradient descent is not a rival to the normal equation. It is what you use when there is no normal equation, which is nearly always.

The learning rate has three regimes, and you can diagnose which you are in

Plot the loss against iteration and read it off directly. Too small: the loss falls smoothly but crawls, and you run out of patience before you run out of distance. About right: a steep initial drop then a graceful flattening. Too large: the loss oscillates, or rises, or becomes NaN — you are overshooting the valley and climbing the far wall each step. There is a fourth, sneakier case: marginally too large, where the loss falls but noisily and plateaus higher than it should, because you are bouncing around the basin rather than settling in it.

So what: “my model won't converge” is a learning-rate question 80% of the time, and the loss curve tells you which way to move it. The standard fix is a schedule: start large to cover ground, decay to settle.

The contours are stretched ellipses, not circles, because the two features have different scales — so the steepest-descent direction does not point at the minimum and the path zig-zags. That is the real reason to standardise features before gradient descent: it makes the bowl round, and a round bowl converges in far fewer steps.

How much data per step: the batch-size trade-off

The gradient is an average over training examples. You get to choose how many you average.

  • Batch (all rows) — the exact gradient, a smooth path, deterministic. But every step costs a full pass over the data, and you cannot start until you have all of it in memory.
  • Stochastic (one row) — an extremely noisy estimate of the gradient, so the path staggers. Enormously cheaper per step, and the noise is useful: it can shake the iterate out of a shallow local trap and it acts as a mild regulariser.
  • Mini-batch (32–512 rows) — what everybody actually uses. Enough averaging to be stable, small enough to be cheap, and the right shape for hardware that likes matrix multiplications.

The subtlety worth stating: batch gradient descent converges to a precise point, while SGD converges to a region around the minimum and keeps jittering inside it — which is why a decaying learning rate is not optional for SGD. It is how you shrink the jitter to zero.

So what: “batch versus stochastic versus mini-batch” is a near-guaranteed question, and the framing that scores is noise-versus-cost, plus the observation that the noise has a benefit.

Momentum, and then adaptive rates

Two problems remain after choosing a batch size, and each has a well-known fix.

Problem one: the path zig-zags in a narrow valley, because the steepest direction points across the valley rather than along it. Momentum fixes this by accumulating a velocity — an exponentially-weighted average of past gradients. Oscillating components cancel out across steps while the consistent downhill component accumulates, so you accelerate along the valley and damp the side-to-side motion. The physical intuition is exact: a ball with mass rather than a hiker taking discrete decisions.

Problem two: one learning rate does not suit every weight. A weight attached to a rare feature gets a gradient signal seldom and should move further when it does. Adaptive methods give each weight its own effective rate by dividing by a running measure of that weight's past gradient magnitudes — AdaGrad accumulates forever and therefore stalls, RMSProp uses a decaying average and does not, and Adam combines RMSProp's per-weight scaling with momentum. AdamW is the one to name: it decouples weight decay from the adaptive scaling, because in plain Adam an L2 penalty gets divided by the same running gradient magnitude and so is applied unevenly across weights, which is not what you asked for. That fix is why AdamW is the default in modern deep learning.

Two things people get wrong about the landscape

Local minima are not the main problem in high dimensions — saddle points are. At a saddle the gradient is zero, but it is a minimum along some directions and a maximum along others. For a random critical point in d dimensions, needing all d curvatures to be positive is vanishingly unlikely, so most zero-gradient points are saddles. Momentum and gradient noise both help you escape them; that is another argument for SGD.

Gradient boosting is gradient descent — in function space rather than parameter space. Each new tree is fitted to the negative gradient of the loss with respect to the current predictions, and the learning rate is the shrinkage parameter. Saying this connects Module 09 to Module 17 and it is a favourite interview bridge, because it explains why boosting can optimise any differentiable loss.

Analogy — descending a foggy mountain by feel. Learning rate is your stride length: shuffle and you camp on the slope overnight; leap and you end up in the next valley. Batch size is how long you spend testing the ground before committing — a careful survey each time is accurate but slow, one quick prod is noisy but you take ten times as many steps. Momentum is being on a bicycle instead of on foot: you stop wobbling side to side in the gully and start rolling down it. And a saddle point is a mountain pass — flat under your feet, downhill if you look the right way, which is why zero gradient is not the same as “arrived”.

3 · Core intuitions

You are walking downhill in the dark, feeling the slope

The gradient tells you the steepest uphill direction at your current position; you step the other way. The learning rate is your stride length. Too short and you take forever; too long and you leap over the valley and end up higher than you started.

Batch versus stochastic is a compute-versus-noise trade

Batch gradient descent uses all n samples per step: the gradient is exact, the path smooth, and each step costs O(nd) — infeasible when n is huge. SGD uses one sample: each step is O(d) and extremely fast, but the gradient is a noisy estimate so the path jitters and it never quite settles without a decaying learning rate. Mini-batch — typically 32 to 512 — is the practical answer: enough samples for a low-variance gradient, small enough to be fast, and shaped to exploit vectorized hardware. The noise in SGD is not purely a cost either: it helps escape sharp local minima and saddle points in non-convex problems.

Unscaled features make bowls into canyons

If one feature ranges 0–1 and another 0–100,000, the loss surface’s contours are extremely elongated ellipses. The gradient at most points is nearly perpendicular to the direction you actually need to travel, so you zig-zag across the canyon making tiny progress along it. Standardizing makes the contours near-circular and the gradient points roughly at the optimum. Formally, convergence rate depends on the condition number of the Hessian, and scaling improves it. This is the reason gradient-based methods need scaling while trees do not.

Momentum is a heavy ball

Instead of stepping purely by the current gradient, accumulate a velocity: v ← βv + ∇J, then w ← w - ηv. In consistent directions the velocity builds and you accelerate; in oscillating directions successive gradients cancel and the oscillation is damped. It directly attacks the canyon-zig-zag problem, and β = 0.9 is the near-universal default. Nesterov’s variant evaluates the gradient at the look-ahead position w - ηβv, correcting the step slightly earlier.

Adaptive methods give each parameter its own learning rate

AdaGrad divides the step by the square root of accumulated squared gradients, so rarely-updated parameters get big steps — great for sparse data, but the accumulator only grows, so the learning rate decays to zero and learning stalls. RMSProp fixes that with an exponentially-decaying average instead of a full sum. Adam combines RMSProp’s per-parameter scaling with momentum, plus bias-correction for the two moving averages’ zero initialization.

AdamW fixes a subtle but real bug in Adam’s handling of L2: applying weight decay through the gradient means the adaptive scaling also rescales the decay, so it is not true L2 regularization. AdamW decouples weight decay from the gradient and applies it directly to the weights, which measurably improves generalization and is why it is standard for transformer training.

Gradient descent and gradient boosting are the same idea in different spaces

Gradient descent takes steps in parameter space: w ← w - η∇_w J. Gradient boosting takes steps in function space: F ← F - η∇_F J, where the “step” is a new weak learner fitted to approximate the negative functional gradient. That is exactly why each boosting tree is fitted to the negative gradient of the loss with respect to the current predictions, and why the boosting learning rate is called shrinkage and plays the same role as η. Stating this connection is a strong senior signal and the cleanest setup for Module 17.

Saddle points, not local minima, are the real problem in high dimensions

For a critical point to be a local minimum, every eigenvalue of the Hessian must be positive; in d dimensions that becomes increasingly unlikely, so most critical points are saddles. This is why SGD’s noise and momentum are so valuable in non-convex optimization — they help you slide off saddles that a purely deterministic method could stall on.

5 · Hands-on drill

  1. Implement batch GD, SGD and mini-batch GD for linear regression from scratch. Plot loss versus iteration for all three on the same axes and observe the noise/smoothness trade.
  2. Run the same problem with η too small, about right, and too large, and produce a plot of divergence. Keep that picture in mind for the debugging question.
  3. Run gradient descent on unscaled versus standardized features and count the iterations to convergence. The ratio is your evidence for the scaling question.
  4. Implement momentum and Adam by hand and compare convergence on an ill-conditioned quadratic with condition number around 1,000.
  5. Verify empirically that logistic regression’s log-loss surface has a single minimum from many random initializations, while an MSE-with-sigmoid objective reaches different optima.
⚠️
Gotchas that fail candidates
  • Saying gradient descent finds the global minimum. Only for convex objectives — linear and logistic regression are convex; neural nets and MSE-with-sigmoid are not.
  • Getting the batch-size / learning-rate direction wrong. Larger batches give lower-variance gradients, which generally supports a larger learning rate.
  • Confusing an epoch with an iteration. An epoch is a full pass; an iteration is one parameter update.
  • Not being able to explain why scaling matters in terms of the loss surface.
  • Saying Adam is always best. For convex problems L-BFGS often converges faster with no learning-rate tuning; for well-tuned vision training, SGD with momentum still frequently generalizes better.
  • Not knowing what AdamW actually changes. “Adam with weight decay” is incomplete — the point is decoupled decay.

6 · Interview questions

6 questions
01Explain gradient descent, and compare batch, stochastic and mini-batch.fundamentalsSGDhardware＋
Strong answer

Gradient descent minimizes a differentiable loss by repeatedly stepping in the negative gradient direction: w ← w - η∇J(w). The gradient points uphill most steeply, so its negative is the locally fastest descent direction, and η controls the step size.

Batch computes the gradient over all n samples: exact gradient, smooth monotone descent for suitable η, but O(nd) per step and it needs the whole dataset per update, so it does not scale. Stochastic uses one sample: O(d) per step, rapid early progress, supports online learning, but the gradient is noisy, the trajectory oscillates, and it needs a decaying learning rate to converge rather than hover. Mini-batch (32–512) is the standard compromise: gradient variance falls roughly as 1/batch_size, updates are frequent, and the batch matrix multiply maps efficiently onto GPU and SIMD hardware.

Worth adding that SGD’s noise is sometimes a feature — it helps escape saddle points and sharp minima in non-convex problems.

30-second version

Step against the gradient with stride η. Batch is exact but O(nd) per step; SGD is O(d) but noisy and needs a decaying rate; mini-batch balances gradient variance against speed and fits vectorized hardware.

What’s being tested

Fundamentals plus practical awareness. The hardware point and “noise as a feature” both signal real experience.

Likely follow-up: “How does batch size interact with learning rate?” — bigger batches mean lower-variance gradients, so you can afford a larger rate; the common heuristic is to scale the rate linearly with batch size, usually with a warmup period to avoid early instability.
02Your training loss oscillates wildly and sometimes increases. What is wrong?debugginglearning rate＋
Strong answer

Most likely the learning rate is too high, so steps overshoot the minimum and can climb the far wall of the valley. I would check in this order:

  1. Lower η by 10× and see if the oscillation resolves — the cheapest decisive test.
  2. Check feature scaling; unscaled features create an ill-conditioned surface where a rate that is fine in one direction diverges in another.
  3. If using SGD with a very small batch, some oscillation is expected from gradient noise — increase the batch size or add momentum, which damps oscillation by construction.
  4. Look for exploding gradients, especially with deep or recurrent models; apply gradient clipping.
  5. Check for numerical issues — NaNs from log(0), division by zero, unclipped probabilities in a hand-rolled log-loss.
  6. Confirm the loss is averaged rather than summed, since a summed loss effectively multiplies your learning rate by the batch size.

Then use a learning-rate schedule or an adaptive optimizer to stabilize.

30-second version

Almost certainly too high a learning rate. Drop it 10×, then check scaling, batch size, gradient explosion, NaNs, and whether the loss is summed rather than averaged.

What’s being tested

Structured debugging with an ordered, cheap-first checklist. The summed-versus-averaged loss detail is a nice practitioner touch.

03Does gradient descent always find the global minimum?convexitysaddle pointstrap＋
Strong answer

Only if the objective is convex, in which case any local minimum is global and gradient descent with an appropriate step size converges to it. Linear regression with squared error and logistic regression with log-loss are both convex, so yes for those. Neural networks, K-Means and sigmoid-plus-MSE are non-convex, so there is no guarantee — you converge to a critical point that depends on initialization.

A crucial nuance for high dimensions: the practical obstacle is usually not local minima but saddle points, because a critical point is only a local minimum if every Hessian eigenvalue is positive, which becomes improbable as dimension grows. That is why SGD’s noise and momentum matter — they help escape saddles.

And empirically, in over-parameterized deep networks most local minima reached in practice have similar loss values, which is why non-convexity turns out to be much less damaging than the theory suggests.

30-second version

Only for convex objectives — true for linear and logistic regression, not for neural nets. And in high dimensions the real obstacle is saddle points rather than local minima, which is why SGD noise and momentum help.

What’s being tested

Precision about convexity plus modern understanding. The saddle-point point elevates this well above “no, it can get stuck in local minima”.

04Compare SGD with momentum, RMSProp, Adam and AdamW.optimizersAdamWweight decay＋
Strong answer

Each fixes a specific deficiency in the previous one.

SGD with momentum accumulates a velocity v ← βv + ∇J, accelerating along consistently-signed directions and damping oscillation across a narrow valley; simple, memory-light, and still best-generalizing in many well-tuned vision setups, but it needs careful learning-rate scheduling.

AdaGrad — worth naming as the ancestor — scales each parameter’s step by the inverse square root of accumulated squared gradients; excellent for sparse features, but the accumulator only grows, so the effective rate decays to zero and learning halts. RMSProp replaces the sum with an exponentially-decaying average, keeping adaptivity without terminal decay.

Adam combines RMSProp’s scaling with momentum and adds bias correction for the two moving averages’ zero initialization; robust to hyperparameters and the default for transformers.

AdamW decouples weight decay from the gradient: in Adam, an L2 term added to the gradient also gets divided by √v̂, so effective regularization varies per parameter and is not true weight decay. AdamW applies w ← w - ηλw directly to the weights instead, which improves generalization and is the standard for large-model training today.

30-second version

Momentum accelerates consistent directions; AdaGrad adapts per parameter but decays to zero; RMSProp fixes that with a decaying average; Adam adds momentum plus bias correction; AdamW decouples weight decay from the gradient.

What’s being tested

Whether you understand optimizers as a chain of fixes rather than a list of names. Correctly stating what AdamW decouples is the single highest-value detail.

05How is gradient boosting related to gradient descent?boostingfunction spacetrap＋
Strong answer

It is gradient descent in function space rather than parameter space. Ordinary gradient descent parameterizes the model and steps w ← w - η∇_w J. Gradient boosting treats the model itself as the thing being optimized: at each stage it computes the negative gradient of the loss with respect to the current predictions, -∂L/∂F(xᵢ) — the pseudo-residuals — then fits a weak learner to approximate that negative gradient and adds it: F_m = F_{m-1} + η·h_m.

So the new tree plays the role of the descent direction, and the boosting learning rate (shrinkage) plays exactly the role of η.

Two clarifying consequences: with squared error the negative gradient is literally the residual y - F(x), which is why the intuitive “fit the residuals” description works for regression; but with log-loss it is y - p, which is why you must say “negative gradient” rather than “residual” to be correct in general. XGBoost extends the analogy to second order using a Taylor expansion with both gradient and Hessian, making it the function-space analogue of Newton’s method.

30-second version

Boosting is gradient descent in function space: each tree approximates the negative gradient of the loss with respect to current predictions, and shrinkage is the step size. It equals the residual only under squared error.

What’s being tested

Whether you see the unifying principle. One of the highest-signal questions in the ML interview canon, and the “residuals only for MSE” nuance is what marks a genuinely deep answer.

06Why does feature scaling speed up gradient descent, when it does not affect a tree at all?scalingcondition numbertrees＋
Strong answer

Gradient descent’s convergence rate depends on the condition number of the loss surface — roughly the ratio of largest to smallest curvature. With features on very different scales the contours are extremely elongated ellipses, so the gradient at most points is nearly perpendicular to the direction of the minimum and the trajectory zig-zags across the narrow valley. Any single learning rate is then simultaneously too large for the steep direction and too small for the flat one. Standardizing makes the contours roughly circular, so the gradient points near the optimum and convergence is fast.

A decision tree, by contrast, evaluates splits as threshold comparisons within a single feature and ranks candidate thresholds — an operation invariant to any monotone rescaling — so scaling changes nothing about the tree it builds.

Same reason distance-based methods (KNN, K-Means, SVM, PCA) need scaling: they combine features into a single geometric quantity, and trees never do.

30-second version

Scaling improves the loss surface’s condition number, so the gradient points at the optimum instead of zig-zagging across a canyon. Trees rank thresholds within one feature, which is invariant to monotone rescaling.

What’s being tested

Whether you can explain the mechanism rather than repeating the rule. The “trees rank thresholds within one feature” framing is the clean way to say it.

07Training loss goes to NaN after 300 steps. Walk me through the debugging.2026debuggingstability＋
Strong answer

NaN means an arithmetic impossibility happened — an overflow, or log(0), or 0/0 — so I would look for the four places those arise, cheapest first.

  1. Learning rate too high. The most common cause by a wide margin: weights overshoot, grow, the loss grows with them, and one step overflows to infinity, after which everything is NaN. The diagnostic is to log the gradient norm and the max absolute weight per step — if either is climbing geometrically before the failure, this is it. Fix: drop the learning rate by 10×, add warmup, and clip gradients by norm.
  2. A logarithm of zero in the loss. Log-loss computes log p, and if the sigmoid or softmax saturates to exactly 0 or 1 in floating point, that is −inf. This is why you use a framework's from_logits / logsumexp-stabilised loss rather than composing a sigmoid and a log yourself, and why libraries clip probabilities to [ε, 1−ε].
  3. Bad data. A NaN or inf already in the input features or labels, or a divide-by-zero in a feature transform — a ratio whose denominator is zero for a few rows, or log of a non-positive value. Assert finiteness on every batch; this is a one-line check that saves hours.
  4. Unscaled features. A feature with values in the millions produces enormous gradients on its weight, so even a sane learning rate is effectively enormous for that one coordinate. Standardising fixes it, and this is the case that looks like a learning-rate problem but is not.

The reason “after 300 steps” rather than immediately is itself a clue: it points to something that grows — divergence or saturation — rather than to bad data, which would usually fail on the first batch containing the offending row. Practically I would set the framework to raise on non-finite values so the failure surfaces at the exact operation instead of propagating silently.

30-second version

Overflow, log of zero, or bad input. Log the gradient norm and max weight per step — geometric growth means the learning rate is too high, so drop it 10×, add warmup and clip gradients. Otherwise check for log(0) from a saturated sigmoid, which is why you use the from-logits loss, then assert finite inputs, then check feature scaling. Failing at step 300 rather than step 1 points to something growing, not bad data.

What’s being tested

Systematic debugging with instrumentation rather than guessing. Logging the gradient norm is the specific habit that separates people who have trained models from people who have read about it.

Likely follow-up: “The loss does not go to NaN, it just plateaus immediately at a constant.” — different failure, so different checks. Usually the learning rate is far too small, or the gradient is genuinely zero because of dead ReLUs or a saturated output, or the labels are shuffled relative to the features so there is no learnable signal. The decisive test is to overfit a single batch of ten rows on purpose: if the model cannot drive the loss to nearly zero on ten examples, the bug is in the model or the data plumbing, not in the optimisation.
08Which optimiser would you reach for, and when would you not use Adam?2026optimiserdefaults＋
Strong answer

AdamW is my default for anything neural, with a warmup-then-cosine-decay schedule. It converges fast without much tuning, handles sparse gradients well because the per-weight scaling gives rare features a larger effective step, and the W matters: in plain Adam an L2 penalty added to the loss gets divided by the same running gradient magnitude as everything else, so the amount of decay each weight receives depends on its gradient history rather than on your intent. AdamW applies the decay directly to the weights instead, which is why it is the modern default and why the distinction is worth naming unprompted.

Where I would not use Adam. For a convex problem — logistic regression, linear SVM, ridge — none of this applies: use L-BFGS or a purpose-built coordinate-descent solver, which converge in far fewer iterations by exploiting curvature, and note that scikit-learn's defaults already do this. For convolutional vision models, plain SGD with momentum plus a good schedule has historically generalised slightly better than Adam, at the cost of more tuning — the usual explanation being that Adam's per-weight scaling weakens the implicit regularisation that SGD's uniform noise provides. And for gradient-boosted trees the question does not arise at all: the optimiser is the boosting procedure itself, and the learning rate is the shrinkage parameter.

The practical framing I would end on: the schedule usually matters more than the choice of optimiser. AdamW with warmup and cosine decay beats a carefully-chosen optimiser at a constant learning rate, and warmup specifically exists because Adam's second-moment estimate is unreliable in the first few hundred steps, which is exactly when a large step can destroy the run.

30-second version

AdamW with warmup and cosine decay for anything neural — the W matters because plain Adam divides the weight decay by the same running gradient magnitude, so decay lands unevenly. Not Adam for convex problems, where L-BFGS or coordinate descent is far better and is already the sklearn default. Plain SGD with momentum still edges it for convolutional vision models. And the schedule usually matters more than the optimiser.

What’s being tested

Whether you know current defaults and can justify them mechanically. Knowing precisely what AdamW decouples, and that convex problems should not be using SGD at all, are the two differentiators.

Likely follow-up: “Why does warmup help?” — Adam divides by a running estimate of the squared gradient, and at step one that estimate is built from almost no data, so it is both noisy and biased. Dividing by a noisy small number produces enormous effective steps in the first few iterations, which can push the weights somewhere unrecoverable. Warmup keeps the actual step small until the second-moment estimate has stabilised. It also interacts with batch size: larger batches mean fewer, better-informed steps and generally tolerate a higher peak learning rate.
09Explain gradient boosting as gradient descent. Where is the gradient, and where is the learning rate?2026boostingconnection＋
Strong answer

The move is to change what you are taking the derivative with respect to. In ordinary gradient descent you differentiate the loss with respect to the parameters. In boosting you differentiate it with respect to the predictions themselves — treating the vector of current predictions F(xᵢ) as the thing being optimised, one coordinate per training row.

So: at each round, compute gᵢ = ∂L/∂F(xᵢ) for every row. The direction that would most reduce the loss is −g. But you cannot simply add −g to your predictions, because −g is defined only at your training points and you need a function that generalises to new inputs. So you fit a small tree to approximate −g, and add that tree's output instead. That is one step of gradient descent, taken in the space of functions rather than the space of parameters, with the tree acting as your best available approximation to the ideal step direction.

The correspondences are then exact: the negative gradient is the step direction, the tree is a constrained approximation of it, and the learning rate is the shrinkage parameter — usually called learning_rate or eta, and it does exactly what η does above, scaling each step down so no single tree can dominate.

Two payoffs from this framing, and they are why the question is asked. First, it explains why “each tree fits the residuals” is only true for squared error: with squared loss the negative gradient happens to equal y − F(x), the residual, which is where the folklore comes from. With log-loss it is y − p, and with absolute error it is just the sign of the residual — so a candidate who says “residuals” is repeating a special case. Second, it explains why boosting can optimise any differentiable loss: swap the loss and the only thing that changes is the formula for g. That is precisely how Poisson, Tweedie, quantile and ranking objectives are supported without touching the algorithm.

30-second version

Differentiate the loss with respect to the predictions instead of the parameters. The negative gradient at each training point is the ideal step, but it only exists at those points, so you fit a tree to approximate it and add that. The learning rate is the shrinkage parameter. This is why “trees fit the residuals” is only true for squared error — for log-loss it is y − p — and why any differentiable loss can be plugged in.

What’s being tested

Whether you understand boosting mechanically or by slogan. The residual-versus-negative-gradient distinction is one of the highest-signal details in the whole curriculum, and it is exactly what connects Modules 09, 17 and 18.

Likely follow-up: “Where does XGBoost differ from that description?” — it uses a second-order expansion rather than a first-order one, so each round uses both the gradient and the Hessian per row. That yields a closed-form optimal leaf weight, −G/(H+λ), and a split-gain formula derived from the objective itself rather than from a generic impurity measure — which is both faster to converge and where the L1, L2 and per-leaf penalties enter naturally.
