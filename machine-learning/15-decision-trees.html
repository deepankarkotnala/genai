Module 15 · Part D Trees & Ensembles

Decision Trees

The foundation of everything in Modules 16 to 18. A tree is a piecewise-constant function on an axis-aligned partition — that one sentence explains most of its properties.

Prerequisites · Modules 02, 05Study time · 8 h6 interview questionsFoundation
🎯
Your interview goal

Compute a Gini split by hand under pressure, explain why trees need no scaling in terms of the general principle, and know which tree weaknesses ensembling fixes and which persist into XGBoost.

1 · From the ground up

🧩
In plain words

A decision tree is a game of twenty questions. Ask one yes/no question about one feature — "is age above 30?" — and your rows fall into two piles. Ask another question of each pile. Keep going until each pile is mostly one class, then predict that pile's majority. Everything else is about how the tree picks which question to ask, and when to make it stop.

🎯
How far you need to go for a GenAI interview

This section plus the Gini worked example is the right depth. Trees matter to you mainly as the building block of the ensembles that still beat neural networks on tabular data — that is the comparison question you will actually get. You do not need to derive pruning formulas.

Start with the problem, not the formula

Linear and logistic regression both do the same thing: multiply every feature by a weight and add them all up into one number. That assumes a lot — that age matters the same amount whether income is high or low, and that going from 20 to 21 matters as much as going from 60 to 61.

Now try something completely different. Do not combine features at all. Pick one feature, pick a cutoff, split the rows in two. Then treat each half as a fresh, smaller problem and do it again.

So what: because the model never adds or multiplies features together, scaling does not matter — comparing age to 30 gives the same split in years or in months. That is the real reason trees need no standardisation, and it is a common follow-up.

What makes one question better than another

A good question leaves purer piles — piles that lean heavily one way. So you need a number for "how mixed is this pile". The usual one is Gini impurity, and there is an easy way to read it: pick two rows from the pile at random — what is the chance they are different classes?

A pure pile scores 0. A 50/50 pile scores 0.5, the worst possible for two classes.

So what: a split is worth whatever impurity it removed — the parent's number minus the average of its two children's, weighted by how many rows each child holds. Weighting by size matters: carving off three clean rows out of a thousand barely helps, however pure those three are.

Then just try everything

There is no clever formula for the best question. The tree loops over every feature and every sensible cutoff, scores each one, and keeps the winner. That is the whole training step for one node.

So what: it is greedy — best split right now, with no look-ahead. Two splits that would be brilliant together but mediocre alone get missed. That blind spot is exactly why ensembling trees helps so much in the next two modules.

The third candidate is the instructive one. It is a perfectly sensible-looking line, and it divides the data into two equal halves — but each half is still 50/50, so the tree has learned nothing at all. Impurity gain, not visual tidiness, is the only thing the algorithm can see.

Now repeat, and look at the shape it makes

Take the winning split, then run the same procedure on each half separately — new best feature, new best cutoff, chosen independently on each side. Stop when a pile is pure, too small, or you hit a depth limit.

Because every question is "is this one feature above a cutoff?", every cut is a straight line square to an axis. Nesting them carves the space into rectangles, one per leaf, and inside each rectangle the prediction is a single number.

So what: a tree picks up feature interactions for free — "high income and young" is just one split inside another, with nothing to specify. But a genuinely diagonal boundary has to be approximated by a staircase of little rectangles, which is why a tree sometimes needs surprising depth for something a straight line would nail.
Nested questions carve the space into rectangles

Notice that cut ② exists only on the right-hand side. Each branch chooses its own next question independently — that is what "recursive partitioning" means, and it is why a tree can be deep in one region of the data and shallow in another.

Left alone, it just memorises

Nothing in the procedure says stop. Keep splitting and eventually every leaf holds one row, every leaf is pure, and training accuracy is 100%. You have not learned a pattern — you have stored the data in an elaborate lookup table.

So you have to hold it back, either while it grows (max_depth, min_samples_leaf) or afterwards by cutting off branches that were not worth their complexity.

So what: stopping early is cheaper but it is a guess made before you know what was below. Cutting back afterwards is better, because a split that looked useless on its own might have led somewhere good — the greedy blind spot showing up again.

The weaknesses that actually matter

A single tree is unstable. Change a handful of rows and the very first split can flip, redrawing everything below it. It also cannot predict outside the range it was trained on — a leaf holds one fixed number, so a tree will never continue a rising trend past what it has seen. And its built-in feature importances are biased toward features with many distinct values, because those get more cutoffs to try.

So what: the instability is fixable by averaging many trees, which is the next module. The other two are not. A random forest inherits both the extrapolation limit and the importance bias — and knowing that averaging does not cure everything is what separates an understood answer from a memorised one.
Analogy — a hospital triage flowchart. Each question is deliberately crude and about one thing ("is the temperature above 38?"), and nobody multiplies temperature by blood pressure. The power is in the nesting: which question you get asked second depends entirely on your first answer, so two patients can travel through completely different questions. And it shows the danger too — a flowchart with one box per patient ever seen is a filing cabinet, not a diagnosis.

2 · What to learn

  1. The structure: root, internal nodes, branches, leaves; how a prediction is made by traversal.
  2. Greedy recursive binary splitting, and why it is greedy — finding the globally optimal tree is NP-hard.
  3. Impurity measures for classification: Gini and entropy. Definitions, ranges, and their practical near-equivalence.
  4. Information gain and gain ratio; why plain information gain favours high-cardinality features.
  5. Splitting criteria for regression: variance reduction / MSE, and MAE.
  6. The exact split-finding algorithm: for each feature, sort candidate thresholds and evaluate the weighted impurity of the children.
  7. Stopping criteria: max_depth, min_samples_split, min_samples_leaf, max_leaf_nodes, min_impurity_decrease.
  8. Pruning: pre- versus post-pruning; cost-complexity (weakest-link) pruning and ccp_alpha.
  9. Handling categorical features, and the combinatorial explosion of subset splits.
  10. Handling missing values: surrogate splits, and the learned-default-direction approach.
  11. Why trees need no feature scaling.
  12. Why single trees are high-variance — and why that motivates Modules 16 to 18.
  13. Axis-aligned splits and the resulting inability to represent a diagonal boundary compactly.
  14. Failure to extrapolate — trees predict a constant outside the training range.
  15. Feature importance from trees, and its bias toward high-cardinality and continuous features.

3 · Core intuitions

A tree partitions the feature space into boxes

Each split cuts one region into two along a single feature; the leaves are axis-aligned boxes, and the prediction is constant within each box — the majority class or the mean target. So a decision tree is a piecewise-constant function on an axis-aligned partition. That single sentence explains most of its properties: no scaling needed, no extrapolation, jagged boundaries, and easy interaction capture.

Splitting is greedy and locally optimal

At each node the algorithm evaluates every feature and every candidate threshold and takes the single best split right now, never looking ahead. Finding the globally optimal tree is NP-hard, so greediness is a necessity. The consequence: a split that looks mediocre alone but enables an excellent pair of subsequent splits will be passed over. This is a real limitation — trees struggle with XOR-like problems at shallow depth precisely because no single-feature split reduces impurity at the root.

Gini and entropy measure the same thing and rarely disagree

Gini is 1 - Σpᵢ², the probability that two randomly drawn samples from the node have different labels. Entropy is -Σpᵢlog₂pᵢ, the expected information content in bits. Both are zero for a pure node and maximal for a uniform mixture — in the binary case, Gini 0.5 and entropy 1.0. Their curves are nearly the same shape, so they select the same split the overwhelming majority of the time.

The practical difference is that Gini avoids a logarithm and is therefore slightly faster, which is why it is the default in CART and sklearn. The correct interview answer is that the choice almost never matters and is not worth tuning — say that explicitly, because the question is often designed to see whether you will over-claim a difference.

Information gain favours high-cardinality features, and that is a trap

Gain is impurity reduction, and a feature with many distinct values can slice the data into many small, nearly-pure nodes — a customer-ID column can produce perfect purity while carrying zero generalizable signal. C4.5’s gain ratio normalizes by the split’s own entropy to correct for this. The same bias reappears in tree-based feature importance, which is why permutation importance on a held-out set is more trustworthy (Module 21).

Trees do not need scaling, and the general principle is what matters

A split is a threshold comparison within a single feature, and the algorithm only needs the ranking of that feature’s values to enumerate candidate thresholds. Any monotone transformation — scaling, log, rank — leaves that ranking unchanged and produces an identical tree. Trees never combine features into a single geometric quantity, which is exactly what distance- and gradient-based methods do and why they need scaling.

Trees are high-variance, and this is the most important fact about them

Change a few training rows and the root split can change, which changes every subsequent split, which restructures the whole tree. Two trees fitted on bootstrap samples of the same data can look entirely different while achieving similar accuracy. That instability is precisely what bagging exploits — averaging many high-variance, low-bias models reduces variance without adding much bias. So Module 15’s weakness is Module 16’s opportunity, and stating that link is a strong transition in an interview.

Trees cannot extrapolate

A leaf’s prediction is a constant computed from its training samples, so feeding in a value beyond the training range simply lands you in the outermost leaf and returns its constant. Train a regression tree on houses of 50–200 m² and predict for 500 m², and you get the same answer as for 200 m². Linear regression would extrapolate the trend — perhaps wrongly, but at least responsively. This is a genuine reason to prefer a linear model for a trending target, and why boosted trees struggle with time-series trends unless you detrend or add explicit trend features.

Entropy              H = - SUM_k p_k log2 p_k    binary: max 1.0 at p=0.5
Misclassification    E = 1 - max_k p_k           (rarely used for splitting - too flat)

Weighted child impurity for a split:
      I_split = (n_L/n)*I(left) + (n_R/n)*I(right)

Information gain     IG = I(parent) - I_split
Gain ratio (C4.5)    GainRatio = IG / SplitInfo,
                     SplitInfo = - SUM_i (n_i/n) log2(n_i/n)   <- penalizes many-valued splits

Regression split     minimize  SUM_left (y - ybar_L)² + SUM_right (y - ybar_R)²
                     equivalently maximize variance reduction
Leaf prediction      classification: majority class (or class proportions)
                     regression:     mean of y in the leaf

Cost-complexity pruning:
      R_alpha(T) = R(T) + alpha * |leaves(T)|
      alpha = 0 -> full tree ;  alpha up -> smaller tree ;  tune by CV (ccp_alpha)

5 · A worked Gini split — practise until mechanical

Parent node: 100 samples, 50 positive / 50 negative. Candidate split → Left: 60 samples (45 pos / 15 neg); Right: 40 samples (5 pos / 35 neg).

gini_worked_example.txt
G_parent = 1 - (0.5² + 0.5²) = 0.5

G_left   = 1 - (0.75² + 0.25²)  = 1 - (0.5625 + 0.0625)   = 0.375
G_right  = 1 - (0.125² + 0.875²) = 1 - (0.015625 + 0.765625) = 0.21875

Weighted = (60/100)(0.375) + (40/100)(0.21875)
         = 0.225 + 0.0875 = 0.3125

Gini gain = 0.5 - 0.3125 = 0.1875
🧭
Do not forget the weighting

Weighting by child size is essential — an unweighted average would over-value a split isolating a tiny pure node, which is exactly the pathology min_samples_leaf guards against. Being asked to compute a split by hand is common, and fumbling the arithmetic on a question you conceptually understand is an avoidable loss.

6 · Hands-on drill

  1. Implement a decision tree from scratch: Gini computation, exhaustive split search over features and thresholds, recursive building, depth and leaf-size stopping rules, prediction by traversal. This is a common live-coding task, and building it once makes Modules 16 to 18 far easier.
  2. Compute the worked Gini example above by hand, then verify with your implementation.
  3. Fit trees at max_depth 1 through 20 and plot train and test accuracy — produce the overfitting curve yourself.
  4. Fit the same tree specification on 10 bootstrap samples and visualize all ten. The structural variation you see is the high-variance argument for bagging.
  5. Train a regression tree on a linear trend and plot its step-function predictions, including beyond the training range. That flat line past the boundary is your extrapolation answer.
  6. Use cost_complexity_pruning_path to obtain the ccp_alpha sequence and pick one by cross-validation.
⚠️
Gotchas that fail candidates
  • Claiming Gini and entropy give substantially different trees. They rarely do — say so.
  • Saying trees need scaling.
  • Not knowing trees cannot extrapolate.
  • Treating tree feature importance as reliable without mentioning its high-cardinality bias.
  • Not knowing the greedy-versus-optimal distinction, or why greediness is necessary.
  • Confusing pre-pruning with post-pruning, or not knowing ccp_alpha exists.
  • Saying a deep tree is interpretable. A depth-20 tree has up to a million leaves; nobody reads that.

7 · Interview questions

6 questions
01Gini or entropy? Which is better?impurityGinientropytrap＋
Strong answer

Neither, practically. Both measure node impurity, both are zero for a pure node and maximal for a uniform class mixture, and their curves are so similar in shape that they select the same split the great majority of the time — differences in resulting trees are usually negligible and rarely change test performance.

The real differences are minor: Gini (1 - Σpₖ²) avoids computing a logarithm, so it is slightly faster and is CART’s and sklearn’s default; entropy (-Σpₖlog₂pₖ) has the cleaner information-theoretic reading — the expected bits needed to encode the class — and is very marginally more sensitive near pure nodes. Gini also has a nice interpretation of its own: it is the probability that two samples drawn at random from the node have different labels.

My practical position is that this is not a hyperparameter worth tuning; I would spend that compute on max_depth, min_samples_leaf or feature engineering instead.

30-second version

Practically neither — they pick the same split almost always. Gini avoids a logarithm so it is marginally faster and is the default; entropy has the cleaner information-theoretic reading. Not worth tuning.

What’s being tested

Whether you will invent a difference to sound knowledgeable. Saying plainly that it rarely matters — while still knowing both definitions precisely — is the strong answer.

02Compute the Gini gain for this split. (Parent 50/50 of 100; Left 45/15 of 60; Right 5/35 of 40.)arithmeticGiniworked example＋
Strong answer

G_parent = 1 - (0.5² + 0.5²) = 0.5. The left node has p = 0.75/0.25, so G_left = 1 - (0.5625 + 0.0625) = 0.375. The right node has p = 0.125/0.875, so G_right = 1 - (0.015625 + 0.765625) = 0.21875.

Weighted child impurity is 0.6(0.375) + 0.4(0.21875) = 0.225 + 0.0875 = 0.3125. So Gini gain = 0.5 − 0.3125 = 0.1875.

Note the weighting by child size is essential — an unweighted average would over-value a split that isolates a tiny pure node, which is exactly the pathology min_samples_leaf guards against.

30-second version

G_parent 0.5; G_left 0.375; G_right 0.21875; weighted 0.3125; gain 0.1875.

What’s being tested

Arithmetic under pressure, and whether you remember to weight by node size. Practise it — these are free marks.

03Why is a single decision tree high-variance, and what do we do about it?varianceensemblesbridge＋
Strong answer

Because splitting is greedy and hierarchical. The root split is chosen from the whole dataset, and a small change in the training sample can change which feature or threshold wins — and once the root changes, every subsequent split is computed on different data, so the entire tree restructures. Fit the same specification on ten bootstrap samples and you get ten visibly different trees with similar accuracy. Deep trees make it worse, because leaves contain few samples so their predictions rest on very little data.

Remedies fall into two groups: constrain the single tree — max_depth, min_samples_leaf, min_impurity_decrease, or cost-complexity pruning via ccp_alpha — which trades variance for bias; or, far more effectively, average many trees, which is exactly what bagging and Random Forests do.

That is why single trees are almost never a production model while tree ensembles dominate tabular ML: the instability making one tree unreliable is precisely what makes averaging so effective, since averaging works best on models whose errors are uncorrelated.

30-second version

Greedy hierarchical splitting means a small data change flips the root, which restructures everything below it. Fix by constraining depth and leaf size, or far better by averaging many trees — which is what bagging exploits.

What’s being tested

Whether you can explain the mechanism of the instability and connect it to the motivation for ensembles. That connection is the natural bridge into Module 16 and interviewers listen for it.

04Do decision trees need feature scaling? Why or why not?scalinginvariancetrap＋
Strong answer

No. Each split is a threshold test on a single feature — “is xⱼ ≤ t?” — and the algorithm only needs the ordering of that feature’s values to enumerate candidate thresholds. Any monotone transformation (standardization, min-max, log, rank) preserves that ordering, so it produces an identical tree with correspondingly transformed thresholds.

This extends to all tree ensembles: Random Forest, gradient boosting, XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost.

Contrast with methods that do need scaling — KNN, K-Means, SVM, PCA and gradient-descent-trained models — all of which combine multiple features into a single geometric quantity (a distance, an inner product, or a loss-surface direction), where relative magnitudes therefore matter. The general principle: scaling matters exactly when features are combined; trees never combine them.

30-second version

No — a split only needs the ranking of one feature’s values, which any monotone transform preserves. Scaling matters when features are combined into one quantity; trees never do that.

What’s being tested

A very common question with a precise answer. The general principle at the end is what distinguishes understanding from memorization.

Likely follow-up: “Does a monotone transform of the target matter for a regression tree?” — yes, that is different. The leaf prediction is the mean of y and splits minimize squared error in y, so log-transforming the target changes which splits are chosen and what the leaves predict.
05Explain pruning. Pre-pruning vs post-pruning.pruningccp_alpharegularization＋
Strong answer

Pruning reduces a tree’s size to lower variance.

Pre-pruning (early stopping) prevents growth during construction via max_depth, min_samples_split, min_samples_leaf, max_leaf_nodes or min_impurity_decrease. It is cheap since you never build the full tree, but it is myopic — it can stop at a split that looks unhelpful even though excellent splits lay just beneath it, which is the greediness problem in another form.

Post-pruning grows the tree fully, then removes subtrees that do not pay for themselves. The standard method is cost-complexity (weakest-link) pruning: minimize R_α(T) = R(T) + α|leaves(T)|, which for each α identifies a specific optimal subtree, generating a nested sequence of trees as α increases; you then select α by cross-validation. In sklearn that is ccp_alpha, with cost_complexity_pruning_path giving the candidate values.

Post-pruning generally yields better trees because it evaluates a subtree by its full realized contribution rather than by a single split’s immediate gain; it just costs more to build the full tree first.

30-second version

Pre-pruning stops growth via depth and leaf constraints — cheap but myopic. Post-pruning grows fully then removes subtrees by cost-complexity, minimizing R(T) + α|leaves|, with α chosen by CV via ccp_alpha.

What’s being tested

Whether you know both, and specifically whether you know cost-complexity pruning and ccp_alpha. Many candidates only know max_depth.

06What are the main weaknesses of decision trees?critiqueensemblestrap＋
Strong answer

Five, each with a specific consequence. (1) High variance — small data changes restructure the tree, so single trees are unstable and rarely production-grade. (2) Greedy construction — locally optimal splits can miss globally better structures, which is why trees handle XOR-like interactions poorly at shallow depth. (3) Axis-aligned splits only — a diagonal boundary must be approximated by a staircase of many splits, so a relationship a linear model captures in one coefficient may take a large subtree. (4) No extrapolation — leaves predict constants, so anything beyond the training range returns the outermost leaf’s value, making plain trees a poor fit for trending targets. (5) Biased feature importance — impurity-based importance favours high-cardinality and continuous features, so it can mislead badly.

Worth adding that (1) is largely solved by ensembling — which is why the field moved to Random Forests and boosting — while (3) and (4) are structural and persist even in XGBoost. That is a genuinely useful thing to know when deciding whether to use a linear model or add engineered features.

30-second version

High variance, greedy myopia, axis-aligned-only boundaries, no extrapolation, and biased importances. Ensembling largely fixes the variance; the axis-alignment and extrapolation limits persist all the way into XGBoost.

What’s being tested

Depth of critique, and specifically whether you know which weaknesses ensembling fixes and which it does not. That distinction is the senior-level insight.
