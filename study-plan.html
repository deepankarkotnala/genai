Your minimum study commitment

Target 2–4 focused hours each weekday and 8 focused hours across the weekend. That produces roughly 18–28 hours per week. At that pace the full route below takes approximately 20–36 weeks. A beginner should expect some steps—especially Transformers, RAG end-to-end, LangGraph and the capstone—to need a second pass.

How to use this plan

This is a sequential, page-by-page route. Every page in the learning hub that belongs on the AI Engineer path has a step number, and the steps are ordered so that nothing asks you to know something you have not studied yet. Open step 01, finish it, open step 02. There are no judgement calls left for you to make about ordering.

Phase 1 — Neural and LLM foundations

Steps 01–06 · 50–77 hours. Everything downstream assumes you can explain a forward pass, a gradient and next-token prediction without notes. Do not skip step 01 because it lives in the Interview Questions folder—it is a teaching page, and it is the correct starting point of the whole route.

🧠Phase 1 reading order
  1. Foundations of LLMs 8–12 hTokenization, next-token prediction, decoding, context windows and prompting. Needs the gradient intuition from step 01.
  2. Neural Networks for AI Engineers 12–18 hNeuron intuition, forward pass, loss, backpropagation, optimization and PyTorch. No prerequisites beyond basic Python.
  3. Transformers Deep Dive 14–22 hQ/K/V, attention math, masking, positional encoding and residuals. The hardest page in phase 1 — budget a second pass.
  4. Local LLMs & Ollama 8–12 hQuantization, memory estimates and local serving. Install Ollama here — phase 4's lab depends on it.
  5. Hermes & Open Local Models 4–7 hDeep dive that extends step 04: run a local model, test its tool calling and explain the operational trade-offs.
  6. Question bank — Foundations & prompting 4–6 hConvert phase 1 into spoken answers. Do this before moving on, not at the end of the route.
✓
Phase 1 checkpoint

Draw the transformer block from memory and explain why attention is quadratic. Estimate the VRAM a 7B model needs at 4-bit quantization. Answer every question in step 06 aloud without reading.

Phase 2 — Retrieval and RAG

Steps 07–11 · 50–76 hours. RAG is the single most likely subject of a technical round. It lives on exactly one page in this portal, and that page is long on purpose — read it in order rather than skipping to reranking. Step 10 is your first technology drill; the remaining five drills appear in phases 5 and 7, each placed directly after the material it tests.

🔎Phase 2 reading order
  1. Embeddings 8–12 hVector intuition, similarity, model choice and failure cases. Build the intuition before the database that stores them.
  2. Vector Databases 9–14 hANN, HNSW, filtering, indexing and the recall/latency trade-off. Implement one small index yourself.
  3. RAG, End-to-End 24–36 hRead straight through: ingestion, chunking, retrieval, grounding and citations first, then hybrid search, reranking, query rewriting, parent retrieval, evaluation and tuning.
  4. Drill — RAG 4–6 hTimed technical drill on what you just read. Take it before the question bank, while the gaps are still visible.
  5. Question bank — Embeddings & RAG 5–8 hCloses phase 2. You should be able to defend a chunking strategy against a challenge.
✓
Phase 2 checkpoint

Ship one small retrieval application end to end. Explain when hybrid search beats dense-only, what a reranker actually costs you in latency, and how you would measure whether retrieval or generation caused a bad answer.

Phase 3 — Agent fundamentals

Steps 12–13 · 20–30 hours. Two conceptual pages. They give you the vocabulary — loop, tool, plan, memory — that the hands-on course in phase 4 assumes you already have.

⚙Phase 3 reading order
  1. Agentic AI 12–18 hAgent loops, tools, planning, memory, retries and failure modes. Build one bounded agent by hand before touching a framework.
  2. Model Context Protocol 8–12 hHost/client/server roles, tools and resources, transport and security. Write a small server.
✓
Phase 3 checkpoint

Write an agent loop on a whiteboard with no framework: the prompt, the tool schema, the parse step, the guard, the retry. Name three ways it fails in production.

Phase 4 — Understanding AI Agents (the hands-on course)

Steps 14–29 · 46–52 hours. This is the practical centre of the route and the part most candidates skip. It is one continuous build: a single support-ticket triage agent, developed across 15 lessons, with a runnable Python lab (teach-agents/eda-lab) that plans with a local Gemma model and computes with pandas. Roughly one lesson per weekday for six to seven weeks. Do it before the frameworks in phase 5 — otherwise LangGraph teaches you its abstractions before you know what they abstract over.

🛠Phase 4 reading order — all 15 lessons, in sequence
  1. Course index & environment setup 1–2 hRead the curriculum, then install the lab: eda-lab needs Python 3.10+, pandas, matplotlib, pydantic and the Ollama model from step 04.
  2. Lesson 1 — LLM mechanics ~3 hWhat the model actually does inside an agent, at the level you need to debug it.
  3. Lesson 2 — The agent loop ~3 hThe loop from phase 3, now as running code you own.
  4. Lesson 3 — Tools & validation ~3 hTool schemas and Pydantic validation at the boundary. The single highest-yield lesson for interviews.
  5. Lesson 4 — Reasoning patterns ~3 hReAct, plan-then-execute, reflection — and when each is the wrong choice.
  6. Lesson 5 — Retrieval as a tool ~3 hConnects phase 2 to the agent: retrieval as one tool among several, not a separate architecture.
  7. Lesson 6 — Context & memory ~3 hWhat to keep, what to summarise, what to throw away.
  8. Lesson 7 — Reliability ~3 hRetries, timeouts, idempotency and partial failure.
  9. Lesson 8 — Irreversible actions ~3 hConfirmation, dry runs and blast radius. Senior interviewers ask about this specifically.
  10. Lesson 9 — Security & guardrails ~3 hPrompt injection and the tool boundary, in the concrete case you have been building.
  11. Lesson 10 — Agent evaluation ~3 hGolden sets and trajectory scoring. The lab ships an evals set — run it.
  12. Lesson 11 — Tracing, latency & cost ~3 hWhere the tokens and the seconds actually go. Prepares the Langfuse deep dive in phase 6.
  13. Lesson 12 — MCP & the tool boundary ~3 hThe protocol from step 13, applied to the agent you built.
  14. Lesson 13 — Multi-agent & A2A ~3 hWhen a second agent helps and when it just doubles the failure modes.
  15. Lesson 14 — Deployment & operations ~3 hGetting the thing running somewhere other than your laptop.
  16. Lesson 15 — Interview capstone ~3 hAssemble the course into an answer you can give in a round. Not the same as the large capstone in phase 8.
✓
Phase 4 checkpoint

Run python -m eda_lab.cli "Which ticket categories have the longest resolution times?" and explain every step the agent took and why each guard exists. Then deliver lesson 15's capstone answer out loud in under five minutes.

Phase 5 — Frameworks and orchestration

Steps 30–41 · 88–137 hours. The longest phase. You now know what an agent is, so these pages are about what each framework buys you and what it costs. Note that the two LangGraph prerequisites—AsyncIO and Pydantic—come before the LangGraph module, not after.

🕸Phase 5 reading order
  1. LangChain 10–16 hRunnables, LCEL, tools, structured output and how to debug through the abstraction.
  2. Drill — LangChain 4–6 hTimed drill immediately after the module, while the API is fresh.
  3. LlamaIndex 8–12 hDocuments, nodes, indexes, retrievers and query engines. Compare against your phase 2 hand-built pipeline.
  4. AsyncIO for LangGraph 5–8 hPrerequisite. LangGraph's execution model is async; learn the event loop first or the module will not land.
  5. Pydantic for LangGraph 4–6 hPrerequisite. Graph state is typed. This also sharpens the validation work from phase 4 lesson 3.
  6. LangGraph 16–24 hState, nodes, reducers, routing, checkpointing and human review.
  7. LangGraph & Components (deep dive) 10–16 hBuild and debug a stateful graph with routing, checkpoints and human control. Extends step 35 rather than repeating it.
  8. Multi-Agent Systems 10–16 hSupervisor, handoff, shared state and coordination failure modes.
  9. Agent Protocols — MCP · A2A · A2UI 6–10 hThe interoperability layer above the frameworks. Reads best right after multi-agent.
  10. Drill — MCP 4–6 hTimed drill covering steps 13, 40 and the protocol page.
  11. How a Claude Agent Works 5–8 hOne production agent traced end to end: tool loop, context handling and safety boundaries in real code.
  12. Question bank — Agents, LangGraph & MCP 6–9 hCloses phases 3, 4 and 5 together.
✓
Phase 5 checkpoint

Given a requirement, choose between a plain loop, LangChain, LangGraph and a multi-agent design — and justify the choice against latency, debuggability and failure blast radius. Explain what a checkpoint gives you that a retry does not.

Phase 6 — Production, evaluation and safety

Steps 42–50 · 63–99 hours. This is the phase that separates a candidate who has built a demo from one who has run a system. The module comes first and sets the frame; the five deep dives then go one level down on the parts interviewers actually probe.

📈Phase 6 reading order
  1. Production GenAI 16–24 hEvaluation, observability, security, latency, cost, SLOs and incident reasoning. The frame for everything below.
  2. LLM Evals 8–12 hOffline and online evaluation, LLM-as-judge and its failure modes. Extends phase 4 lesson 10.
  3. Langfuse — Observability 6–10 hInstrument traces, define useful scores and reason through a production incident.
  4. LLMOps 8–12 hVersioning, rollout, regression gates and the lifecycle around a prompt.
  5. Guardrails 6–10 hDesign layered controls and implement representative input/output checks.
  6. Memory in LLM Systems 5–8 hCompare memory patterns and select one against a concrete product constraint.
  7. Question bank — Evaluation & LLMOps 5–8 hCovers steps 43–45.
  8. Question bank — Production, latency & cost 5–8 hCovers step 42 and the numeric reasoning interviewers push on.
  9. Question bank — Security & responsible AI 4–7 hCovers step 46 and phase 4 lessons 8–9.
✓
Phase 6 checkpoint

Take a described production incident — quality regression after a model swap — and walk through detection, diagnosis, rollback and the gate you would add. Give real numbers for p95 latency and cost per request.

Phase 7 — Backend, APIs and data

Steps 51–55 · 22–34 hours. AI Engineer roles are backend roles. This short phase covers the engineering that surrounds the model, and it comes before the capstone because you will use all of it while building.

🔌Phase 7 reading order
  1. Drill — Sync vs Async Python 4–6 hBuilds on step 33. Concurrency questions appear in almost every AI backend screen.
  2. Drill — FastAPI 4–6 hThe default serving layer for the capstone in phase 8.
  3. Drill — WebSockets 3–5 hStreaming responses and long-lived agent sessions.
  4. SQL for GenAI roles 5–8 hText-to-SQL, metadata filtering and the queries behind retrieval. Needed for scenario 03 in phase 9.
  5. Question bank — Python, backend & cloud 6–9 hCloses phase 7 and overlaps the Python rail — a good place to check the rail is keeping up.
✓
Phase 7 checkpoint

Stand up a FastAPI endpoint that streams a model response over WebSockets, backed by a real query. Explain when async helps and when it does nothing.

Phase 8 — The capstone

Step 56 · 50–100 hours. One project, built properly, that you can defend for forty minutes. This is the largest single item in the route and the one that most changes interview outcomes. Do not start it earlier than this — you now have retrieval, agents, a framework, evaluation and a serving layer, which is exactly what a defensible project needs.

🏗Phase 8
  1. Capstone Projects 50–100 hArchitecture, implementation, tests, evaluation, deployment, documentation and interview defence. Pick one project and finish it rather than starting three.
✓
Phase 8 checkpoint

The project is deployed, has an eval suite with numbers you can quote, and has a written architecture decision record. You can name the thing you would do differently and why.

Phase 9 — Interview conversion

Steps 57–69 · 39–59 hours. Knowledge you cannot deliver under time pressure does not count. Everything here is spoken, timed and out loud. Read the answer framework first — the eight scenarios are much less useful without it.

🎤Phase 9 reading order
  1. Answer framework 2–3 hThe structure you will use for all eight scenarios and the system-design round. Learn it before drilling.
  2. Scenario 1 — Enterprise knowledge assistant 2–3 hTimed. Exercises phase 2 retrieval at organisational scale.
  3. Scenario 2 — Customer support agent 2–3 hTimed. The closest scenario to the phase 4 build — you should be fast here.
  4. Scenario 3 — Secure text-to-SQL 2–3 hTimed. Needs step 54 and the guardrails from step 46.
  5. Scenario 4 — ATS recruiter copilot 2–3 hTimed. Fairness and responsible-AI constraints on top of retrieval.
  6. Scenario 5 — Multilingual voice assistant 2–3 hTimed. Latency budgets become the dominant constraint.
  7. Scenario 6 — Invoice document workflow 2–3 hTimed. Extraction accuracy and the irreversible-action problem from phase 4 lesson 8.
  8. Scenario 7 — High-scale shopping assistant 2–3 hTimed. Cost per request at volume — the phase 6 numbers matter here.
  9. Scenario 8 — Financial research copilot 2–3 hTimed. Auditability, citations and regulated-domain constraints.
  10. GenAI system design 6–9 hGeneralises the eight scenarios into a repeatable round performance.
  11. Project & behavioural 5–8 hTurn the phase 8 capstone into the project story. Do this after the capstone exists, not before.
  12. Mock interview rounds 8–12 hFull timed rounds. The last technical step — treat a weak round as a pointer back to a phase, not a verdict.
  13. Where to find remote roles 2–3 hStart applying once mock rounds are consistent. Final step.
✓
Phase 9 checkpoint

Deliver any of the eight scenarios cold, in 30 minutes, with a diagram and named trade-offs — and finish two consecutive mock rounds without needing notes.

Optional track — DSA

⌥
Include this only if your target role has a coding screen

DSA Interview Preparation is a separate 90–140 hour track with its own contents page and its own order. It is deliberately outside the nine phases: it shares no material with them and competes for the same hours. If you need it, run it as a second parallel rail at 3–5 hours a week from phase 2 onward rather than trying to insert it into the spine.

Three rules for using the plan

→

Do not reorder the spine

Each step's why line names what it depends on. Skipping ahead is the most common reason a page feels impenetrable — the missing prerequisite is almost always two or three steps back.

⌁

One page per topic

Every topic has a single home in this portal. If you feel you are re-reading something, you have wandered off the sequence — return to your step number.

⌨

Count active work

Hours count when you code, retrieve from memory, draw systems or answer aloud—not when a page is merely open. A checkpoint you cannot pass means the hours have not been spent.
